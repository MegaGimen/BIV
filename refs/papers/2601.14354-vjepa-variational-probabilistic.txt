VJEPA: Variational Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures as Probabilistic World Models

Yongchao Huang Note: Author email: yongchao.huang@abdn.ac.uk
01/01/2026

Abstract

Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPA) offer a scalable paradigm for self-supervised
learning by predicting latent representations rather than reconstructing high-entropy observations.
However, existing formulations rely on deterministic regression objectives, which mask
probabilistic semantics and limit its applicability in stochastic control. In this work, we
introduce Variational JEPA (VJEPA), a probabilistic generalization that learns a predictive
distribution over future latent states via a variational objective. We show that VJEPA unifies
representation learning with Predictive State Representations (PSRs) and Bayesian filtering,
establishing that sequential modeling does not require autoregressive observation likelihoods.
Theoretically, we prove that VJEPA representations can serve as sufficient information states for
optimal control without pixel reconstruction, while providing formal guarantees for collapse
avoidance. We further propose Bayesian JEPA (BJEPA), an extension that factorizes the predictive
belief into a learned dynamics expert and a modular prior expert, enabling zero-shot task transfer
and constraint (e.g. goal, physics) satisfaction via a Product of Experts. Empirically, through a
noisy environment experiment, we demonstrate that VJEPA and BJEPA successfully filter out
high-variance nuisance distractors that cause representation collapse in generative baselines. By
enabling principled uncertainty estimation (e.g. constructing credible intervals via sampling)
while remaining likelihood-free regarding observations, VJEPA provides a foundational framework for
scalable, robust, uncertainty-aware planning in high-dimensional, noisy environments.

1 Introduction

Self-supervised representation learning has traditionally followed two dominant paradigms: (i)
generative modeling, which optimizes likelihoods over high-dimensional observations such as pixels
or tokens [39, 64, 81, 62], and (ii) contrastive learning, which discriminates between positive and
negative pairs [29, 82, 20, 32]. Both approaches suffer from fundamental limitations: generative
models must model nuisance variability and high-entropy details irrelevant for downstream tasks [2,
34, 48], while contrastive methods require carefully engineered negative sampling schemes and can
introduce representational bias [80, 4, 20].

Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPA) offer a third paradigm [5, 9]. Rather than
reconstructing observations or contrasting samples, JEPA learns by predicting representations of
missing or future data [5]. This removes the need for observation-level likelihoods and explicitly
biases learning toward predictable, task-relevant structure [9, 44, 71]. Recent instantiations such
as I-JEPA [5] and V-JEPA [9, 10] have demonstrated strong empirical performance on vision and video
tasks. Early on, LeCun [45] proposed Hierarchical JEPA (H-JEPA), a conceptual framework for
multi-level, multi-timescale predictive models that emphasizes learning latent dynamics without
observation reconstruction as a basis for model-predictive control and long-horizon reasoning.

More recently, several works have begun to instantiate JEPA-style predictors as world models for
planning and control. Bardes et al. [6] show that large-scale JEPA pretraining on internet video,
combined with lightweight action-conditioned prediction, enables zero-shot robotic planning via
latent-space model-predictive control. Terver et al. [78] systematically study action-conditioned
JEPA World Models (JEPA-WMs), demonstrating that planning directly in representation space can
solve navigation and manipulation tasks without observation reconstruction and without training on
reward-labeled data. Destrade et al. [22] further study value-guided JEPA planning, which shapes
the representation space so that distances approximate goal-conditioned value, and improves
optimization-based planning.

Despite these advances, existing JEPA-based world models remain largely deterministic and are
trained using regression-based objectives [5, 9]. As a result, the underlying probabilistic
semantics of JEPA predictions, the representation of uncertainty over future latent states, and the
conditions under which the learned representation constitutes a sufficient information state for
planning and control remain unformalized. In particular, current approaches implicitly assume
predictive sufficiency, but do not characterize when or why this assumption should hold.

In this work, we ask and answer several foundational questions about JEPA:

  • •
   
    Implicit Probabilistic Model: what probabilistic objective does deterministic JEPA implicitly
    optimize, and how can it be generalized to handle uncertainty?

  • •
   
    Dynamics and Control: can JEPA be formalized as a latent dynamical system where the learned
    representation serves as a sufficient information state for optimal control, without
    reconstructing observations?

  • •
   
    Bayesian Connection: how does JEPA relate to Bayesian filtering and Predictive State
    Representations (PSRs), and can this connection enable the injection of structural priors or
    constraints into latent planning?

  • •
   
    Nuisance Invariance: does introducing temporal structure force JEPA to adopt autoregressive
    observation likelihoods, or can it remain likelihood-free to avoid modeling high-variance
    nuisance distractors?

We address these questions by formulating JEPA as a probabilistic predictive state-space model, and
showing that sequential predictive modeling does not require autoregressive likelihood
factorization and that JEPA representations can serve as sufficient information states for control
and planning.

Contributions.

We propose Variational JEPA (VJEPA), a probabilistic formulation that explicates the implicit
generative model of JEPA. We theoretically establish VJEPA as a predictive state-space model,
proving that its representations serve as sufficient information states for optimal control and
providing formal guarantees for collapse avoidance. We further extend this to Bayesian JEPA (BJEPA)
, a modular framework that factorizes predictive belief into learned dynamics and structural
priors, enabling the insertion of constraints and physical laws, as well as zero-shot task transfer
via Product of Experts. Finally, we empirically demonstrate in a noisy environment that VJEPA and
BJEPA successfully filter out high-variance distractors, validating that principled
uncertainty-aware world modeling is achievable without autoregressive observation reconstruction.

2 Related Work

2.1 Predictive State Representations

Predictive State Representations (PSRs) were introduced by Sutton et al. [47] and independently
formalized by Singh et al. [71]. They replace latent states with sufficient statistics of future
observations, representing the state as

 st=p(ot+1:t+H∣o≤t),s_{t}=p(o_{t+1:t+H}\mid o_{\leq t}),

where HH denotes the prediction horizon. Sutton et al. [47] emphasized the theoretical connection
between PSRs and history-based models, showing how state can be represented purely in terms of
observable predictions. Singh et al. [71] developed the system-dynamics matrix formalism and
demonstrated that PSRs generalize nn-th order Markov models and hidden-state models such as HMMs or
POMDPs. Later, we will see that VJEPA can be viewed as a neural, amortized PSR that compresses
predictive distributions into a latent embedding ZtZ_{t}.

2.2 World Models and Latent Dynamics

Model-based latent dynamics have been widely explored in reinforcement learning. PlaNet [31]
introduced a probabilistic latent dynamics model that predicts future latent states zt+1z_{t+1}
conditioned on actions ata_{t} and reconstructs observations xtx_{t}, optimizing an evidence lower
bound (ELBO) over pixels. Dreamer [30] extended PlaNet by integrating actor-critic reinforcement
learning in the learned latent space, enabling policy optimization without decoding full
trajectories to pixels. Formally, these methods learn

 p⁡(zt+1∣zt,at),p⁡(xt∣zt),p(z_{t+1}\mid z_{t},a_{t}),\quad p(x_{t}\mid z_{t}),

where the observation likelihood p⁡(xt∣zt)p(x_{t}\mid z_{t}) is explicitly modeled.

In contrast, JEPA [5], V-JEPA (video-JEPA) [9] and VJEPA (this work) discard observation
reconstruction, learning predictive latent representations that compress future information into
ZtZ_{t}, emphasizing predictive sufficiency rather than pixel fidelity.

MuZero [69] also learns latent dynamics without reconstructing observations, but it still relies on
value and policy heads for planning. VJEPA differs by learning latent dynamics purely through
representation prediction, without access to rewards, policies, or observation likelihoods, making
it a self-supervised model suitable for both control and general representation learning.

2.3 Active Inference

Active Inference is a theoretical framework proposed by Friston and colleagues in which perception,
action, and learning are cast as variational inference in a latent variable model of the world [26,
24, 25]. The central idea is that biological agents act to minimize variational free energy, which
provides an upper bound on sensory surprise (negative log-evidence). Formally, variational free
energy is defined as

 ℱ⁡(q)=𝔼q⁡(z)​[log⁡q⁡(z)−log⁡p⁡(x,z)],\mathcal{F}(q)=\mathbb{E}_{q(z)}\left[\log q(z)-\log p(x,z)\
 right],

where zz denotes latent (hidden) states of the environment, xx denotes sensory observations, and q⁡
(z)q(z) is an approximate posterior belief. Minimizing ℱ⁡(q)\mathcal{F}(q) drives q⁡(z)q(z) towards
the true Bayesian posterior p⁡(z∣x)p(z\mid x), thereby implementing perception as approximate
Bayesian inference. This formulation was introduced and developed in early work by Friston [26] and
extended to perception-action loops in subsequent studies [24].

Within Active Inference, belief updating corresponds to approximate Bayesian filtering over latent
states, often implemented as gradient descent on free energy. More recent work has emphasized a
distinction between perception and action: while perception minimizes current free energy, action
selection is driven by the minimization of expected free energy over future trajectories, enabling
agents to plan by imagining future outcomes and selecting policies that reduce expected surprise
and uncertainty [25].

VJEPA aligns with Active Inference at the level of latent belief propagation: both frameworks aim
to learn compact latent representations that summarize past observations and support prediction of
future states. However, VJEPA deliberately omits modeling sensory likelihoods p⁡(x∣z)p(x\mid z),
preferences, or policy evaluation. Instead, it focuses exclusively on learning predictive latent
dynamics through representation prediction, yielding a self-supervised model of belief dynamics
rather than a full Active Inference agent.

2.4 Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs)

JEPA was introduced to self-supervised learning by Assran et al. [5] in the Image-based JEPA
(I-JEPA) model, where representations of held-out image regions are predicted from encoded context
patches. I-JEPA demonstrated that prediction in a latent representation space can produce highly
semantic features without pixel reconstruction or handcrafted augmentations, and scales to large
vision models with competitive performance [5].

Subsequent work extended the JEPA paradigm to video. Bardes et al. proposed Video JEPA (V-JEPA) for
self-supervised learning from video by predicting masked spatio-temporal regions in a learned
latent space [9]. While V-JEPA improves temporal representation learning relative to image-only
JEPA, it retains the deterministic regression objective.

Beyond these instantiations, the JEPA framework has inspired a variety of architectural and domain
adaptations. For example, MC-JEPA [12] incorporates motion and content prediction objectives for
richer video representation [12], S-JEPA applies the JEPA principle to skeletal action recognition
[1], and Point-JEPA adapts JEPA to point cloud self-supervised learning [68]. Other variants
explore JEPA in graph domains [72] and in joint vision-language models [19]. Additional work (e.g.
C-JEPA [57], DSeq-JEPA [33]) proposes contrastive or sequential extensions that address specific
limitations such as representation collapse or prediction ordering.

Despite this growing body of JEPA-based methods and applications, to our knowledge there has been
no formal variational or Bayesian JEPA that explicitly models predictive uncertainty and the
underlying probabilistic generative process. Some work incorporates auxiliary losses to stabilize
training (e.g. VICReg-style regularization [11] in C-JEPA [57]) or implicitly addresses uncertainty
through architectural choices, but these do not derive from a principled likelihood-based
objective. In contrast, our VJEPA formulates JEPA as a variational predictive model, learning an
explicit predictive distribution over future latent states with likelihood and KL regularization,
enabling a formal probabilistic interpretation and control-theoretic extensions not addressed by
prior JEPA work.

2.5 JEPA World Models and Latent-Space Planning

The use of JEPA as a foundation for world modeling and planning was first articulated at a
conceptual level by LeCun [45]. In his proposal of Hierarchical JEPA (H-JEPA), LeCun argues that
autonomous agents should learn multi-level, multi-timescale predictive representations of the world
that support planning and model-predictive control without relying on observation reconstruction or
explicit reward modeling. H-JEPA provides a normative blueprint for predictive world modeling and
hierarchical control, but does not specify a concrete training objective, probabilistic semantics,
or learning algorithm.

Building on this vision, more recent work has developed concrete, action-conditioned JEPA-based
world models that enable planning directly in representation space. Terver et al. [78] introduce
JEPA World Models (JEPA-WMs), which combine pretrained visual encoders with action-conditioned JEPA
predictors trained via a deterministic representation prediction loss. Planning is performed by
sampling or optimizing action sequences whose predicted latent trajectories minimize a
goal-conditioned cost in embedding space. Their results demonstrate that latent-space planning
without observation reconstruction can match or outperform reconstruction-based world models,
particularly when reward signals are unavailable or sparse.

Several subsequent works explore extensions of JEPA-based planning. Destrade et al. [22] propose
value-guided JEPA planning, in which the latent representation space is shaped so that distances
approximate goal-conditioned value functions, improving the optimization landscape for planning.
More recently, Bardes et al. [6] demonstrate that large-scale JEPA pretraining on internet video,
followed by lightweight action-conditioned prediction, enables zero-shot robotic manipulation via
latent-space model-predictive control.

While these works provide strong empirical evidence that JEPA representations can support planning
and control, they are uniformly based on deterministic predictors trained via regression
objectives. As a result, uncertainty over future latent states, belief propagation, and the
conditions under which the learned representation constitutes a sufficient information state for
optimal control remain implicit and unformalized. In particular, these approaches assume predictive
sufficiency of the latent state without providing a probabilistic model or formal guarantees.

Our work complements JEPA-based world models by providing a variational formulation of JEPA that
makes the underlying predictive distribution explicit. By modeling uncertainty over future latent
states, VJEPA enables belief propagation, distributional planning, and formal connections to
predictive state representations and Bayesian filtering, which are capabilities that not addressed
by existing deterministic JEPA world models.

Taken together, the above lines of work leave open a gap that is not fully addressed by existing
approaches. Predictive State Representations provide a principled account of state as predictive
statistics, but are not designed as scalable, amortized representation learners for
high-dimensional sensory data. Latent world models such as PlaNet, Dreamer, and MuZero learn
predictive dynamics suitable for control, but rely on observation reconstruction, reward
supervision, or policy optimization objectives that entangle representation learning with
task-specific signals. Active Inference offers a unifying theoretical framework for perception and
action, but requires explicit modeling of sensory likelihoods, preferences, and policies, and is
not typically instantiated as a self-supervised representation learning algorithm. Finally,
existing JEPA formulations learn predictive representations using deterministic regression
objectives, which obscure the underlying probabilistic structure and preclude explicit modeling of
uncertainty over future latent states.

VJEPA occupies a complementary point in this design space by extending JEPA with an explicit
probabilistic formulation. It learns predictive latent states as amortized representations while
modeling a full predictive distribution over future embeddings, rather than a single point
estimate. This enables principled uncertainty estimation, belief propagation, and compatibility
with Bayesian filtering and control, while retaining JEPA’s core advantages: avoiding observation
reconstruction, reward supervision, and autoregressive likelihood factorization. By focusing
exclusively on predictive sufficiency through variational representation prediction, VJEPA bridges
predictive state representations, latent world models, and belief propagation frameworks, while
remaining a purely self-supervised model that is directly applicable to downstream control and
planning.

3 JEPA: Background and Deterministic Formulation

Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPA) were introduced as a self-supervised learning
framework that learns representations by predicting embeddings of missing or future data, rather
than reconstructing observations or discriminating between positive and negative samples [5, 9].
The central design principle of JEPA is to encourage representations that capture predictable,
task-relevant structure while discarding nuisance variability that is difficult or unnecessary to
predict.

Given an input xx (e.g. an image or a video clip), JEPA first partitions the input into two
disjoint subsets: a context xCx_{C} and a target xTx_{T}. The context contains the information
available to the model, while the target represents missing or held-out content that must be
predicted at the representation level. This partitioning can correspond to spatial masking (as in
I-JEPA [5]) or temporal masking (as in video JEPA [9]), but the formulation itself is agnostic to
the specific modality. The context is mapped to a latent representation using a trainable encoder

 ZC=fθ​(xC),Z_{C}=f_{\theta}(x_{C}),   (1)

which produces a compact embedding summarizing the available information. The target is encoded
using a separate encoder

 ZT=fθ′​(xT),Z_{T}=f_{\theta^{\prime}}(x_{T}),   (2)

whose parameters θ′\theta^{\prime} are updated as an exponential moving average (EMA) of the
context encoder parameters[56, 55, 46, 27]:

 θ′←τ​θ′+(1−τ)​θ.\theta^{\prime}\leftarrow\tau\theta^{\prime}+(1-\tau)\theta.  (3)

This asymmetric design, inspired by prior work [27], stabilizes training and prevents
representational collapse by ensuring that the target embeddings evolve more slowly than the
predictor.

To predict the target representation, JEPA employs a predictor network

 Z^T=gϕ​(ZC,ξT),\hat{Z}_{T}=g_{\phi}(Z_{C},\xi_{T}),  (4)

where ξT\xi_{T} encodes structural (side) information about the target, such as spatial location or
temporal index. The predictor is tasked with mapping the context representation to the target
embedding without direct access to the target observations themselves.

With these settings, training is then performed by minimizing a regression loss between the
predicted and target embeddings:

4 VJEPA: Variational Formulation of JEPA

Eq. (5) shows that the deterministic JEPA objective is equivalent to maximum likelihood under an
isotropic Gaussian predictive model with fixed variance (Eq. (6)). This suggests a natural
generalization: instead of predicting a single target embedding Z^T\hat{Z}_{T} (i.e. a point
estimate), we can model an explicit predictive distribution over possible target embeddings,
enabling uncertainty-aware prediction and multi-modal futures. Importantly, as in deterministic
JEPA, prediction is conditioned not only on the context representation but also on structural
(side) information describing the target.

From deterministic JEPA to VJEPA.

VJEPA can be understood as a minimal probabilistic extension of deterministic JEPA. Rather than
altering the architectural principles of JEPA, VJEPA replaces point prediction in representation
space with distributional prediction while preserving the same context-target structure,
conditioning mechanism, and training asymmetry. As a preview, we summarise the key differences in

4.1 Probabilistic Predictive Model

Let xx be an input sample and (xC,xT)(x_{C},x_{T}) be a context/target partition. As in JEPA, the

probabilistic predictive model over the target representation:

VJEPA reduces to the deterministic JEPA. The probabilistic, predictive model Eq. (7) can be further
decomposed, e.g. formulated as a Bayesian posterior (see later Section.8).

Optionally, one may also posit an observation model

 pψ​(xT∣ZT),p_{\psi}(x_{T}\mid Z_{T}),   (8)

 (Z_{T}\mid Z_{C},\xi_{T})\,p_{\psi}(x_{T}\mid Z_{T}).

In this text, in the spirit of JEPA, we do not optimize an observation-level reconstruction
objective over pψ​(xT∣ZT)p_{\psi}(x_{T}\mid Z_{T}); the learning signal is provided solely through
the predictive distribution in representation space, pϕ​(ZT∣ZC,ξT)p_{\phi}(Z_{T}\mid Z_{C},\xi_
{T}).

4.2 Inference Model from the Target Encoder

To train the predictive model without reconstructing xTx_{T}, we require a representation-space
“posterior” for ZTZ_{T} given the target observation. We define an amortized inference distribution
using the (EMA) target encoder:

 qθ′​(ZT∣xT),q_{\theta^{\prime}}(Z_{T}\mid x_{T}),  (10)

which may be instantiated as a diagonal Gaussian whose mean and variance are parameterized by fθ′​
(xT)f_{\theta^{\prime}}(x_{T}). The EMA update in Eq. (3) ensures that the inference target evolves
slowly, which stabilizes training and prevents degenerate fixed points.

explicit predictive uncertainty, (ii) permits multi-modal futures through expressive pϕ(⋅∣ZC,ξT)p_
{\phi}(\cdot\mid Z_{C},\xi_{T}), and (iii) yields collapse-avoidance guarantees under mild

4.6 Prediction and Uncertainty Propagation

After training, VJEPA can be used as a predictive model in representation space. Given a new input
xCx_{C} and target specification ξT\xi_{T}, we first compute the context representation (i.e. the
context encoder Eq. (1))

 ZC=fθ​(xC),Z_{C}=f_{\theta}(x_{C}),

and then form the predictive distribution (i.e. the predictor Eq. (7))

 pϕ​(ZT∣ZC,ξT).p_{\phi}(Z_{T}\mid Z_{C},\xi_{T}).

Point prediction.

A point estimate of the target representation may be obtained from the predictive distribution pϕ​
(ZT∣ZC,ξT)p_{\phi}(Z_{T}\mid Z_{C},\xi_{T}) in several standard ways. A common choice is the
predictive mean,

 Z^Tmean=𝔼pϕ​(ZT∣ZC,ξT)​[ZT],\hat{Z}_{T}^{\text{mean}}=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\phi}(Z_{T}\mid Z_{C},\xi_
 {T})}[Z_{T}],

which reduces to the deterministic JEPA predictor when pϕp_{\phi} is Gaussian with fixed variance.

Alternatively, a maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate may be used:

 Z^TMAP=arg⁡maxZT​pϕ​(ZT∣ZC,ξT),\hat{Z}_{T}^{\text{MAP}}=\arg\max_{Z_{T}}p_{\phi}(Z_{T}\mid Z_{C},
 \xi_{T}),

which coincides with the predictive mean for unimodal Gaussian models but differs for multi-modal
or heavy-tailed distributions.

More generally, one may consider a mode prediction,

 Z^Tmode=mode[pϕ​(ZT∣ZC,ξT)],\hat{Z}_{T}^{\text{mode}}=\operatorname*{mode}\left[p_{\phi}(Z_{T}\
 mid Z_{C},\xi_{T})\right],

which selects a single most probable latent realization. For expressive predictive models (e.g.
mixture models or normalizing flows), MAP or mode predictions allow VJEPA to select a
representative future consistent with the learned multi-modal predictive structure.

Distributional prediction and uncertainty representation.

Rather than using a single predicted embedding, VJEPA represents future uncertainty explicitly
through the predictive distribution pϕ​(ZT∣ZC,ξT)p_{\phi}(Z_{T}\mid Z_{C},\xi_{T}). Downstream
tasks may operate directly on this distribution, either by propagating summary statistics (e.g.
moments) when pϕp_{\phi} admits a closed form, or by approximating expectations using samples. In
this sense, VJEPA produces a belief over future representations rather than a point forecast. A
general and flexible way to work with the predictive belief is via Monte Carlo sampling:

 ZT(m)∼pϕ(ZT∣ZC,ξT),m=1,…,M.Z_{T}^{(m)}\sim p_{\phi}(Z_{T}\mid Z_{C},\xi_{T}),\qquad m=1,\dots,M.

where MM is the total number of samples. These samples represent alternatively plausible future
representations consistent with the observed context and target specification. Expectations of
downstream quantities can then be approximated as

 𝔼⁡[f⁡(ZT)]≈1M​∑m=1Mf⁡(ZT(k)),\mathbb{E}[f(Z_{T})]\approx\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}f\left(Z_{T}^
 {(k)}\right),

allowing uncertainty-aware prediction even when pϕp_{\phi} is complex (e.g. mixtures or normalizing
flows).

Multi-step prediction and uncertainty propagation.

For sequential prediction, VJEPA propagates uncertainty by integrating the predictive model in
latent space. Given a belief p⁡(Zt)p(Z_{t}), the predictive belief at a future step t+Δt+\Delta is

 p⁡(Zt+Δ)=∫pϕ​(Zt+Δ∣Zt,ξt+Δ)​p​(Zt)​d​Zt,p(Z_{t+\Delta})=\int p_{\phi}(Z_{t+\Delta}\mid Z_     (12)
 {t},\xi_{t+\Delta})\,p(Z_{t})\,dZ_{t},

which defines a belief-state evolution analogous to Bayesian filtering. In practice, this integral
may be approximated by moment propagation (when available) or by Monte Carlo rollouts:

 Zt+1(m)∼pϕ(Zt+1∣Zt(m),ξt+1),m=1,…,M,Z_{t+1}^{(m)}\sim p_{\phi}(Z_{t+1}\mid Z_{t}^{(m)},\xi_
 {t+1}),\qquad m=1,\dots,M,

which yields a distribution over latent trajectories rather than a single deterministic path. This
form of distributional rollout underlies the planning and control algorithms introduced in later
sections, e.g. model predictive control (MPC).

Connection to ensemble world models.

Sampling-based prediction in VJEPA is closely related to ensemble world models commonly used in
model-based reinforcement learning. In ensemble methods, multiple deterministic dynamics models {fϕ
(m)}m=1M\{f_{\phi^{(m)}}\}_{m=1}^{M} are trained independently, and uncertainty is approximated by
disagreement across model predictions. In contrast, VJEPA represents uncertainty within a single
probabilistic model pϕ​(ZT∣ZC,ξT)p_{\phi}(Z_{T}\mid Z_{C},\xi_{T}), where stochasticity arises from
an explicit predictive distribution rather than parameter diversity. Monte Carlo sampling from
VJEPA therefore plays a role analogous to sampling from an ensemble, while avoiding the
computational overhead and calibration issues associated with maintaining multiple models. This
perspective highlights VJEPA as a distributional generalization of ensemble world models operating
directly in representation space.

4.7 Collapse Avoidance

Here we formalize the claim that VJEPA discourages representational collapse.

Definition 1 (Collapse).

We say the context representation collapses if there exists a constant c∈ℝdc\in\mathbb{R}^{d} such
that

 fθ​(xC)=c∀xC.f_{\theta}(x_{C})=c\qquad\forall x_{C}.

Remark 1.

Theorem 1 shows that collapse is not merely discouraged but is fundamentally incompatible with
global optimality of the VJEPA objective (Eq. (11)) under target diversity. A collapsed context
representation forces the predictor to ignore informative conditioning, yielding an irreducible
excess loss relative to non-collapsed solutions. Therefore, collapse avoidance in VJEPA arises from
the structure of the predictive objective itself, rather than relying solely on architectural
asymmetry, stop-gradient operations, or EMA updates.

5 Time-indexed VJEPA as a Latent Space Dynamical System

VJEPA always learns a conditional predictive relation in representation space of the form (Eq. (7
)):

 ZT∼pϕ​(ZT∣ZC,ξT),Z_{T}\sim p_{\phi}(Z_{T}\mid Z_{C},\xi_{T}),

where ZCZ_{C} is a context representation, ZTZ_{T} is a target representation, and ξT\xi_{T}
specifies structural information about the target (e.g. spatial location, temporal index, or
masking pattern). However, whether this predictive relation constitutes a latent dynamical system
depends on how the context-target pair is constructed and how ξT\xi_{T} is interpreted. JEPA is
therefore not inherently a sequential or observation-likelihood model; in particular, JEPA does not
inherently impose sequential or Markovian structure. This distinction is important for
understanding both the capabilities and the limitations of existing JEPA variants, including Video
JEPA [9, 10].

Here we formalize a conditional dynamical-system perspective on VJEPA, introducing a latent
dynamical system in representation space when the context and target are indexed by time. Under
this time-indexed formulation, VJEPA defines a latent state, a predictive transition model, and a
belief-propagation mechanism, enabling filtering-style updates and control directly in latent
space. Table.2 summarizes the different regimes in which JEPA-style objectives ^1^1 1 The temporal

5.5 Clip-Level Prediction (V-JEPA) vs. Sequential Dynamics

It is important to distinguish between latent prediction and latent dynamics. In its current
instantiation, V-JEPA [9, 10] is trained to predict masked spatio-temporal regions within a fixed
video clip, using a large masked context that may already contain information from both past and
future frames [9]. As a result, the learned predictor is not explicitly constrained to represent a
compositional, step-by-step temporal transition model.

This design choice has been noted in recent discussions [10] of V-JEPA: while V-JEPA captures rich
temporal cues and yields strong representations for downstream video understanding tasks, it does
not enforce sequential consistency across multiple prediction steps, nor does it learn a Markovian
latent transition operator that can be iterated forward in time. In particular, predictions at
different horizons are made independently, and the training objective does not require that

 Zt+2≈pϕ(⋅∣Zt+1,ξt+2)withZt+1∼pϕ(⋅∣Zt,ξt+1).Z_{t+2}\approx p_{\phi}(\,\cdot\mid Z_{t+1},\xi_{t+2})
 \quad\text{with}\quad Z_{t+1}\sim p_{\phi}(\,\cdot\mid Z_{t},\xi_{t+1}).

The dynamical-system perspective adopted here makes this distinction explicit. By parameterizing
JEPA/VJEPA with a time-indexed context Zt=fθ​(x≤t)Z_{t}=f_{\theta}(x_{\leq t}) and a
horizon-dependent target specification ξt+Δ\xi_{t+\Delta}, the predictor (Eq. (16)) pϕ​
(Zt+Δ∣Zt,ξt+Δ)p_{\phi}(Z_{t+\Delta}\mid Z_{t},\xi_{t+\Delta}) can be interpreted as a latent
transition model. When this transition is applied recursively, it induces a belief-state evolution
analogous to classical state-space models, enabling filtering, multi-step prediction, and planning
directly in representation space.

From this perspective, clip-level V-JEPA can be seen as learning a powerful one-shot latent
predictor, whereas the sequential formulation studied here extends JEPA/VJEPA into a latent
dynamical system. This extension does not contradict the original V-JEPA design, but rather
clarifies the additional structural assumptions required to support temporal composition,
uncertainty propagation, and control.

Summary.

Time-indexed JEPA/VJEPA can be viewed as learning a latent state-space model in which: (i) the
encoder defines the latent state ZtZ_{t}, (ii) the predictor defines a latent transition model, and
(iii) optional observation modeling is decoupled from representation learning. This is the
mathematical sense in which JEPA is sequential but not autoregressive.

6 JEPA/VJEPA for Control

Having interpreted JEPA/VJEPA as defining a latent dynamical system in representation space, we now
show how such latent predictive states can be used for planning and control. The key message is
classical in partially observable control: optimal control does not require reconstructing
observations, but only a sufficient belief (information state) for predicting future consequences
of actions.

6.1 Background: POMDPs and Belief-State Control

A partially^4^4 4 POMDP [49, 37] generalizes an MDP to settings where the agent cannot directly
observe the true system state. The environment evolves according to Markovian dynamics in a latent
state space, but the agent only receives observations generated probabilistically from the
underlying state. As a result, the agent must maintain a belief state, which is a probability
distribution over latent states, based on the history of observations and actions. Policies in a
POMDP therefore map from belief states (or equivalently observation histories) to actions, rather
than from fully observed states as in an MDP. observable Markov decision process (POMDP) is given
by

 ℳ=(𝒮,𝒰,𝒳,P,R,γ),\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{U},\mathcal{X},P,R,\gamma),

with latent state st∈𝒮s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}, action ut∈𝒰u_{t}\in\mathcal{U}, observation xt∈𝒳x_{t}\in
\mathcal{X}, transition kernel P⁡(st+1∣st,ut)P(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t},u_{t}), reward (utility, or
negative cost) R⁡(st,ut)R(s_{t},u_{t}), and discount γ∈(0,1)\gamma\in(0,1). The agent does not
observe sts_{t} directly and must act based on history

 ht:=(x1:t,u1:t−1).h_{t}:=(x_{1:t},u_{1:t-1}).

A standard result is that an information state (a Bayesian belief state) is sufficient for optimal
control [37, 73, 74, 7]:

 bt​(s):=p⁡(st=s∣ht),b_{t}(s):=p(s_{t}=s\mid h_{t}),

then there exists an optimal policy that depends on history only through btb_{t}:

 π⋆​(ut∣ht)=π⋆​(ut∣bt).\pi^{\star}(u_{t}\mid h_{t})=\pi^{\star}(u_{t}\mid b_{t}).  (22)

Thus, control reduces to propagating and planning in belief space rather than observation space.

The sufficiency of the belief state (or information state) for optimal control is a foundational
result established across the control theory and operations research literature. Åström (1965) [7]
originally demonstrated that the conditional probability distribution of the latent state serves as
a sufficient statistic for the complete history of actions and observations, thereby transforming
the partially observable problem into a completely observable Markovian problem over the space of
distributions. This theoretical basis was operationally expanded by Smallwood and Sondik (1973) [73
] and Sondik (1978) [74], who proved the existence of optimal policies that depend exclusively on
this ”information vector” for both finite and infinite horizon settings, deriving the associated
dynamic programming equations. Synthesizing these classical results for artificial intelligence
planning, Kaelbling et al. (1998) [37] re-affirm that because the belief state encapsulates all
necessary historical information, the control problem formally reduces to solving a ”belief MDP”
which is a fully observable Markov decision process defined over the continuous space of
probability distributions. These collective work confirm that the optimal policy need only map the
current belief to an action π⋆​(ut∣bt)\pi^{\star}(u_{t}\mid b_{t}), and justify the reduction of
general history-based planning to belief-space planning.

6.2 JEPA/VJEPA Latent State as a Predictive Information State

JEPA/VJEPA does not explicitly model p⁡(xt∣st)p(x_{t}\mid s_{t}) and therefore does not construct
the Bayesian belief btb_{t} in 𝒮\mathcal{S}. Instead, it learns a latent representation ZtZ_{t}

Why reconstruction is unnecessary.

In model-based control, planning means using a predictive model to compare candidate actions by
their anticipated consequences ^5^5 5 Concretely, a planner simulates candidate futures under
different action sequences and selects the action (or sequence) that optimizes an objective such as

applies. In this sense, VJEPA can be viewed as learning an amortized predictive information state
whose sufficiency for control follows from correct specification and optimization, rather than from
observation reconstruction or explicit belief-state estimation. As in PSR and belief-state
analyses, these assumptions characterize an idealized regime; in practice VJEPA is expected to
approximate predictive sufficiency to the extent allowed by data, capacity, and optimization.

6.4 Latent-Space Planning Objective

Planning in POMDPs.

In a POMDP, optimal control cannot be expressed directly as a function of the unobserved latent
state sts_{t}. Instead, planning must be carried out in terms of the belief state

 bt(s):=p(st=s∣x1:t,u1:t−1),b_{t}(s):=p(s_{t}=s\mid x_{1:t},u_{1:t-1}),

which summarizes all information available to the agent. Given a transition model P⁡(st+1∣st,ut)P
(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t},u_{t}), an observation model p⁡(xt∣st)p(x_{t}\mid s_{t}), and a reward (or cost)
function, the POMDP induces a belief-space MDP, in which actions are selected to maximize expected
cumulative reward under the evolution of beliefs. Planning in a POMDP therefore amounts to
simulating how candidate action sequences propagate the belief distribution forward in time and
selecting the action that optimizes expected return [73, 37, 67]:

 π⋆​(ut∣ht)=π⋆​(ut∣bt).\pi^{\star}(u_{t}\mid h_{t})=\pi^{\star}(u_{t}\mid b_{t}).

This belief-space formulation is exact but typically intractable in high-dimensional or continuous
settings [58, 51, 42, 75].

Latent belief approximation via VJEPA.

VJEPA replaces the intractable Bayesian belief btb_{t} with a learned latent representation Zt=fθ​
(x≤t)Z_{t}=f_{\theta}(x_{\leq t}) that serves as an amortized, predictive belief state ^6^6 6 Here
‘belief’ refers to a predictive distribution over latent representations rather than a posterior
over true environment states.. The predictive model pϕ​(Zt+1∣Zt,ξt+1,ut)p_{\phi}(Z_{t+1}\mid Z_{t},
\xi_{t+1},u_{t}) then plays the role of a belief transition operator, enabling planning directly in
latent space without requiring an explicit observation likelihood.

where the expectation is taken under the latent dynamics induced by the VJEPA predictive model^7^7

6.5 Sampling vs. Point Prediction Planning

The choice of how to use the predictive distribution is central in practice.

MAP/mean (trajectory) planning.

A computationally cheap approximation is to plan using a single representative trajectory, e.g. the
predictive mean (or MAP point when well-defined):

 Z^t+1=μϕ​(Zt,ut)orZ^t+1=arg⁡maxZt+1​pϕ​(Zt+1∣Zt,ut).\hat{Z}_{t+1}=\mu_{\phi}(Z_{t},u_{t})\quad\
 text{or}\quad\hat{Z}_{t+1}=\arg\max_{Z_{t+1}}p_{\phi}(Z_{t+1}\mid Z_{t},u_{t}).

This reduces planning to deterministic rollout and is often effective when the predictive
distribution is unimodal and uncertainty is weak or approximately action-independent.

Sampling-based (distributional) planning.

Alternatively, one can approximate (24) by Monte Carlo rollouts:

 Zt+k+1(m)∼pϕ(⋅∣Zt+k(m),ut+k),m=1,…,M,Z_{t+k+1}^{(m)}\sim p_{\phi}(\,\cdot\mid Z_{t+k}^{(m)},u_
 {t+k}),\qquad m=1,\dots,M,

and estimate the expected cost by sample averaging. MM is the number of samples drawn at each time
step. This is useful in multi-modal dynamics or partial observability, where committing to a single
MAP/mean trajectory can ignore low-probability but potentially high-cost outcomes. Sampling also
naturally supports robust or risk-sensitive planning by changing how samples are aggregated.

7 Information-Theoretic Analysis

In this section, we analyze VJEPA through the lens of information theory, specifically relating the
variational objective to the maximization of predictive mutual information and the Predictive
Information Bottleneck (PIB) principle [79, 14, 2, 3, 85, 84].

7.2 Predictive Information Bottleneck

The Information Bottleneck (IB) method [79] provides a general information-theoretic framework for
finding a compressed representation ZZ of an input source XX that retains the maximum possible
information about a relevant target variable YY. Formally, it seeks to minimize the functional

 ℒIB=I⁡(X,Z)−β​I​(Z,Y),\mathcal{L}_{\text{IB}}=I(X;Z)-\beta I(Z;Y),

where I⁡(⋅,⋅)I(\cdot;\cdot) denotes mutual information^10^10 10 The mutual information I⁡(⋅,⋅)I(\
cdot;\cdot) is a measure of mutual dependence between two random variables defined as the 
Kullback-Leibler divergence between their joint distribution and the product of their marginals: I
(X;Y)=DKL(P(X,Y)∥PX⊗PY)=H(X)−H(X|Y)I(X;Y)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{(X,Y)}\|P_{X}\otimes P_{Y})=H(X)-H(X|
Y). See Appendix.B. and β\beta is a Lagrange multiplier controlling the trade-off between
complexity (compression of XX) and accuracy (prediction of YY). The Predictive Information
Bottleneck (PIB) [14] specializes this principle to temporal data: it aims to extract a summary of
the past (X=x≤tX=x_{\leq t}) that is maximally predictive of the future (Y=xt+ΔY=x_{t+\Delta}),
while discarding irrelevant, noisy, or redundant details.

A core distinction of VJEPA is its adherence to this predictive principle. Unlike autoencoders,
which effectively maximize I⁡(Zt,x≤t)I(Z_{t};x_{\leq t}) by reconstructing the full input history,
VJEPA acts as a PIB. It seeks to capture information about the future while remaining invariant to
non-predictive details of the past.

Let x≤tx_{\leq t} be the observation history. We posit a generative process where observations
consist of a predictable signal SS and nuisance noise NN: x=S+Nx=S+N. The future xt+Δx_{t+\Delta}
depends on history only through SS.

Proposition 1 (Invariance to Nuisance Variability).

Let the input xx decompose into a predictive signal SS and a nuisance variable NN (where NN is
independent of the future target Zt+ΔZ_{t+\Delta}). Generative models maximizing log⁡p⁡(x∣Z)\log p
(x\mid Z) must encode both SS and NN to minimize reconstruction error (since the entropy H⁡(x)H(x)
includes H⁡(N)H(N)). In contrast, the VJEPA objective is invariant to representations that discard
NN provided they preserve the mutual information with the future, I⁡(Zt,Zt+Δ)I(Z_{t};Z_{t+\Delta}).
Consequently, VJEPA admits minimal sufficient statistics that filter out nuisance variability while
maintaining optimal prediction loss. (See Proof in Appendix.H).

This proposition implies that VJEPA allows the representation to be minimal (compressing away
noise), whereas reconstruction-based objectives force the representation to be maximal (retaining
noise). This efficiency is critical for control, where acting on noise can lead to instability.

We can now formally distinguish VJEPA from autoregressive (AR) world models [28, 30] by analyzing

 pϕ​(ZT∣ZC)=𝒩⁡(μϕ​(ZC),Σϕ​(ZC)),p_{\phi}(Z_{T}\mid Z_{C})=\mathcal{N}\!\big(\mu_{\phi}(Z_      (29)
 {C}),\,\Sigma_{\phi}(Z_{C})\big),

which scales naturally to high-dimensional embeddings and aligns with the interpretation of ZtZ_{t}
as a predictive information state.

In this section, we introduce Bayesian JEPA (BJEPA), which extends VJEPA by explicitly factorizing
the predictive model into independent factors: a learned dynamics expert and a constraint-based
expert. This enables principled incorporation of goals, constraints, and task structure while
preserving JEPA’s core design principles.

8.1 Bayesian Factorization via Product of Experts

BJEPA approximates the target posterior distribution using a Product of Experts (PoE) mechanism^12^
12 12 PoE is a machine learning framework that models a probability distribution by combining the
output from several simpler probability models (experts), this is achieved by multiplying their
probability distributions and renormalizing.. Strictly speaking, PoE is the computational operation
used to fuse multiple density functions; however, we structure the experts such that their product
corresponds to a Bayesian update.

We treat the historical context and the auxiliary constraints as conditionally independent sources
of information regarding the future state, combining them via the product of their respective
probability densities:

 p⁡(ZT∣ZC,η)∝plike​(ZT∣ZC)​pprior​(ZT∣η).p(Z_{T}\mid Z_{C},\eta)\propto p_{\text{like}}(Z_{T}  (30)
 \mid Z_{C})p_{\text{prior}}(Z_{T}\mid\eta).

This formulation allows us to interpret the factors through a Bayesian lens:

  • •
   
    plike​(ZT∣ZC)p_{\text{like}}(Z_{T}\mid Z_{C}) acts as the predictive likelihood (or transition
    model). It is learned from data and encodes the system’s typical latent dynamics conditioned on
    past context.

  • •
   
    pprior​(ZT∣η)p_{\text{prior}}(Z_{T}\mid\eta) acts as a latent-space prior. It injects auxiliary
    information η\eta, such as goal regions, safety constraints, or physical manifolds, into the
    inference process.

Mathematically, the product formulation implements a logical AND ^13^13 13 Unlike a mixture model,
which effectively performs a logical OR.: the predicted state must satisfy the dynamics and the
constraints simultaneously. Just as a Kalman Filter update intersects the prediction with a
measurement, BJEPA intersects physical possibility with task necessity. This effectively carves the
latent space to the intersection of physically probable and task-compliant states, i.e. we get a
predictive distribution that only has high probability in regions that are both physically
reachable (dynamics) and closer to the goal (prior). As shown in Appendix I, this formulation ^14^
14 14 The full Eq.(30) is: p⁡(ZT∣ZC,η)=1𝒵​(plike​(ZT∣ZC)×pprior​(ZT∣η))p(Z_{T}\mid Z_{C},\eta)=\

right). In the context of PoE, multiplying probabilities (element-wise multiplication of two PDFs)
corresponds to a logical AND operation on constraints; the resulting distribution represents states
that are consistent with both the dynamics (history) AND the auxiliary constraints (goals). After

marginal scaling. Thus, BJEPA utilizes the PoE mechanism to realize a Bayesian inference process
entirely within the representation space. Compared to a standard autoregressive (AR) model, we
would have to search the entire predicted pixel space to find a path to a goal; in BJEPA, the PoE
constraints this search within the intersected space ^15^15 15 If both pl​i​k​ep_{like} and

8.2 The BJEPA Architecture

BJEPA builds on the standard JEPA/VJEPA architecture by retaining the context/target encoder
structure used for representation learning, and augmenting it with an explicit latent-space prior
encoder. Concretely, BJEPA consists of the following components:

  • •
   
    a context (online) encoder fθf_{\theta} that maps past observations to a latent context

     ZC=fθ​(xC),Z_{C}=f_{\theta}(x_{C}),

    future latent representations conditioned on context;

  • •
   
    a prior encoder that maps auxiliary information η\eta, e.g. goals, constraints, demonstrations,
    physical structure, to a latent-space prior distribution:

     pprior​(ZT∣η),p_{\text{prior}}(Z_{T}\mid\eta),  (32)

    which is new relative to JEPA/VJEPA and enables Bayesian conditioning in representation space.

8.3 Training and Inference

BJEPA allows for disentangled learning: the dynamics (what is possible) can be learned from
large-scale unlabeled data, while the prior (what is desired) can be specified or learned
separately.

Representation Learning: Training with Structural Priors.

The primary training phase focuses on the context encoder fθf_{\theta} and the predictive
likelihood expert plike​(ZT∣ZC)p_{\text{like}}(Z_{T}\mid Z_{C}). While we can assume an
uninformative prior^16^16 16 We can assume an uninformative (uniform) prior, pprior​(ZT∣η)∝1p_{\
text{prior}}(Z_{T}\mid\eta)\propto 1, allowing the model to learn pure latent dynamics driven
solely by observational data. Note that, in a modular architecture such as BJEPA, the training of
the prior (the ”constraint expert”) is often distinct from the training of the dynamics (the
”likelihood expert”). If they were to be trained jointly end-to-end, the dynamics model might
overfit to specific tasks rather than learning general world physics. to learn pure data-driven
dynamics, BJEPA also allows us to inject structural priors pstruct​(ZT)p_{\text{struct}}(Z_{T})
during training. These priors can enforce desirable properties, such as stationarity, sparsity, or
slowness, acting as an information bottleneck that filters out high-entropy nuisance variables
(e.g. the ”Noisy TV” distractors in later experiment).

The objective minimizes the variational negative log-likelihood of the target representations
provided by the target encoder fθ′f_{\theta^{\prime}} (which serves as the “teacher” via EMA). The
target encoder fθ′f_{\theta^{\prime}} provides stable regression targets (the “ground truth” latent
states) derived from the data, exactly as in the standard VJEPA formulation. We optimize the
parameters of the context encoder and the likelihood expert to maximize the predictive likelihood
of the target representations while optionally regularizing the dynamics against the structural
prior. Adapting Eq. (11), the general BJEPA training objective is:

unit Gaussian) and the asymmetry of the target encoder.

Note that we explicitly maximize the log-likelihood of the dynamics expert plike​(ZT∣ZC)p_{\text
{like}}(Z_{T}\mid Z_{C}) rather than the full posterior p⁡(ZT∣ZC,η)p(Z_{T}\mid Z_{C},\eta) (Eq. (30
)). This factorization is deliberate: the likelihood expert is responsible for learning how the
world actually evolves (data-driven physics), while the prior expert encodes how we expect or want
the world to behave (constraints). Optimizing the full, fused posterior directly against the ground
truth ZTZ_{T} can be risky, as it would force the model to pretend that the data satisfies our
arbitrary constraints, for example, ignoring ”Noisy TV” drift because the prior enforces
stationarity (see later the experiment section). Instead, we require the model to fit the
observational data (via the negative log-likelihood term) while being penalized for deviating from
the structural expectations (via the KL regularization term). This ensures accurate physical
modeling while filtering out nuisance variables that violate the structural prior.

Inference with Task-Specific Priors.

Once the dynamics plikep_{\text{like}} are trained, we can introduce new, task-specific priors
ptask​(ZT∣η)p_{\text{task}}(Z_{T}\mid\eta) at inference time (or fine-tuning) to guide the agent,
without retraining the underlying physics model. Unlike the likelihood, the prior expert pprior​
(ZT∣η)p_{\text{prior}}(Z_{T}\mid\eta) encodes task-specific constraints or goals. It is specified
or trained in one of two ways, depending on the nature of the auxiliary information η\eta:

  • •
   
    Learned Goal Priors (e.g. image goals): if η\eta represents a goal observation (e.g. an image
    of the desired outcome), the prior encoder can be trained to map η\eta to a distribution in
    latent space. A common and efficient strategy is to reuse the frozen target encoder fθ′f_{\
    theta^{\prime}} or train a separate lightweight mapper to predict the representation of the
    goal, e.g.

     pprior​(ZT∣ηimg)≈𝒩⁡(ZT∣fθ′​(ηimg),σgoal2​I).p_{\text{prior}}(Z_{T}\mid\eta_{\text{img}})\
     approx\mathcal{N}(Z_{T}\mid f_{\theta^{\prime}}(\eta_{\text{img}}),\sigma^{2}_{\text{goal}}
     I).

    This allows the agent to pursue visual goals without retraining the dynamics model.

  • •
   
    Analytic / Energy-Based Priors: for logical constraints or safety regions (e.g. “avoid region
    ZunsafeZ_{\text{unsafe}}”), the prior can be specified analytically as an energy function:

     pprior​(ZT∣η)∝e−E⁡(ZT,η).p_{\text{prior}}(Z_{T}\mid\eta)\propto e^{-E(Z_{T};\eta)}.

    For example, a “stay close to reference trajectory” prior might be defined as a quadratic
    energy well centered on a reference path.

Inference: Planning via Bayesian Fusion.

During planning, the target encoder is inactive^17^17 17 The target encoder is not required at
inference time unless new observations xTx_{T} are incorporated. (as the future is unknown).
Instead, we fuse the learned dynamics with the task-specified prior using the Product of Experts
mechanism to obtain the BJEPA posterior:

 1. 1.
   
    Likelihood Step: the learned expert plike​(ZT∣ZC)p_{\text{like}}(Z_{T}\mid Z_{C}) predicts
    where the system will go based on history ZCZ_{C}.

 2. 2.
   
    Prior Step: the prior expert pprior​(ZT∣η)p_{\text{prior}}(Z_{T}\mid\eta) specifies where the
    system should go to satisfy auxiliary information η\eta (e.g. goals or safety constraints).

 3. 3.
   
    Fusion: the planner samples or optimizes against the product distribution:

     p⁡(ZT∣ZC,η)∝plike​(ZT∣ZC)×pprior​(ZT∣η).p(Z_{T}\mid Z_{C},\eta)\propto p_{\text{like}}(Z_{T}\
     mid Z_{C})\times p_{\text{prior}}(Z_{T}\mid\eta).

This effectively restricts the search space to the intersection of dynamically feasible and
task-compliant trajectories, enabling efficient latent-space planning without requiring the model
to have seen the specific task η\eta during the representation learning phase.

The planner then samples or optimizes latent states ZTZ_{T} that maximize the joint posterior:

 log⁡p⁡(ZT∣ZC,η)≅log⁡plike​(ZT∣ZC)+log⁡pprior​(ZT∣η)+const.\log p(Z_{T}\mid Z_{C},\eta)\cong\log
 p_{\text{like}}(Z_{T}\mid Z_{C})+\log p_{\text{prior}}(Z_{T}\mid\eta)+\text{const}.

This effectively intersects the manifold of physically feasible futures (from the Likelihood) with
the manifold of task-compliant futures (from the Prior), enabling zero-shot generalization to new
tasks defined by η\eta.

The distinction between the training and inference phases in BJEPA represents a transition from
latent alignment to explicit manifold intersection. During training, the system employs soft fusion
, where the task prior η\eta acts as a regularizer via a KL divergence term; this biases the
dynamics predictor to learn a world model that naturally favors task-compliant regions without
strictly forcing the prediction to originate from the fused distribution p⁡(ZT∣ZC,η)p(Z_{T}\mid Z_
{C},\eta). In contrast, the inference phase utilizes hard fusion through the PoE mechanism,
explicitly intersecting the manifold of physically feasible trajectories with the task-specific
prior. This ensures that the final latent plan is strictly confined to the intersection of what the
world model deems possible and what the task requires, enabling efficient, zero-shot generalization
to new constraints.

MAP vs. Sampling in the Posterior.

Given the fused posterior in Eq. (30), prediction and planning may formally proceed via:

  • •
   
    MAP prediction:

     Z^TMAP=arg⁡maxZT​[log⁡plike​(ZT∣ZC)+log⁡pprior​(ZT∣η)];\hat{Z}_{T}^{\text{MAP}}=\arg\max_{Z_
     {T}}\big[\log p_{\text{like}}(Z_{T}\mid Z_{C})+\log p_{\text{prior}}(Z_{T}\mid\eta)\big];

  • •
   
    Sampling-based prediction:

     ZT(m)∼p(ZT∣ZC,η),m=1,…,M.Z_{T}^{(m)}\sim p(Z_{T}\mid Z_{C},\eta),\qquad m=1,\dots,M.

    with MM being the total number of samples.

MAP prediction is computationally efficient and sufficient for unimodal posteriors, while sampling
becomes essential in multi-modal or risk-sensitive settings where preserving the full uncertainty
profile is necessary for robust control.

8.4 Sequential BJEPA as Latent Bayesian Filtering

When applied sequentially over time, BJEPA naturally induces a recursive filtering mechanism in
representation space. By treating the context ZtZ_{t} as the current state summary (Zt=fθ​(x≤t)Z_
{t}=f_{\theta}(x_{\leq t}) summarizes history) and the auxiliary information ηt+1\eta_{t+1} as a
“virtual observation” or constraint for the next step, the Product of Experts formulation
implements a Bayesian filter update.

The BJEPA Filter Recursion.

Standard Bayesian filtering consists of a prediction step (time update) via a transition model and
a correction step (measurement update) via an observation likelihood. BJEPA implements this
structure entirely in latent space:

 1. 1.
   
    Prediction (Time Update): The predictive likelihood plike​(Zt+1∣Zt)p_{\text{like}}(Z_{t+1}\mid
    Z_{t}) propagates the state forward based on system dynamics.

 2. 2.
   
    Correction (Constraint Update): The latent prior pprior​(Zt+1∣ηt+1)p_{\text{prior}}(Z_{t+1}\mid
    \eta_{t+1}) acts as a measurement likelihood for the auxiliary task information ηt+1\eta_{t+1},
    effectively “correcting” the predicted state to satisfy the goal or constraint.

Combining these via the PoE mechanism yields the recursive update for the belief over the future
trajectory:

 p⁡(Zt+1∣Zt,ηt+1)∝plike​(Zt+1∣Zt)⏟Dynamics (Prediction)×pprior​(Zt+1∣ηt+1)⏟Constraint
 (Correction).p(Z_{t+1}\mid Z_{t},\eta_{t+1})\propto\underbrace{p_{\text{like}}(Z_{t+1}\mid    (34)
 Z_{t})}_{\text{Dynamics (Prediction)}}\times\underbrace{p_{\text{prior}}(Z_{t+1}\mid\eta_
 {t+1})}_{\text{Constraint (Correction)}}.

This formulation mirrors Bayesian filtering, but avoids explicit observation likelihoods. It also
unifies planning and filtering: planning becomes the task of filtering the state distribution
conditioned on the evidence of “optimality” or goal achievement encoded by η\eta.

Comparison to Standard Filtering.

In a standard Kalman Filter or HMM, the correction comes from sensory data xt+1x_{t+1} via p⁡
(xt+1∣Zt+1)p(x_{t+1}\mid Z_{t+1}). In BJEPA, the correction comes from the intention or constraint
ηt+1\eta_{t+1} via ppriorp_{\text{prior}}. This allows the agent to reason about the “posterior”
future that is consistent with both physics and goals, without needing to generate or score
high-dimensional observations.

Remark 2 (Belief-Space MPC).

This perspective allows BJEPA planning to be interpreted as Stochastic Model Predictive Control
(MPC) performed via belief propagation. The likelihood expert propagates the physically feasible
set (the learned belief), while the prior expert injects goals or constraints and carves out the
task-relevant subset. The resulting posterior p⁡(Zt+1∣Zt,ηt+1)p(Z_{t+1}\mid Z_{t},\eta_{t+1})
represents the optimal control distribution, generalizing the notion of “Control as Inference” to
latent representation spaces and yielding a belief-space MPC formulation without explicit state
estimation.

potentials or logical constraints) that are difficult to normalize.

  • •

    (the learned dynamics).

  • •
   
    The second term acts as a task cost or potential field pulling the solution towards the goal.

Since all components in VJEPA/BJEPA are differentiable, this optimization can be solved efficiently
via gradient descent directly in the representation space, effectively performing planning as
inference without creating a separate discrete graph or search tree.

8.8 Modularity, Transfer, and Continual Learning

A fundamental advantage of the BJEPA factorization (Eq. 30) is the semantic disentanglement of
environmental dynamics from task objectives. This modularity unlocks capabilities that are
difficult to achieve in monolithic world models:

Zero-Shot Transfer via Prior Swapping.

In standard reinforcement learning or world modeling (e.g. Dreamer, MuZero), the reward function is
often entangled with the representation or dynamics. To change tasks, one must typically retrain or
fine-tune the model weights. In BJEPA, the predictive likelihood plikep_{\text{like}} learns
“objective-agnostic physics.” Once trained, it can be transferred zero-shot to arbitrarily many new
tasks simply by swapping the prior expert pprior​(Z∣η)p_{\text{prior}}(Z\mid\eta). The agent
understands how the world evolves (Likelihood) independently of what it is currently trying to
achieve (Prior). This ’reuse dynamics, adapt priors’ paradigm facilitates zero-shot task transfer.

Continual Learning without Catastrophic Forgetting.

Because task-specific information is injected at inference time via the prior (or the energy
function), the dynamics model does not need to be updated to accommodate new goals. This mitigates
catastrophic forgetting: the agent does not overwrite its knowledge of physics to learn a new task.
The shared world model accumulates general knowledge, while task specificity is handled by
lightweight, modular priors.

Summary.

BJEPA generalizes JEPA-based world models by introducing an explicit ‘Bayesian posterior’ in
representation space. This enables uncertainty-aware planning, structured priors, belief
propagation, and principled transfer and continual learning, while retaining JEPA’s core
advantages: the avoidance of observation reconstruction, reward supervision, and autoregressive
likelihood factorization.

Comparison with Existing WM Frameworks.

Table.3 contextualizes BJEPA within the landscape of latent world models. While methods such as 
Dreamer handle uncertainty, they rely on computationally expensive observation reconstruction.
Conversely, recent JEPA-based world models avoid reconstruction but typically lack explicit
uncertainty quantification and rely on monolithic, learned reward functions. BJEPA uniquely
occupies the intersection of these capabilities: it is likelihood-free (like JEPA) yet
probabilistic (like Dreamer), and it replaces monolithic rewards with modular, energy-based priors.
This specific combination allows for flexible, zero-shot task specification without retraining the

9 Toy Experiment: a “Noisy TV” Linear System

We present an analytically tractable experiment designed to stress-test the nuisance invariance
property (Proposition 1) of the JEPA family (JEPA/VJEPA/BJEPA). We construct an environment where
observations contain a low-variance signal embedded in high-variance, yet predictable, nuisance
noise (‘‘distractors’’). This setup mimics the ‘‘Noisy-TV’’ problem^19^19 19 The “Noisy-TV” problem
originated as a thought experiment in Burda et al. [17] regarding curiosity-driven reinforcement
learning. It describes a failure mode where an agent, rewarded for seeking novelty or prediction
error, becomes fixated on a source of uncontrollable, unpredictable noise (like a TV displaying
static). Because the noise is inherently unpredictable, the agent continuously extracts high
intrinsic rewards without making meaningful progress, effectively becoming a “couch potato”., where
a model is presented with a dominant source of high-entropy, task-irrelevant variability that
threatens to overwhelm the signal representation. Implementation details can be found in Appendix.J
.

10 Discussion

In this work, we have formalized Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPA) not merely as a
self-supervised training trick, but as a principled probabilistic framework for learning latent
dynamical systems. By introducing Variational JEPA (VJEPA) and its Bayesian extension BJEPA, we
bridged the gap between deterministic representation learning and probabilistic world models. Our
analysis and experiments support several key conclusions regarding the nature of predictive
learning.

10.1 Unifying JEPA with Probabilistic State-Space Models

JEPA is probably best understood as a predictive state-space model trained by representation
prediction rather than observation likelihood. A central contribution of this work is the
decoupling of sequential modeling from autoregressive observation likelihoods. Standard world
models (e.g. Dreamer [30]) and generative sequence models (e.g. Transformers [83]) typically
conflate the two, forcing the model to predict the next observation xt+1x_{t+1} given history. This
forces the latent state to account for high-entropy local noise.

VJEPA demonstrates that sequential structure does not harm representation learning; only
autoregressive factorization over pixels does. By adopting a time-indexed specialization (as in
Section.5 and Section.6), where contexts and targets are explicitly associated with temporal
indices, VJEPA learns a mathematically rigorous latent transition model pϕ​(Zt+1|Zt)p_{\phi}(Z_
{t+1}|Z_{t}). Notably, this transition kernel does not need to be autoregressive (as seen in Eq.12)
and is capable of belief propagation and filtering without ever estimating a density over pixels.
This positions VJEPA as a scalable foundation for model-based control in high-dimensional, noisy
environments.

10.2 Nuisance Invariance: The “PCA vs. CCA” Distinction

Our ”Noisy TV” experiment (Section 9) provides empirical validation for the Information-Theoretic
analysis in Section 7. The failure of VAE and AR baselines on high-variance distractors highlights
a fundamental distinction in representation learning objectives:

  • •
   
    Generative Reconstruction (e.g. VAE): These models implicitly perform a non-linear
    generalization^20^20 20 VAEs do not perform standard linear PCA by design, but they often
    implicitly discover the same principal directions (subspaces) as PCA while learning,
    particularly due to their regularization and training dynamics [66]. of Principal Component
    Analysis (PCA) [66]. They prioritize latent dimensions that explain the maximum variance in the
    input xx. If nuisance noise has higher variance than the signal (as in our experiment), a
    reconstruction objective is mathematically compelled to model the noise.

  • •
   
    Predictive Association (VJEPA): JEPA-based models implicitly perform a non-linear
    generalization of Canonical Correlation Analysis^21^21 21 CCA is a multivariate statistical
    method that finds the strongest linear relationships between two sets of variables, which
    creates new composite variables (canonical variates) from each set that are maximally
    correlated with each other [87]. (CCA [87]) or the Information Bottleneck. As established in
    Theorem 4 (Section 7), minimizing the VJEPA loss maximizes a lower bound on the mutual
    information between the past and the future (I⁡(Zt,Zt+Δ)I(Z_{t};Z_{t+\Delta})). Therefore,
    VJEPA (implicitly CCA) prioritizes dimensions with the highest predictive mutual information.
    It will ignore the noise regardless of its magnitude. This ”nuisance invariance” property
    (Proposition 1) makes VJEPA as a semantic noise filter for downstream planning.

10.3 Applications and Broader Impact

While this work focuses on theoretical foundations, the VJEPA framework enables new capabilities in
applied domains where state estimation and planning are required but observation reconstruction is
costly or unnecessary.

Robotics and Embodied AI.

In visual robotic control, xtx_{t} represents high-dimensional sensory streams (e.g. camera feeds,
lidar). Standard world models (e.g. Dreamer [30]) often spend significant capacity modeling local
textures and camera noise. VJEPA allows the agent to learn a task-specific physics engine in latent
space. For a manipulation task, the context ZtZ_{t} captures the geometry of objects, while the
predictive model pϕ​(Zt+1∣Zt,ut)p_{\phi}(Z_{t+1}\mid Z_{t},u_{t}) predicts the consequences of
motor commands. Planning can be performed via the VJEPA-MPC algorithm (Algo.2) to minimize a latent
distance to a target goal embedding, enabling efficient visual control without pixel-level
prediction. Furthermore, the BJEPA extension allows for zero-shot transfer: a robot can learn the
environmental dynamics (Likelihood Expert) once, and then swap in different goal constraints (Prior
Expert) at inference time to solve varied tasks without retraining the underlying model.

Game Playing and Strategic Reasoning.

In high-dimensional game environments (e.g. Atari, Minecraft), VJEPA functions as a self-supervised
latent simulator. Unlike MuZero [69], which couples representation learning with a specific reward
signal, VJEPA learns a reward-agnostic model of the game mechanics. The predictive model pϕ​
(Zt+1∣Zt,ut)p_{\phi}(Z_{t+1}\mid Z_{t},u_{t}) captures the transition rules and physics of the game
world. This enables planning via Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS [77]) or trajectory optimization in
the latent space, allowing the agent to reason about long-term strategies and effectively transfer
the learned dynamics to new in-game tasks or modified rule sets without retraining the visual
encoder.

Language-as-Action Planning.

Current LLMs typically operate by minimizing the surface-level entropy of the next token. VJEPA
offers an alternative view where language generation can be framed as planning in semantic space.
Here, the ‘world’ is informational or symbolic, and the latent state ZtZ_{t} represents the current
discourse status or reasoning state.

  • •
   
    Control Inputs: The tokens (or chunks of text) act as discrete actions driving the system
    (ut∈𝒱u_{t}\in\mathcal{V}). utu_{t} can represent both generated content and user inputs such as
    prompts, which function as exogenous control signals that steer the initial semantic
    trajectory.

  • •
   
    Latent Dynamics: The model learns how choosing a word utu_{t} updates the semantic state:

     Zt+1∼pϕ​(Zt+1∣Zt,ut).Z_{t+1}\sim p_{\phi}(Z_{t+1}\mid Z_{t},u_{t}).

In this framework, a response is not evaluated by its likelihood ∏p⁡(ut∣u<t)\prod p(u_{t}\mid u_
{<t}), but by its effect on the latent world. Generation becomes a trajectory optimization problem:
identifying a sequence of tokens ut:Tu_{t:T} that drives the initial thought ZtZ_{t} to a target
state ZgoalZ_{\text{goal}} (e.g. a state satisfying a logical constraint or answer condition). This
allows text generation to leverage planning algorithms such as Beam Search [53], Monte Carlo Tree
Search (MCTS [77]), or latent-space Cross-Entropy Method (CEM [52]), which decouples reasoning from
surface-form statistics and potentially helps address hallucinations and long-horizon coherence.

10.4 Limitations and Future Directions

While VJEPA provides a rigorous probabilistic footing, our experiments revealed specific
trade-offs:

Expressivity of the Predictive Distribution.

In our experiments, the probabilistic VJEPA sometimes slightly underperformed the deterministic
JEPA in peak accuracy, or required more careful hyperparameter tuning. We attribute this to the
unimodality of the Gaussian assumption used in our implementation of pϕp_{\phi}. In environments
with complex, multi-modal bifurcations (e.g. a robot encountering an obstacle and choosing left or
right), a unimodal Gaussian may average distinct modes, resulting in a blurry or incorrect belief.
Future work should explore more expressive predictive heads, such as Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs)
[36] or Latent Diffusion heads, to capture complex aleatoric uncertainty while retaining the VJEPA
objective.

Optimization Dynamics.

We observed that the balance between the predictive loss and the KL regularization terms
(controlled by β\beta in the VJEPA objective Eq. 11, and γ\gamma in the BJEPA objective Eq. 33) is
important. If regularization is too strong, the representation collapses; if too weak, the variance
estimates become uncalibrated. Developing adaptive mechanisms for balancing these terms, similar to
KL-balancing in latent overshooting, is a promising direction.

11 Conclusion

This work introduces Variational JEPA (VJEPA) and Bayesian JEPA (BJEPA), establishing the first
formal probabilistic framework for Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures. By recasting JEPA as a
variational inference problem in representation space, we resolve the ambiguity^22^22 22 Standard
JEPA trains a deterministic predictor using Mean Squared Error. In stochastic environments with
multimodal futures (e.g. a fork in the road), such a predictor converges to the conditional mean,
which may be a physically invalid state (e.g. the average of ‘go left’ and ‘go right’ is ‘hit the
wall’). This creates ambiguity regarding the semantics of the embedding: does it represent a
specific future or an average? VJEPA resolves this by explicitly modeling the predictive
distribution. Further, the variational derivation provides the theoretical guarantee that the
learned state maximizes predictive mutual information, a necessary condition for it to serve as a
sufficient information state for control. regarding the probabilistic semantics of predictive
embeddings and their suitability for control.

VJEPA: A Probabilistic Foundation.

VJEPA generalizes standard deterministic JEPA by learning an explicit predictive distribution over
future latent states. We showed that minimizing the VJEPA objective maximizes a variational lower
bound on the predictive mutual information between the past and the future. Unlike generative world
models, VJEPA achieves this without an autoregressive observation likelihood, effectively filtering
out high-entropy nuisance variables (the ”Noisy TV” problem) while retaining the information
necessary for dynamics modeling. A key practical advantage of this formulation is the ability to
perform principled uncertainty estimation (e.g. constructing credible intervals) by drawing
multiple samples (M≫1M\gg 1) from the predictive distribution during inference ^23^23 23 We
distinguish between two sampling regimes using KK and MM to denote total number of samples. First.
Training Sampling (K=1K=1): drawn from the target encoder distribution qθ′(⋅∣xT)q_{\theta^{\prime}}
(\cdot\mid x_{T}) during training to generate a concrete regression target. A single sample is
sufficient because the stochasticity of mini-batch updates averages out the noise over time. 
Second. Inference Sampling (M≫1M\gg 1): drawn from the predictor pϕ(⋅∣ZC)p_{\phi}(\cdot\mid Z_{C})
during testing or planning (inference) to estimate uncertainty. Multiple samples are required here
to accurately map the distribution’s shape and construct credible intervals. This is also a key
advantage of VJEPA/BJEPA over deterministic JEPA., a capability absent in deterministic JEPA.

BJEPA: Modular and Structural Control.

We extended this VJEPA framework to BJEPA, which factorizes prediction into a learned Likelihood
Expert (dynamics) and a Prior Expert (constraints). This modularity allows for the injection of
structural priors, such as stationarity or goal-directedness, directly into the latent inference
process via Product of Experts which bridges the gap between ”what is physically possible” and
”what is task-relevant”. This separation enables zero-shot task transfer and robust planning in the
presence of non-stationary noise.

Theoretical and Practical Unification.

Our analysis unifies JEPA with classical ideas from Predictive State Representations (PSRs) and
Bayesian filtering. We established that observation reconstruction is theoretically unnecessary for
optimal control, provided the latent state captures sufficient predictive mutual information.
Furthermore, we demonstrated that:

  • •
   
    Sequential modeling does not imply autoregression: one can learn rigorous temporal dynamics p⁡
    (Zt+1|Zt)p(Z_{t+1}|Z_{t}) without the computational burden of factorizing the high-dimensional
    observation density p⁡(xt+1|x≤t)p(x_{t+1}|x_{\leq t}).

  • •
   
    Predictive representations suffice for control: we proved that under standard conditions, an
    optimal policy can be derived solely from the predictive latent state, rendering pixel
    reconstruction redundant for planning.

  • •
   
    Likelihood-free world models can be principled: VJEPA breaks the historical conflation of
    probabilistic rigor with pixel-level likelihoods. It replaces heuristic regression losses with
    a formal variational framework operating entirely in latent space, ensuring principled
    uncertainty estimation and information maximization without modeling nuisance variability.

By combining the representational efficiency of JEPA with the rigor of probabilistic state-space
models, VJEPA positions itself as a foundational framework for scalable, robust, uncertainty-aware
world modeling and planning in high-dimensional, possibly noisy, real-world environments.

 {T}\mid Z_{C})           hat{Z}_{T}\|^{2}\right)\right]
                          =12​σ2​‖ZT−Z^T‖2+d2​log⁡(2​π​σ2),\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|Z_

where dd is the dimensionality of the embedding space.

Appendix B Relationships Between Information-Theoretic Quantities

In this section, we summarize the fundamental relationships between entropy, cross-entropy,
Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, and mutual information. These identities underpin the theoretical
analysis of VJEPA and BJEPA. For further info, readers are encouraged to refer to standard texts
such as [50]. Here, let’s denote p⁡(x)p(x) as the true (or target) data distribution and q⁡(x)q(x)
the approximating (or predicted) distribution.

B.1 Cross-Entropy, Entropy, and KL Divergence

Cross-entropy is the sum of the true entropy and the KL divergence. Intuitively, it represents the
average number of bits needed to encode data from pp using a code optimized for qq, which is the
optimal bits (entropy of pp) plus the penalty for using the wrong code (KL divergence):

 H(p,q)=H(p)+DKL(p∥q),H(p,q)=H(p)+D_{\text{KL}}(p\,\|\,q),  (36)

where the terms are defined as:

  • •
   
    Cross-Entropy: H(p,q)=−∑xp(x)logq(x)H(p,q)=-\sum_{x}p(x)\log q(x)

  • •
   
    Entropy: H(p)=−∑xp(x)logp(x)H(p)=-\sum_{x}p(x)\log p(x)

  • •
   
    KL Divergence: DKL(p∥q)=∑xp(x)logp⁡(x)q⁡(x)D_{\text{KL}}(p\,\|\,q)=\sum_{x}p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}
    {q(x)}

B.2 Mutual Information and Entropy

Mutual information (MI) measures the reduction in uncertainty (entropy) of one variable given
knowledge of another. It satisfies the following additive identities:

 I⁡(X,Y)\displaystyle I(X;Y) =H⁡(X)−H⁡(X∣Y)\displaystyle=H(X)-H(X\mid Y)         (37)
 I⁡(X,Y)\displaystyle I(X;Y) =H⁡(Y)−H⁡(Y∣X)\displaystyle=H(Y)-H(Y\mid X)         (38)
 I⁡(X,Y)\displaystyle I(X;Y) =H⁡(X)+H⁡(Y)−H⁡(X,Y)\displaystyle=H(X)+H(Y)-H(X,Y)  (39)

where H⁡(X∣Y)H(X\mid Y) is the conditional entropy. We used the first formula later in our proof of
the non-collapsed global optimum associated with the VJEPA objective (Theorem 1, and its detailed
proof in Appendix.F).

B.3 Chain Rule for Mutual Information

The chain rule for mutual information describes how the information provided by a pair of variables
(X,Y)(X,Y) about a third variable ZZ can be decomposed into two distinct stages. The step-by-step
expansion is as follows:

 1. 1.
   
    Definition of Mutual Information: The mutual information between (X,Y)(X,Y) and ZZ is the
    difference between the marginal entropy of ZZ and the conditional entropy of ZZ given (X,Y)
    (X,Y):

     I⁡(X,Y,Z)=H⁡(Z)−H⁡(Z∣X,Y)I(X,Y;Z)=H(Z)-H(Z\mid X,Y)  (40)

 2. 2.
   
    Decomposition of Conditional Entropy: Using the identity for conditional entropy, we can
    express H⁡(Z∣X,Y)H(Z\mid X,Y) by first conditioning on XX:

     H⁡(Z∣X,Y)=H⁡(Z∣X)−I⁡(Y;Z∣X)H(Z\mid X,Y)=H(Z\mid X)-I(Y;Z\mid X)  (41)

    Rearranging this reveals that H⁡(Z∣X,Y)H(Z\mid X,Y) represents the remaining uncertainty in ZZ
    after observing both XX and YY.

 3. 3.
   
    Substitution and Re-grouping: Substituting the decomposition into the original definition:

B.4 Symmetry Properties

A crucial distinction between these metrics is their behavior under argument swapping:

  • •
   
    Mutual Information is Symmetric:

     I⁡(X,Y)=I⁡(Y,X).I(X;Y)=I(Y;X).

    Intuitively, the amount of information XX provides about YY is identical to the information YY
    provides about XX. This symmetry arises from the identity I⁡(X,Y)=H⁡(X)+H⁡(Y)−H⁡(X,Y)I(X;Y)=H
    (X)+H(Y)-H(X,Y), where the joint entropy H⁡(X,Y)H(X,Y) is invariant to ordering.

  • •
   
    KL Divergence is Asymmetric:

     DKL(p∥q)≠DKL(q∥p)(generally).D_{\text{KL}}(p\,\|\,q)\neq D_{\text{KL}}(q\,\|\,p)\quad(\text
     {generally}).

    Minimizing DKL(p∥q)D_{\text{KL}}(p\,\|\,q) (forward KL) encourages the approximation qq to
    cover the support of pp (mode-covering), whereas minimizing DKL(q∥p)D_{\text{KL}}(q\,\|\,p)
    (reverse KL) encourages qq to seek a single mode of pp (mode-seeking).

  • •
   
    Cross-Entropy is Asymmetric:

     H⁡(p,q)≠H⁡(q,p).H(p,q)\neq H(q,p).

    Since H(p,q)=H(p)+DKL(p∥q)H(p,q)=H(p)+D_{\text{KL}}(p\,\|\,q), the asymmetry of the KL
    divergence imparts asymmetry to the cross-entropy.

Common Confusion: Cross-Entropy vs. Joint Entropy.

It is easy to confuse Cross-Entropy H⁡(p,q)H(p,q) with Joint Entropy H⁡(X,Y)H(X,Y).

  • •
   
    Cross-Entropy H⁡(p,q)H(p,q) is defined between two probability distributions pp and qq:

     H(p,q)=−∑xp(x)logq(x).H(p,q)=-\sum_{x}p(x)\log q(x).

    It is NOT symmetric (H⁡(p,q)≠H⁡(q,p)H(p,q)\neq H(q,p)) because the roles of the weighting
    distribution p⁡(x)p(x) and the log-term q⁡(x)q(x) are distinct.

  • •
   
    Joint Entropy H⁡(X,Y)H(X,Y) is defined over two random variables XX and YY:

     H(X,Y)=−∑x∑yp(x,y)logp(x,y).H(X,Y)=-\sum_{x}\sum_{y}p(x,y)\log p(x,y).

    It IS symmetric (H⁡(X,Y)=H⁡(Y,X)H(X,Y)=H(Y,X)) because the joint probability p⁡(x,y)p(x,y) is
    invariant to ordering.

B.5 Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO)

In variational inference, we often aim to approximate an intractable posterior p⁡(z∣x)p(z\mid x)
using a simpler distribution q⁡(z∣x)q(z\mid x). The Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) provides a
tractable lower bound on the log-likelihood of the data (the evidence) log⁡p⁡(x)\log p(x). It
relates the log-evidence to the KL divergence between the approximate and true posteriors:

 logp(x)=ℒELBO(q)+DKL(q(z∣x)∥p(z∣x)),\log p(x)=\mathcal{L}_{\text{ELBO}}(q)+D_{\text{KL}}(q(z  (45)
 \mid x)\,\|\,p(z\mid x)),

where the ELBO is defined as:

 ℒELBO​(q)=𝔼q⁡(z∣x)​[log⁡p⁡(x,z)−log⁡q⁡(z∣x)].\mathcal{L}_{\text{ELBO}}(q)=\mathbb{E}_{q(z\    (46)
 mid x)}[\log p(x,z)-\log q(z\mid x)].

Since the KL divergence is non-negative (DKL≥0D_{\text{KL}}\geq 0), the ELBO serves as a valid
lower bound on the evidence:

 log⁡p⁡(x)≥ℒELBO​(q).\log p(x)\geq\mathcal{L}_{\text{ELBO}}(q).  (47)

Maximizing the ELBO is therefore equivalent to minimizing the KL divergence between the approximate
posterior q⁡(z∣x)q(z\mid x) and the true posterior p⁡(z∣x)p(z\mid x), effectively pushing the
approximation towards the true distribution.

B.6 Non-Negativity Properties

An important property of these quantities is their non-negativity (for discrete variables), which
ensures they act as valid objective functions or distance/divergence metrics:

 1. 1.

 2. 2.

 3. 3.

Appendix C Compare VJEPA with Hidden Markov Models (HMMs)

VJEPA shares a structural resemblance to a Hidden Markov Model (HMM) in that both posit a latent
state sequence to explain temporal data. However, the resemblance is only partial: classical HMMs
are generative probabilistic models of observations, whereas VJEPA is a representation-predictive
model whose training objective operates entirely in the latent space. Here we clarify the
similarities and differences.

C.1 Classical HMMs

An HMM defines a discrete-time latent Markov process {st}t≥1\{s_{t}\}_{t\geq 1} and a sequence of
observations {xt}t≥1\{x_{t}\}_{t\geq 1} via the joint factorization:

 p(s1:T,x1:T)=p(s1)∏t=2Tp(st∣st−1)∏t=1Tp(xt∣st),p(s_{1:T},x_{1:T})=p(s_{1})\prod_{t=2}^{T}p    (48)
 (s_{t}\mid s_{t-1})\prod_{t=1}^{T}p(x_{t}\mid s_{t}),

where p⁡(st∣st−1)p(s_{t}\mid s_{t-1}) is the transition model and p⁡(xt∣st)p(x_{t}\mid s_{t}) is
the emission (observation) model. Learning^24^24 24 A (discrete) HMM assumes an unobserved state
sequence s1:Ts_{1:T} where each st∈{1,…,N}s_{t}\in\{1,\dots,N\} (the state space) and an observed
sequence x1:Tx_{1:T} where each xt∈{v1,…,vM}x_{t}\in\{v_{1},\dots,v_{M}\} (the observation
symbols). It is parameterized by λ=(A,B,π)\lambda=(A,B,\pi), where: A=[ai​j]A=[a_{ij}] with ai​j=
Pr⁡(st=j∣st−1=i)a_{ij}=\Pr(s_{t}=j\mid s_{t-1}=i) (transitions); B=[bj​(k)]B=[b_{j}(k)] with bj​(k)
=Pr⁡(xt=vk∣st=j)b_{j}(k)=\Pr(x_{t}=v_{k}\mid s_{t}=j) (emissions); and π=[πi]\pi=[\pi_{i}] with πi=
Pr⁡(s1=i)\pi_{i}=\Pr(s_{1}=i) (initial distribution). The three canonical tasks are: (i) evaluation
: compute Pr(x1:T∣λ)\Pr(x_{1:T}\mid\lambda) (e.g. via the Forward algorithm); (ii) decoding: find
the most likely hidden state sequence argmaxs1:TPr(s1:T∣x1:T,λ)\arg\max_{s_{1:T}}\Pr(s_{1:T}\mid x_
{1:T},\lambda) (e.g. via the Viterbi algorithm); and (iii) learning: estimate λ\lambda from data
(e.g. via the Baum-Welch/EM algorithm). Details can be found in standard course materials, e.g. 
Computational Intelligence, which the author taught at University of Aberdeen. typically proceeds
by maximum likelihood (e.g. Expectation-Maximization), and inference computes the filtering
posterior p⁡(st∣x≤t)p(s_{t}\mid x_{\leq t}) or smoothing posterior p(st∣x1:T)p(s_{t}\mid x_{1:T}).

In modern state-space model variants (SSMs), sts_{t} may be continuous and the dynamics nonlinear.
However, the defining property remains: the model is trained to explain observations through an
explicit hidden-to-observation likelihood p⁡(xt∣st)p(x_{t}\mid s_{t}).

C.2 VJEPA as “Latent-Only” Predictive Modeling

VJEPA is not an observation-likelihood model. As detailed in Section 4, VJEPA introduces a latent
representation for the target region and learns a predictive distribution (Eq. 7):

 pϕ​(ZT∣ZC,ξT),p_{\phi}(Z_{T}\mid Z_{C},\xi_{T}),

matched against an amortized target-encoder “inference” distribution (Eq. 10):

 qθ′​(ZT∣xT).q_{\theta^{\prime}}(Z_{T}\mid x_{T}).  (49)

Training minimizes a representation-space negative log-likelihood plus regularization (Eq. 11),
strictly avoiding the computation of a sensory emission likelihood p⁡(xT∣ZT)p(x_{T}\mid Z_{T}).
While one could optionally introduce a decoder pψ​(xT∣ZT)p_{\psi}(x_{T}\mid Z_{T}), VJEPA does not
optimize it; the learning signal flows solely through latent prediction.

C.3 Similarities: Latent-State Semantics, Dynamics, and Beliefs

Both HMMs and VJEPA posit latent variables that summarize information needed for prediction.

  • •
   
    Latent State: In an HMM, sts_{t} is the latent state; in VJEPA, ZtZ_{t} plays an analogous role
    as a predictive latent embedding.

  • •
   
    Transitions: The VJEPA predictive distribution pϕ​(ZT∣ZC,ξT)p_{\phi}(Z_{T}\mid Z_{C},\xi_{T})
    acts as a transition model in latent space, mapping a “current” latent summary (context) to a
    distribution over a “future” latent representation.

  • •
   
    Filtering: If qθ′​(ZT∣xT)q_{\theta^{\prime}}(Z_{T}\mid x_{T}) has nontrivial variance, VJEPA
    supports belief-state style reasoning (means, covariances, sampling), similar to probabilistic
    filtering in HMMs.

C.4 Differences

(1) Observations are not modeled.

HMMs are trained to maximize logp(x1:T)\log p(x_{1:T}) via the emission likelihood p⁡(xt∣st)p(x_{t}
\mid s_{t}). In contrast, VJEPA focuses exclusively on predictive structure in representation
space. It learns a predictive latent process without committing to a generative model of sensory
observations, thereby avoiding the modeling of high-entropy noise.

(2) The “inference model” is defined by the encoder, not the generator.

In an HMM, the posterior p⁡(st∣x≤t)p(s_{t}\mid x_{\leq t}) is derived by inverting the generative
model Eq. (48). In VJEPA, qθ′​(ZT∣xT)q_{\theta^{\prime}}(Z_{T}\mid x_{T}) is an amortized
distribution defined by the target encoder (the “teacher”). It serves as the ground truth for the
predictor, rather than being an approximation of the predictor’s posterior.

(3) The Markov property is optional.

The HMM assumes a strict first-order Markov property p⁡(st∣s<t)=p⁡(st∣st−1)p(s_{t}\mid s_{<t})=p(s_
{t}\mid s_{t-1}). VJEPA does not require a particular temporal factorization; it can predict
multi-step futures directly (Zt→Zt+ΔZ_{t}\to Z_{t+\Delta}) without iterating a 1-step transition,
and without autoregressive observation factorization (see Section 5).

(4) Representation geometry is learned, not prescribed.

Classical HMMs typically impose strong inductive biases on the latent space (e.g. discrete states
or Gaussian dynamics). VJEPA learns the embedding geometry via neural encoders, making it closer in
spirit to continuous Predictive State Representations (PSRs) than to classical HMM fitting.

C.5 Sequential VJEPA as an “HMM Without Emissions”

Since an HMM is defined by the tuple (Transition, Emission), one can interpret sequential VJEPA as
learning a latent predictive process (the Transition component) while deliberately omitting the
Emission optimization. This design choice retains probabilistic semantics and uncertainty
propagation while discarding the burden of reconstructing irrelevant observation details.

Appendix D Kalman-Filter-Like Interpretation of VJEPA

Although VJEPA does not explicitly model observation likelihoods, its operation in latent space
closely mirrors the prediction step of a Bayesian filter. This analogy becomes exact in the limit
of linear-Gaussian dynamics, providing a rigorous grounding for VJEPA’s uncertainty estimates and
justifying its use in belief-space planning.

D.1 The Linear-Gaussian Benchmark

Consider a classical linear dynamical system with Gaussian noise. The latent state sts_{t} evolves
according to:

 st+1=A​st+B​ut+wt,wt∼𝒩⁡(0,Q),s_{t+1}=As_{t}+Bu_{t}+w_{t},\quad w_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,Q),  (50)

where AA is the state transition matrix, BB is the control matrix, and QQ is the process noise
covariance.

A Kalman Filter maintains the belief state as a Gaussian distribution bt​(st)=𝒩⁡(μt,Pt)b_{t}(s_{t})
=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t},P_{t}). The prediction step (or time update) propagates this belief forward:

 μt+1|t\displaystyle\mu_{t+1|t} =A​μt+B​ut,\displaystyle=A\mu_{t}+Bu_{t},   (51)
 Pt+1|t\displaystyle P_{t+1|t}  =A​Pt​A⊤+Q.\displaystyle=AP_{t}A^{\top}+Q.  (52)

Here, the mean is shifted deterministically by the dynamics, while the uncertainty (covariance)
grows due to the additive process noise QQ.

D.2 VJEPA as Generalized Latent Prediction

VJEPA generalizes this structure to non-linear, amortized inference in representation space. If we
interpret the context embedding ZtZ_{t} as a sufficient statistic for the current belief history
(i.e. Zt≅μtZ_{t}\cong\mu_{t}), then the probabilistic predictor pϕp_{\phi} implements a generalized
transition update.

Recall the VJEPA predictive distribution^25^25 25 Unlike Theorem.6.7 (MAP/mean predictive control
is optimal under quadratic costs), in which we assumed the Gaussian covariance Σ\Sigma of the
predictive distribution does not depend on utu_{t}, i.e. pϕ​(Zt+1∣Zt,ut)=𝒩⁡(Zt+1∣μϕ​(Zt,ut),Σ)p_{\

here we make it a more general case.:

 pϕ​(Zt+1∣Zt,ut)=𝒩⁡(μϕ​(Zt,ut),Σϕ​(Zt,ut)).p_{\phi}(Z_{t+1}\mid Z_{t},u_{t})=\mathcal{N}\big(\mu_
 {\phi}(Z_{t},u_{t}),\Sigma_{\phi}(Z_{t},u_{t})\big).

Comparing this to the Kalman equations (51) and (52):

 1. 1.

    approximates the non-linear flow of the system. Unlike the fixed matrix AA, it can model
    state-dependent transitions and complex control interactions.

In the standard Kalman Filter, the predictive uncertainty Pt+1|tP_{t+1|t} depends on the previous

Thus, VJEPA implicitly captures the full Kalman prediction logic: aleatoric uncertainty is output

D.3 Amortized Correction

A full Bayesian filter also requires a correction step (measurement update) to incorporate new
observations xt+1x_{t+1}:

 μt+1=μt+1|t+Kt​(xt+1−H​μt+1|t).\mu_{t+1}=\mu_{t+1|t}+K_{t}(x_{t+1}-H\mu_{t+1|t}).

VJEPA handles this differently. Instead of an explicit update equation involving observation
likelihoods and Kalman gains KtK_{t}, VJEPA amortizes the correction into the context encoder fθf_
{\theta}.

 Zt+1=fθ​(x≤t+1).Z_{t+1}=f_{\theta}(x_{\leq t+1}).

The encoder learns to map the updated history x≤t+1x_{\leq t+1} directly to the posterior latent
state Zt+1Z_{t+1}. The VJEPA training objective (minimizing the divergence between the predicted
belief pϕ(⋅|Zt)p_{\phi}(\cdot|Z_{t}) and the encoded belief qθ′(⋅|xt+1)q_{\theta^{\prime}}(\cdot|x_
{t+1})) effectively forces the predictor to be consistent with the encoder’s implicit Bayesian
updates.

Interpretation.

Collapse is avoided because predictive uncertainty depends on the target input. VJEPA therefore
prevents collapse through information mismatch, not architectural heuristics. Theorem 1 formalizes
the intuitive point that if target embeddings vary with the target input, then a collapsed context
representation forces the predictor to fit a single unconditional distribution to multiple distinct
targets, incurring an irreducible prediction loss. Consequently, any solution satisfying the
collapsed mode hypothesis cannot be globally optimal for the VJEPA objective. Under the stated
assumptions, preventing collapse is therefore an intrinsic property of the objective itself, rather
than a consequence of architectural heuristics or training asymmetries.

J.2 Model Architectures and Training Strategy

We implement five models to map the current observation xt∈ℝDxx_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{x}} to a
latent representation zt∈ℝDzz_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{z}}. To impose a hard trade-off between signal
and distractor encoding, we set the latent dimension equal to the true signal dimension (Dz=Ds=4D_
{z}=D_{s}=4). All models utilize strictly linear transformations (f⁡(x)=W​xf(x)=Wx) without bias
terms (‘bias=False‘), matching the linear nature of the data generation process.

We compare two modeling paradigms: Generative Reconstruction (VAE, AR) operating in pixel space,
and Joint-Embedding Prediction (JEPA, VJEPA, BJEPA) operating via latent-space autoregression.

J.2.1 Generative Baselines (Pixel Reconstruction)

These models must reconstruct the full observation xx, including high-variance distractors.

Linear Variational Autoencoder (VAE).

The VAE treats the problem as a static modeling task. It maps xtx_{t} to a latent distribution q⁡
(zt|xt)q(z_{t}|x_{t}) and reconstructs xtx_{t}.

  • •
   
    Architecture: A probabilistic encoder with two linear heads μϕ,log⁡σϕ2\mu_{\phi},\log\sigma^{2}
    _{\phi} mapping Dx→DzD_{x}\to D_{z}, and a deterministic decoder x^t=Wd​e​c​zt\hat{x}_{t}=W_
    {dec}z_{t}.

  • •
   
    Objective: The Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO)^27^27 27 Under the assumption of a Gaussian
    likelihood with identity covariance, p⁡(xt|zt)=𝒩⁡(x^t,I)p(x_{t}|z_{t})=\mathcal{N}(\hat{x}_
    {t},I), minimizing the negative log-likelihood is equivalent to minimizing the squared
    Euclidean distance ‖xt−x^t‖2\|x_{t}-\hat{x}_{t}\|^{2} plus a constant.:

     ℒVAE=∥xt−x^t∥2+βDK​L(q(zt|xt)∥𝒩(0,I)).\mathcal{L}_{\text{VAE}}=\|x_{t}-\hat{x}_{t}\|^{2}+\
     beta D_{KL}(q(z_{t}|x_{t})\|\mathcal{N}(0,I)).

  • •
   
    Hypothesis: To minimize reconstruction error, the VAE must prioritize features with the highest
    variance. We predict it will encode distractors and ignore the signal.

Linear Pixel-Autoregressive (AR).

The AR model is a predictive autoencoder operating in pixel space. It compresses xtx_{t} into a
bottleneck to predict the next observation xt+1x_{t+1}.

  • •
   
    Architecture: A deterministic encoder zt=We​n​c​xtz_{t}=W_{enc}x_{t} mapping Dx→DzD_{x}\to D_
    {z} and a predictive decoder x^t+1=Wp​r​e​d​zt\hat{x}_{t+1}=W_{pred}z_{t} mapping Dz→DxD_{z}\to
    D_{x}.

  • •
   
    Objective: Mean Squared Error in pixel space: ℒAR=‖xt+1−x^t+1‖2\mathcal{L}_{\text{AR}}=\|x_
    {t+1}-\hat{x}_{t+1}\|^{2}.

  • •
   
    Hypothesis: Since distractors are predictable (dt+1≈0.9​dtd_{t+1}\approx 0.9d_{t}), the AR
    model must track them to minimize prediction error, wasting capacity on distractor texture.

J.2.2 Joint-Embedding Architectures (Latent Autoregression)

These models are time-indexed, order-1 Markovian in latent space, trained to predict zt+1z_{t+1}
without reconstructing pixels.

Linear JEPA (Deterministic).

JEPA aligns the predicted next state with the encoded future state.

  • •
   
    Architecture:

     1. 1.
       
        Context Encoder: zt=We​n​c​xtz_{t}=W_{enc}x_{t}.

     2. 2.
       
        Predictor: A linear layer z^t+1=Wp​r​e​d​zt\hat{z}_{t+1}=W_{pred}z_{t} mapping Dz→DzD_{z}\
        to D_{z}.

     3. 3.
       
        Target Encoder (EMA): Same architecture as Context Encoder.

  • •
   
    Objective: VICReg loss, minimizing prediction error ‖z^t+1−zt+1′‖2\|\hat{z}_{t+1}-z^{\prime}_
    {t+1}\|^{2} with variance/covariance regularization to prevent collapse.

Linear Probabilistic VJEPA.

VJEPA extends JEPA by treating the prediction as a probabilistic distribution.

  • •
   
    Architecture:

     1. 1.
       
        Deterministic Encoder: zt=We​n​c​xtz_{t}=W_{enc}x_{t}.

     2. 2.
       
        Probabilistic Predictor: Two linear heads outputting μp​r​e​d,log⁡σp​r​e​d2=fϕ​(zt)\mu_
        {pred},\log\sigma^{2}_{pred}=f_{\phi}(z_{t}) mapping Dz→DzD_{z}\to D_{z}.

     3. 3.
       
        Target Encoder (EMA): Two heads outputting μθ′,log⁡σθ′2\mu_{\theta^{\prime}},\log\sigma^{2}
        _{\theta^{\prime}} mapping Dx→DzD_{x}\to D_{z}.

  • •
   
    Objective: The regularized negative log-likelihood (Eq. 11):

     ℒVJEPA=𝔼z~t+1∼qθ′[−logpϕ(z~t+1|zt)]+βDK​L(qθ′(zt+1′|xt+1)∥𝒩(0,I)).\mathcal{L}_{\text{VJEPA}}=
     \mathbb{E}_{\tilde{z}_{t+1}\sim q_{\theta^{\prime}}}[-\log p_{\phi}(\tilde{z}_{t+1}|z_{t})]+\
     beta D_{KL}(q_{\theta^{\prime}}(z^{\prime}_{t+1}|x_{t+1})\|\mathcal{N}(0,I)).

Linear Bayesian JEPA (BJEPA).

BJEPA incorporates a static Bayesian prior to filter out unpredictable dynamics by fusing a
Predictive Likelihood (Dynamics Expert) with a Latent-Space Prior (Static Expert).

  • •
   
    Architecture: Identical to VJEPA, but adds learnable static parameters
    μp​r​i​o​r,log⁡σp​r​i​o​r2\mu_{prior},\log\sigma^{2}_{prior} (initialized to zero) to define a
    preferred latent manifold.

  • •

     μp​o​s​t,log⁡σp​o​s​t2=PoE​((μd​y​n,σd​y​n2),(μp​r​i​o​r,σp​r​i​o​r2)).\mu_{post},\log\sigma^
     {2}_{post}=\text{PoE}((\mu_{dyn},\sigma^{2}_{dyn}),(\mu_{prior},\sigma^{2}_{prior})).

  • •
   
    Objective: ℒBJEPA=ℒVJEPA+γDK​L(plike(zt+1|zt)∥pprior(zt+1))\mathcal{L}_{\text{BJEPA}}=\mathcal
    {L}_{\text{VJEPA}}+\gamma D_{KL}(p_{\text{like}}(z_{t+1}|z_{t})\|p_{\text{prior}}(z_{t+1})).

Stochastic Training Strategy.

In our implementation, we parameterize the context encoder fθ​(xC)f_{\theta}(x_{C}) as a
deterministic map, delegating uncertainty modeling entirely to the predictive transition pϕ​(ZT∣ZC)
p_{\phi}(Z_{T}\mid Z_{C}) and the target inference qθ′​(ZT∣xT)q_{\theta^{\prime}}(Z_{T}\mid x_{T}).
For both VJEPA and BJEPA, optimization of the variational objective (Eq. 11 and Eq. 33) requires
estimating an expectation over the target distribution 𝔼ZT∼qθ′\mathbb{E}_{Z_{T}\sim q_{\theta^{\
prime}}}. Following standard practice in stochastic gradient variational inference (SGVI), we
approximate this expectation using a single Monte Carlo sample (K=1K=1) drawn from the target
encoder’s distribution qθ′​(ZT∣xT)q_{\theta^{\prime}}(Z_{T}\mid x_{T}) via the reparameterization
trick at each training step. While this single-sample estimate is noisy for any individual data
point, the stochasticity of mini-batch gradient descent (SGD) smooths the objective over the course
of training, providing an unbiased gradient estimator.

Note that the sampling operation is applied only to the target encoder qθ′q_{\theta^{\prime}} to
obtain a concrete regression target ZTZ_{T}. We do not sample from the predictor pϕp_{\phi}.
Instead, the predictor outputs the parameters of its belief distribution (e.g. μϕ,σϕ2\mu_{\phi},\
sigma^{2}_{\phi}), and we analytically evaluate the negative log-likelihood density (Gaussian in
our implementation) of the sampled target ZTZ_{T} under these parameters. Mathematically, this
evaluates how well the predictor’s belief explains the specific realization drawn from the target
encoder’s belief.

Necessity of Target Sampling.

Sampling ZTZ_{T} from the target distribution qθ′q_{\theta^{\prime}} (rather than simply using its
mean μθ′\mu_{\theta^{\prime}}) is strictly necessary to prevent variance collapse in the predictor.
Consider the Gaussian negative log-likelihood term minimized by the predictor:

 ℒpred≈(ZT−μϕ)22​σϕ2+12​log⁡(σϕ2).\mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}\approx\frac{(Z_{T}-\mu_{\phi})^{2}}{2\
 sigma^{2}_{\phi}}+\frac{1}{2}\log(\sigma^{2}_{\phi}).

If we were to use the target mean as the training target (i.e. set ZT=μθ′Z_{T}=\mu_{\theta^{\
prime}}), the predictor could learn to output μϕ≈μθ′\mu_{\phi}\approx\mu_{\theta^{\prime}}
perfectly, driving the squared error term to zero. This would encourage the variance σϕ2\sigma^{2}_
{\phi} to collapse to zero (or log⁡σϕ2→−∞\log\sigma^{2}_{\phi}\to-\infty) to minimize the second
term, resulting in a deterministic, overconfident model. By sampling ZT∼𝒩⁡(μθ′,σθ′2)Z_{T}\sim\
mathcal{N}(\mu_{\theta^{\prime}},\sigma^{2}_{\theta^{\prime}}), the target becomes a “moving
target” that fluctuates around the mean according to the target’s uncertainty. The squared error
term (ZT−μϕ)2(Z_{T}-\mu_{\phi})^{2} remains non-zero, forcing the predictor to maintain a non-zero
variance σϕ2\sigma^{2}_{\phi} to cover the distribution of potential futures.

Implementation and Limitations.

In our experimental implementation, we parameterize both the target encoder qθ′q_{\theta^{\prime}}
and the predictor pϕp_{\phi} as independent diagonal Gaussian distributions:

 qθ′​(Z∣xT)=𝒩⁡(Z,μθ′​(xT),diag​(σθ′2​(xT))),pϕ​(Z∣ZC)=𝒩⁡(Z,μϕ​(ZC),diag​(σϕ2​(ZC))).q_{\theta^{\
 prime}}(Z\mid x_{T})=\mathcal{N}(Z;\mu_{\theta^{\prime}}(x_{T}),\text{diag}(\sigma^{2}_{\theta^{\
 prime}}(x_{T}))),\quad p_{\phi}(Z\mid Z_{C})=\mathcal{N}(Z;\mu_{\phi}(Z_{C}),\text{diag}(\sigma^
 {2}_{\phi}(Z_{C}))).

While this choice simplifies optimization and admits closed-form KL terms, the unimodal Gaussian
assumption represents a limitation. In environments with complex, multi-modal bifurcations (e.g.
distinct “go left” vs. “go right” futures), a single Gaussian may average the modes, resulting in a
high-variance belief centered on an invalid state. Future work could address this by implementing
more expressive heads, such as Gaussian Mixture Models or normalizing flows.

J.2.3 Temporal Alignment in Training and Evaluation

All models receive observation pairs (xt,xt+1)(x_{t},x_{t+1}) during training, where xtx_{t}
denotes the current observation and xt+1x_{t+1} denotes the next-step observation. The temporal
alignment differs between model families based on their predictive structure:

Training Alignment.

  • •
   
    VAE: trained on single observations xtx_{t} with reconstruction target xtx_{t} (no temporal
    prediction).

  • •
   
    AR: trained on pairs (xt,xt+1)(x_{t},x_{t+1}) with pixel-space prediction target xt+1x_{t+1}.

  • •
   
    JEPA/VJEPA/BJEPA: trained on pairs (xt,xt+1)(x_{t},x_{t+1}) with latent-space prediction target
    zt+1=fθ′​(xt+1)z_{t+1}=f_{\theta^{\prime}}(x_{t+1}).

Evaluation Alignment.

As predictive models (AR, JEPA, VJEPA, BJEPA) are trained to predict future states, their latent
representations should be evaluated against the future ground-truth signal st+1s_{t+1}, not the
current signal sts_{t}. Concretely:

  • •
   
    VAE: The encoder output zt=μϕ​(xt)z_{t}=\mu_{\phi}(x_{t}) represents the current state and is
    evaluated against sts_{t}.

  • •
   
    AR: The encoder output zt=We​n​c​xtz_{t}=W_{enc}x_{t} is designed to predict xt+1x_{t+1}, hence
    is evaluated against st+1s_{t+1}.

  • •
   
    JEPA: The predictor output z^t+1=Wp​r​e​d​zt\hat{z}_{t+1}=W_{pred}z_{t} explicitly predicts the
    next latent state and is evaluated against st+1s_{t+1}.

  • •
   
    VJEPA: The predictor mean μp​r​e​d​(zt)\mu_{pred}(z_{t}) represents the expected next latent
    state and is evaluated against st+1s_{t+1}.

  • •
   
    BJEPA: At inference, the hard-fused posterior mean μp​o​s​t=PoE​(μd​y​n,μp​r​i​o​r)\mu_{post}=\
    text{PoE}(\mu_{dyn},\mu_{prior}) represents the constrained prediction of the next latent state
    and is evaluated against st+1s_{t+1}.

This temporal alignment ensures that each model’s representation is evaluated against the
appropriate ground-truth signal consistent with its predictive objective.

J.2.4 Linear Probe Evaluation Protocol

To assess how well each model’s latent representation captures the true underlying signal sts_{t}
(while ignoring the distractor dtd_{t}), we employ a linear probe methodology. For each model, we
extract latent representations and fit a linear regression to predict the ground-truth signal, then
measure the coefficient of determination (R2R^{2}) on held-out test data.

Latent Extraction.

The latent representation used for probing differs by model, reflecting their distinct
architectures:

  • •
   
    VAE: ztprobe=μϕ​(xt)z_{t}^{\text{probe}}=\mu_{\phi}(x_{t}) — the encoder mean, representing the
    current state.

  • •
   
    AR: ztprobe=We​n​c​xtz_{t}^{\text{probe}}=W_{enc}x_{t} — the encoder output, which implicitly
    predicts the next state.

  • •
   
    JEPA: ztprobe=Wp​r​e​d​(We​n​c​xt)=z^t+1z_{t}^{\text{probe}}=W_{pred}(W_{enc}x_{t})=\hat{z}_
    {t+1} — the predictor output, explicitly predicting the next latent state.

  • •
   
    VJEPA: ztprobe=μp​r​e​d​(We​n​c​xt)z_{t}^{\text{probe}}=\mu_{pred}(W_{enc}x_{t}) — the
    predictor mean, representing the expected next latent state.

  • •
   
    BJEPA: ztprobe=μp​o​s​tz_{t}^{\text{probe}}=\mu_{post} — the hard-fused posterior mean computed
    via Product of Experts:

     μp​o​s​t=σp​r​i​o​r2⋅μd​y​n+σd​y​n2⋅μp​r​i​o​rσd​y​n2+σp​r​i​o​r2,\mu_{post}=\frac{\sigma^{2}
     _{prior}\cdot\mu_{dyn}+\sigma^{2}_{dyn}\cdot\mu_{prior}}{\sigma^{2}_{dyn}+\sigma^{2}_
     {prior}},

    where (μd​y​n,σd​y​n2)(\mu_{dyn},\sigma^{2}_{dyn}) are the dynamics predictor parameters and
    (μp​r​i​o​r,σp​r​i​o​r2)(\mu_{prior},\sigma^{2}_{prior}) are the learned static prior
    parameters.

Probe Fitting and Evaluation.

Given extracted latents {ziprobe}i=1Ntrain\{z_{i}^{\text{probe}}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\text{train}}} from
the training set and corresponding ground-truth signals {sitarget}i=1Ntrain\{s_{i}^{\text{target}}
\}_{i=1}^{N_{\text{train}}}, we fit a linear regression:

 s^=Wprobe​zprobe+bprobe,\hat{s}=W_{\text{probe}}z^{\text{probe}}+b_{\text{probe}},

where (Wprobe,bprobe)(W_{\text{probe}},b_{\text{probe}}) are learned via ordinary least squares.
The target signal stargets^{\text{target}} is chosen according to the temporal alignment described
above: sts_{t} for VAE, and st+1s_{t+1} for all predictive models (AR, JEPA, VJEPA, BJEPA).

We then evaluate on the test set by computing the R2R^{2} score:

 R2=1−∑i‖sitarget−s^i‖2∑i‖sitarget−s¯‖2,R^{2}=1-\frac{\sum_{i}\|s_{i}^{\text{target}}-\hat{s}_{i}\
 |^{2}}{\sum_{i}\|s_{i}^{\text{target}}-\bar{s}\|^{2}},

where s¯\bar{s} is the mean of the test targets. An R2R^{2} close to 1 indicates that the latent
representation successfully captures the true signal, while an R2R^{2} close to 0 indicates that
the representation has failed to disentangle signal from distractor.

Interpretation.

This evaluation protocol directly tests the core hypothesis: predictive models operating in latent
space (JEPA, VJEPA, BJEPA) should learn representations that capture the predictable signal while
discarding unpredictable distractors, whereas generative models (VAE, AR) operating in pixel space
are forced to allocate capacity to high-variance distractors regardless of their task relevance.
The use of predictor outputs (rather than encoder outputs) for JEPA-family models ensures we
evaluate the representation that the model is actually trained to produce, i.e. the predicted
future state.

J.4 Results

We measure performance using the coefficient of determination (R2R^{2}) between the learned latent
ZtZ_{t} and the true signal sts_{t} via a linear probe. The quantitative results ^28^28 28 We do
not compare training loss values across methods because their objectives define optimality in
fundamentally different metric spaces. Generative models (VAE, AR) minimize pixel-level
reconstruction error, whereas JEPA-based models minimize feature distance or maximize likelihood in
an abstract latent space with arbitrary scaling. Consequently, the raw loss magnitudes are not
comparable. are summarized in Table.4 and visualized across all 9 noise scales in Fig.3.
Additionally, Fig.4 provides a qualitative view of the signal reconstruction at low, medium, and
high noise levels.

Failure of Generative Models.

As shown in Table.7 and Fig.3, the reconstructive baselines (VAE, AR) suffer catastrophic
performance degradation as the distractor noise scale increases. At the highest noise scale (σ=8.0\
sigma=8.0), VAE and AR signal recovery drops to R2≈0.50R^{2}\approx 0.50 and 0.580.58 respectively,
while their noise recovery remains high (R2=0.62R^{2}=0.62 and R2=0.45R^{2}=0.45). This confirms
that likelihood-based objectives force the models to encode high-variance distractors, leading to
representation collapse on the target task.

Robustness of Joint-Embedding Architectures.

In contrast, all joint-embedding architectures (JEPA, VJEPA, BJEPA) demonstrate robustness to the
“Noisy TV” distractor. They maintain high signal recovery (R2>0.85R^{2}>0.85) even at σ=8.0\sigma=
8.0, effectively filtering out the distractor dynamics. In this specific trial, the deterministic
JEPA achieved the highest final accuracy (R2=0.93R^{2}=0.93), though it exhibited higher variance
in training stability compared to the probabilistic variants^29^29 29 While the deterministic JEPA
achieved high performance in the reported run (Seed 111), we observed in auxiliary experiments with
different random seeds that it is prone to occasional training collapse (sudden performance drops
to R2<0.5R^{2}<0.5) at intermediate noise scales. We attribute this to the difficulty of optimizing
a deterministic predictor on highly stochastic targets. The probabilistic methods (VJEPA and BJEPA)
consistently exhibited greater training stability across seeds by accounting for aleatoric
uncertainty via their variance outputs..

Stability and Optimization.

The high-variance noise makes the target embeddings (zt+1z_{t+1}) jittery and unpredictable. As
standard JEPA is deterministic, it struggles to average out this jitter, leading to potential
training instability. Even in this successful run, JEPA showed a momentary instability at Noise
Scale 3.0, dropping to 0.8410.841 (Test) while VJEPA/BJEPA remained at >0.90>0.90. This collapse at
Scale 3.0 likely indicates optimization difficulties where the variance regularization term fought
against the predictive loss. This hints at the optimization difficulty deterministic models face
when the noise magnitude creates complex gradients, even if the model recovered at Scale 4.0.

J.5 Computational Resources

All experiments were conducted using the standard CPU runtime provided by Google Colab [23]. The
key hardware specifications are summarized below:

  • •
   
    Platform: Google Colab (Linux x86_64)

  • •
   
    CPU: Intel(R) Xeon(R) CPU @ 2.20GHz

  • •
   
    CPU Topology: 2 Logical CPUs (1 Physical Core, 2 Threads per core)

  • •
   
    Memory (RAM): 13.61 GB

  • •
   
    Disk Space: 242.49 GB

  • •
   
    Instruction Set: x86_64 with AVX2 and FMA support

Given the lightweight nature of the linear toy experiment (latent dimension Dz=4D_{z}=4), training
was performed entirely on the CPU, with individual model runs completing in approximately 10-35
seconds (see Table 7).
