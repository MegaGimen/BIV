Exact Expressive Power of Transformers with Padding

William Merrill ^†^†thanks: Work partially conducted as a PhD student at New York University. 
Affiliation: Allen Institute for AI Email: willm@allenai.org    Ashish Sabharwal Affiliation: Allen
Institute for AI Email: ashishs@allenai.org

Abstract

Chain of thought is a natural inference-time method for increasing the computational power of
transformer-based large language models (LLMs), but comes at the cost of sequential decoding. Are
there more efficient alternatives to expand a transformer’s expressive power without adding
parameters? We consider transformers with padding tokens as a form of parallelizable test-time
compute. We show that averaging-hard-attention, masked-pre-norm transformers with polynomial
padding recognize precisely the class 𝖥𝖮​-uniform​𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{FO}\textrm{-uniform}\ \mathsf{TC}^{0}
of extremely parallelizable problems. While the 𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{TC}^{0} upper bound was known, proving a
matching lower bound had been elusive. Further, our novel analysis reveals the precise expanded
power of padded transformers when coupled with another form of inference-time compute, namely
dynamically increasing depth via looping. Our core technical contribution is to show how padding
helps bring the notions of complete problems and reductions, which have been a cornerstone of
classical complexity theory, to the formal study of transformers. Armed with this new tool, we
prove that padded transformers with O⁡(logd⁡n)\mathrm{O}(\log^{d}n) looping on inputs of length nn
recognize exactly the class 𝖥𝖮\mathsf{FO}-uniform 𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} of moderately parallelizable
problems. Thus, padding and looping together systematically expand transformers’ expressive power:
with polylogarithmic looping, polynomially padded transformers recognize precisely the class
𝖥𝖮​-uniform​𝖭𝖢\mathsf{FO}\textrm{-uniform}\ \mathsf{NC}, the best that could be expected without
losing parallelism (unless 𝖭𝖢=𝖯\mathsf{NC}=\mathsf{P}). Our results thus motivate further
exploration of padding and looping as parallelizable alternatives to chain of thought for test-time
compute.

1 Introduction

Due to the computational limitations of transformers (Merrill and Sabharwal, 2023c; Strobl et al.,
2024; Chiang, 2025), solving complex reasoning problems requires extending their computational
power at inference time, typically by allowing models to generate long chains of thought (CoT)
before their outputs (Wei et al., 2022; Nye et al., 2022). Theoretical work has shown how CoT
expands the expressive power of transformers to sequential problems outside the class 𝖳𝖢0\mathsf
{TC}^{0} of highly parallelizable problems, but it also sacrifices parallelism (Merrill and
Sabharwal, 2023b; Li et al., 2024), making inference slow. Are there alternative inference-time
compute approaches that increase expressive power while preserving parallelism?

One method for parallelizable inference-time compute with LLMs is using padding tokens rather than
CoT. Padding can be understood as restricted CoT where the tokens on the chain are restricted to
some “blank” symbol rather than tokens generated by the LLM. Since all the input tokens are known
in advance, padding is more parallelizable than CoT. There have been some attempts to make padding
practical with mixed results (Goyal et al., 2024; Pfau et al., 2024), but it is not fully
understood. Specifically, while it is known that padded transformers remain in 𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{TC}^{0},
it has been open and elusive whether they can solve all problems in 𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{TC}^{0} or even the
smaller class 𝖠𝖢0\mathsf{AC}^{0} (Pfau et al., 2024).

formal model, detailed in Section 2, assumes a fully uniform transformer with fixed parameters,
fixed width, logarithmic or polynomial precision (showing their equivalence in this setting),
masked pre-norm, and causal masking (Theorem 1 also applies to unmasked transformers, showing their
equivalence under padding). with polynomial padding as 𝖥𝖮​-uniform​𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{FO}\textrm{-uniform}\
\mathsf{TC}^{0}, answering an open question (Pfau et al., 2024). The result emerges through a
finer-grained analysis of transformers in terms of string logics (Merrill and Sabharwal, 2023a;
Chiang et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024; Yang and Chiang, 2024). Thinking in terms of logic, we show
that nkn^{k} padding tokens give transformers enough “storage space” to resolve any first-order
majority logic formula over kk variables. This suffices to capture all of 𝖥𝖮​-uniform​𝖳𝖢0\mathsf
{FO}\textrm{-uniform}\ \mathsf{TC}^{0} (Mix Barrington et al., 1990), giving an exact
characterization of the expressive power of such transformers.

This first result, however, does not clarify whether padded transformers can be minimally extended
to gain expressivity beyond 𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{TC}^{0}. To address this, we consider the combination of
padding with looping, i.e., repeating a block of layers dynamically as a function of input length,
also referred to as universal transformers (Dehghani et al., 2019; Giannou et al., 2023). This can
be understood as a form of inference-time compute where padding controls the computation width and
looping controls the computation depth—crucially, without adding any parameters. If the number of
repetitions is minimal (e.g., sublinear) in sequence length, this transformer model remains highly
parallelizable relative to CoT (Merrill and Sabharwal, 2025).

Extending this result, we show that, for d≥1d\geq 1, transformers with polynomial padding and O⁡
(logd⁡n)\mathrm{O}(\log^{d}n) looping recognize exactly 𝖥𝖮​-uniform​𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{FO}\textrm{-uniform}
\ \mathsf{TC}^{d}. Thus log or polylog-looped transformers can solve many problems outside 𝖳𝖢0\
mathsf{TC}^{0} under standard complexity conjectures, including boolean formula evaluation,
iterated matrix multiplication, graph connectivity, and context-free language recognition. It also
follows that polylog-looped padded transformers converge in expressive power to 𝖥𝖮​-uniform\mathsf
{FO}\textrm{-uniform} 𝖭𝖢\mathsf{NC}, the ceiling that could be expected while preserving
parallelism under standard complexity conjectures.

Our first result about the expressive power of fixed-depth padded transformers provides crucial
insight for our extended results about looped padded transformers (cf. Figure 1), by allowing, for
the first time, the use of familiar tools of reductions and complete problems from classical
complexity theory in the analysis of transformers. First, the ability to express 𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{TC}^{0}
implies that padded transformers can implement 𝖥𝖮\mathsf{FO} reductions. We then scaffold the
ability to implement 𝖥𝖮\mathsf{FO} reductions to show that transformers can also implement 𝖫\mathsf
{L} reductions, via known results about transformers’ ability to implement graph connectivity, an
𝖭𝖫\mathsf{NL}-complete problem under 𝖥𝖮\mathsf{FO} reductions. We then use this to show that O⁡
(logd⁡n)\mathrm{O}(\log^{d}n) looping recognizes exactly 𝖥𝖮​-uniform​𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{FO}\textrm
{-uniform}\ \mathsf{TC}^{d}, via the “wide” 𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} circuit evaluation problem, which we
prove to be complete for this class under 𝖫\mathsf{L} reductions.

Finally, we prove a novel circuit complexity result that 𝖥𝖮​-uniform​𝖳𝖢d=𝖫​-uniform​𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{FO}\
textrm{-uniform}\ \mathsf{TC}^{d}=\mathsf{L}\textrm{-uniform}\ \mathsf{TC}^{d} for any d≥1d\geq 1.
This was obtained in our analysis of looped transformers with d≥1d\geq 1. Besides being potentially
of independent interest in circuit complexity, this allows us to see that, for d≥1d\geq 1, O⁡
(logd⁡n)\mathrm{O}(\log^{d}n) looping allows recognizing not just 𝖥𝖮​-uniform​𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{FO}\textrm
{-uniform}\ \mathsf{TC}^{d} but also 𝖫​-uniform​𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{L}\textrm{-uniform}\ \mathsf{TC}^{d}
(because they are the same).

Overall, our results provide an exact characterization of the expressive power of padded
fixed-depth transformers as 𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{TC}^{0}, answering an open question (Pfau et al., 2024).
Further, we exactly characterize the expressive power of polylog-looped padded transformers, which
reveals that padding and looping dramatically extend the expressive power of transformers under
standard complexity conjectures. We take these results to motivate empirical investigation of
padding and looping as forms of inference-time compute that are more parallelizable than standard
CoT.

2 Preliminaries

2.1 Averaging-Hard-Attention Transformers

Following previous work, we analyze a model of transformers that is slightly idealized compared to
standard soft-attention transformers. Specifically, we analyze transformers with averaging hard
attention (AHAT; also called “saturated” by Merrill et al., 2022) and masked pre-norm (Merrill and
Sabharwal, 2023b). AHAT attention heads only attend to the values that maximize the attention
score; in the case of ties, the head returns a uniform average of all values with tied scores.
Masked pre-norm means that the transformer can apply a linear projection before pre-norm at the
beginning of each sublayer, which is useful for reading individual values from the residual stream
without interference from other values. Overall, we believe these idealizations are minimal changes
that make it easier to implement algorithmic constructions that generalize to any sequence length.

More formally, we define the transformer sublayers in this AHAT model following Merrill and
Sabharwal (2025). Each sublayer uses masked pre-norm (Xiong et al., 2020; Merrill and Sabharwal,
2023b), reading input 𝐳i=𝗅𝖺𝗒𝖾𝗋​_​𝗇𝗈𝗋𝗆​(𝐌𝐡i)\mathbf{z}_{i}=\mathsf{layer\_norm}(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{h}
_{i}), where 𝐡i\mathbf{h}_{i} is the previous sublayer output, 𝐌\mathbf{M} is some matrix, and

2.2 Padded and Looped AHATs

We assume a looped transformer model as previously defined by Merrill and Sabharwal (2025):

Definition 4 (d⁡(n)d(n)-looped transformer).

A looped transformer’s layers are partitioned into blocks ⟨A,B,C⟩\langle A,B,C\rangle. On an input
of length nn, block BB is repeated depth-wise d⁡(n)d(n) times.

Looped transformers provide a way of dynamically scaling width at test time by looping a block of
layers. In addition, we also consider padding (Pfau et al., 2024) as a simple way to increase
width:

Definition 5 (w⁡(n)w(n)-padded transformer).

On an input of length nn, we first append w⁡(n)w(n) “blank” tokens (□∉Σ\square\not\in\Sigma) and
then feed this full string through the transformer.

Looping and padding can also be combined, giving us the following language class for transformers:

2.3 Circuit Complexity

We define the circuit complexity classes 𝖠𝖢d\mathsf{AC}^{d} and 𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} in the standard
way (Arora and Barak, 2009; Strobl et al., 2024). A circuit family is a mapping 𝒞={Cn}n=0∞\mathcal
{C}=\{C_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty} from input lengths nn to circuits that take nn inputs. 𝖠𝖢d\mathsf{AC}^
{d} is the class of language that can be recognized by polynomial-size, fixed-depth circuit
families with unbounded-arity AND/OR gates. 𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} is the same class but augmented with
unbounded-arity MAJ gates that take a majority vote over their input bits.

It will often be useful to talk about uniform variants of these classes. A 𝖷\mathsf{X}-uniform
circuit family obeys that constraint that circuit CnC_{n} for input size nn can be “built” from the
input string 1n1^{n} using computation in 𝖷\mathsf{X}. We will consider two standard notions of
uniformity: 𝖥𝖮\mathsf{FO} uniformity (which is equivalent to 𝖣𝖫𝖮𝖦𝖳𝖨𝖬𝖤\mathsf{DLOGTIME} uniformity;
Mix Barrington et al., 1990) and the weaker 𝖫\mathsf{L} uniformity (i.e., log-space uniformity).
For a circuit class 𝖷𝖢\mathsf{XC}, we will write 𝖠\mathsf{A}-uniform 𝖷𝖢\mathsf{XC} to denote that
class constrained to circuit families satisfying 𝖠\mathsf{A} uniformity. See Appendix B for formal
definitions of each type of uniformity and Strobl et al. (2024) for further context.

Finally, we will also use the notion of completeness for circuit classes. Informally, an 𝖷\mathsf
{X}-complete problem is a problem in 𝖷\mathsf{X} to which any other problem in 𝖷\mathsf{X} can be
mapped via some simple reduction 𝖱\mathsf{R}. Whether a problem is complete depends on the notion
of reduction used. See Definition 9 for a more formal definition of reductions, which makes
completeness fully defined.

        ∃i.P\exists i.P means setting ii to some value m∈[1,n]m\in[1,n] makes PP true (more
        formally, ⟦P⟧w,v|i=m=1\llbracket P\rrbracket^{w,v\mid i=m}=1).

     2. (b)
       
        ∀i.P\forall i.P means setting ii to any value m∈[1,n]m\in[1,n] make ϕ\phi true.

An 𝖥𝖮\mathsf{FO} formula PP with no free variables is called a sentence and returns a value in
{0,1}\{0,1\} for each input string. The language defined by a sentence is the set of strings mapped
to 1. The nesting depth of an 𝖥𝖮\mathsf{FO} formula is the depth of its syntactic tree constructed
by the rules above. The number of distinct variables is the number of different variable names used
in PP. This can be minimized by allowing two independent quantifiers in parallel subformulas to use
the same variable name.

It is known that the class of languages defined by 𝖥𝖮\mathsf{FO} is exactly the circuit complexity
class 𝖣𝖫𝖮𝖦𝖳𝖨𝖬𝖤​-uniform​𝖠𝖢0=𝖥𝖮​-uniform​𝖠𝖢0\mathsf{DLOGTIME}\textrm{-uniform}\ \mathsf{AC}^{0}=\
mathsf{FO}\textrm{-uniform}\ \mathsf{AC}^{0} (Mix Barrington et al., 1990). This class captures the
languages recognized by unique hard-attention transformers (Hao et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2024),
but it is not large enough to capture soft-attention transformers (Merrill and Sabharwal, 2023c).
To consider a more expressive logic capable of modeling soft-attention transformers, we can extend
𝖥𝖮\mathsf{FO} with majority quantifiers (Merrill and Sabharwal, 2023a). Specifically, we will
define 𝖥𝖮+𝖬2\mathsf{FO}+\mathsf{M}^{2} as 𝖥𝖮\mathsf{FO} extended to include a paired majority
quantifier:

Definition 8 (Mix Barrington et al., 1990).

𝖥𝖮+𝖬2\mathsf{FO}+\mathsf{M}^{2} is 𝖥𝖮\mathsf{FO} extended to include the 𝖬2\mathsf{M}^{2}
quantifier, defined as follows: 𝖬2​(i,j).P⁡(i,j)\mathsf{M}^{2}(i,j).P(i,j) is true if ϕ⁡(i,j)\phi
(i,j) holds for a majority of pairs of positions (i,j)∈[n]2(i,j)\in[n]^{2} in the string.

It is known that 𝖥𝖮+𝖬2\mathsf{FO}+\mathsf{M}^{2} defines exactly 𝖥𝖮​-uniform​𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{FO}\textrm
{-uniform}\ \mathsf{TC}^{0} (Mix Barrington et al., 1990, Theorem 10.2, which uses paired
majority). It is also possible to use majority quantifiers to define addition over indices, so
without loss of generality, we can assume 𝖥𝖮+𝖬2\mathsf{FO}+\mathsf{M}^{2} formulas have no addition
(unlike 𝖥𝖮\mathsf{FO} formulas). Moreover, it is possible to simulate 𝖻𝗂𝗍\mathsf{bit} in terms of
𝖬2\mathsf{M}^{2}, so we can consider 𝖥𝖮+𝖬2\mathsf{FO}+\mathsf{M}^{2} formulas not to contain 𝖻𝗂𝗍\

that also defines 𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{TC}^{0}. This makes 𝖥𝖮+𝖬2\mathsf{FO}+\mathsf{M}^{2} a simpler target
for our transformer constructions than 𝖥𝖮+𝖬⁡[𝖻𝗂𝗍]\mathsf{FO}+\mathsf{M}[\mathsf{bit}].

3 Masked and Unmasked Transformers

Before presenting our main results, we briefly discuss a simple but important relationship between
different kinds of masking that will come in handy. When using a transformer as a recognizer for
formal languages, there is a choice of whether to use an encoder (with no attention masking) or a
decoder (with causal attention masking) to encode the input string. Causally masked (decoder)
models are more standard these days, as well as theoretically more convenient (e.g., no need for
position embedding, as position information can be derived from causal attention), so we take them
as our default model. However, it is sometimes easier to reason about how to solve problems with
unmasked transformers. Fortunately, the following lemma shows we can simulate an unmasked
transformer (encoder) with a causally masked transformer (decoder), if we allow padding tokens
proportional to the transformer depth. This will be useful going forward in several places where we
convert unmasked and mixed-masked constructions to causally masked constructions.

Lemma 1 (Unmasked to Causally Masked).

Let EE be an unmasked (with position encoding 1/i1/i) AHAT encoder with depth ℓ≥1\ell\geq 1. Then
there exists a causally masked AHAT decoder DD (without any position encoding) with depth ℓ\ell and
with ℓ​n\ell n padding tokens on input length nn that is equivalent to EE in the following sense:
the final nn outputs of DD match the original nn outputs of EE.

Proof.

We first observe that the causally masked decoder DD can compute 1/i1/i (the position encoding used
in the unmasked encoder EE) by attending uniformly with value 11 only for the beginning-of-sequence
symbol. To simulate unmasked attention with causally masked attention via padding, the idea is for
DD to unroll a sequence of ℓ\ell “blocks” (one for each of the ℓ\ell layers of EE) along the
padding length dimension, each of width nn. Each block will attend to the previous block in the
previous layer and can thus see all tokens despite causal masking.

To implement the block construction, we first compute ϕ⁡(n)\phi(n). We then use this to compute ϕ⁡
(bi)\phi(b_{i}), where bi=⌊i/n⌋∈[1,ℓ]b_{i}=\lfloor i/n\rfloor\in[1,\ell], which represents the
block that each token belongs to. We also compute ϕ⁡(bi−1)∈[0,ℓ−1]\phi(b_{i}-1)\in[0,\ell-1] using
a head that ignores the beginning-of-sequence token $\$. Next, we modify each original head from UU
to have an additional term in the query/key product. The query is C​ϕ​(bi−1)C\phi(b_{i}-1) and the
key is ϕ⁡(bj)\phi(b_{j}), where CC is a large weight. We set CC to a fixed large value (independent
of nn) such that this term dominates all other terms in the inner product computation at each
token. As a result, the head is constrained over keys in the context where bj=bi−1b_{j}=b_{i}-1,
i.e., the keys from the last block. Within these keys, it computes exactly the same AHAT output as
the original unmasked head. In this way, we are able to simulate an unmasked (or mixed mask)
transformer with a causally masked transformer. ∎

Since causally masked heads of a mixed-masked transformer can be trivially simulated by a causally
masked transformer, Lemma 1 allows us to relate various masking variants (proof in Appendix C):

Proposition 1.

Unmasked (with position encoding 1/i1/i or i/ni/n; cf. Lemma 10 in §C) and mixed-masked padded
transformers (i.e., uAHATs and mAHATs) can be simulated by causally masked transformers (AHATs).
Specifically, for any d,k∈ℕd,k\in\mathbb{N}, the following holds for the corresponding problem
classes:

    𝗎𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳k0⊆𝗆𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳k0⊆𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳max⁡{k,1}0\mathsf{uAHAT}^{0}_{k}\subseteq\mathsf{mAHAT}^{0}_{k}\subseteq\
    mathsf{AHAT}^{0}_{\max\{k,1\}}

 2. 2.
   
    𝗎𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳kd⊆𝗆𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳kd⊆𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳1+max⁡{k,1}d\mathsf{uAHAT}^{d}_{k}\subseteq\mathsf{mAHAT}^{d}_{k}\subseteq\
    mathsf{AHAT}^{d}_{1+\max\{k,1\}} for d≥1d\geq 1

 3. 3.
   
    𝗎𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳∗d⊆𝗆𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳∗d⊆𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳∗d\mathsf{uAHAT}^{d}_{*}\subseteq\mathsf{mAHAT}^{d}_{*}\subseteq\mathsf

4 Fixed-Depth Padded Transformers Recognize Exactly 𝖥𝖮​-uniform​𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{FO}\textrm{-uniform}\ \

We are now ready to prove our first main result, namely that padding tokens allow transformers to

they can simulate formulas with the special paired majority quantifiers.

An 𝖥𝖮+𝖬2\mathsf{FO}+\mathsf{M}^{2} formula with k≥1k\geq 1 distinct variables and nesting depth ℓ\

Our construction for Lemma 2 somewhat resembles (though is distinct from) a result of Lange (2004,
Corollary 6.8) that any problem in 𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{TC}^{0} can be reduced to majority logic via a
transformation that appends some extra tokens to the input. However, their transformation is not
padding since it appends some “non-blank” tokens, and their result also does not clearly apply to
transformers.

Combined with previous results about transformers being in 𝖥𝖮​-uniform​𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{FO}\textrm
{-uniform}\ \mathsf{TC}^{0}, Lemma 2 yields an exact characterization of constant-depth
transformers with padding:

Theorem 1.

𝗎𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳∗0=𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳∗0=𝖥𝖮​-uniform​𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{uAHAT}^{0}_{*}=\mathsf{AHAT}^{0}_{*}=\mathsf{FO}\textrm
{-uniform}\ \mathsf{TC}^{0}, i.e., 𝖥𝖮⁡[𝖬,𝖻𝗂𝗍]\mathsf{FO}[\mathsf{M},\mathsf{bit}].

It is known that 𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳0⊆𝖥𝖮​-uniform​𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{AHAT}^{0}\subseteq\mathsf{FO}\textrm{-uniform}\ \
mathsf{TC}^{0} (Merrill and Sabharwal, 2023c; Merrill and Sabharwal, 2023a; Chiang, 2025), and this
generalizes to transformers with padding tokens (Pfau et al., 2024). We will show
𝖥𝖮​-uniform​𝖳𝖢0⊆𝗎𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳∗0\mathsf{FO}\textrm{-uniform}\ \mathsf{TC}^{0}\subseteq\mathsf{uAHAT}^{0}_
{*}. Mix Barrington et al. (1990, Proposition 10.3) proved that 𝖥𝖮​-uniform​𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{FO}\textrm

quantifiers: notably, the 𝖻𝗂𝗍\mathsf{bit} predicate is not necessary when using 𝖬2\mathsf{M}^{2}
quantifiers. Thus, Lemma 2 establishes that 𝖥𝖮​-uniform​𝖳𝖢0⊆𝗎𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳∗0\mathsf{FO}\textrm{-uniform}\ \
mathsf{TC}^{0}\subseteq\mathsf{uAHAT}^{0}_{*}. Finally, from Proposition 1, we have 𝗎𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳∗0⊆𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳∗0\
mathsf{uAHAT}^{0}_{*}\subseteq\mathsf{AHAT}^{0}_{*}. Hence, 𝗎𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳∗0=𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳∗0=𝖥𝖮​-uniform​𝖳𝖢0\mathsf
{uAHAT}^{0}_{*}=\mathsf{AHAT}^{0}_{*}=\mathsf{FO}\textrm{-uniform}\ \mathsf{TC}^{0}. ∎

A technical hurdle to obtaining this characterization in prior work was simulating the 𝖻𝗂𝗍\mathsf
{bit} predicate in standard definitions of 𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{TC}^{0} (Pfau et al., 2024). Our results
circumvent this by instead relating transformers to 𝖥𝖮+𝖬2\mathsf{FO}+\mathsf{M}^{2}, which is
equivalent to standard 𝖥𝖮⁡[𝖬,𝖻𝗂𝗍]\mathsf{FO}[\mathsf{M},\mathsf{bit}] (Mix Barrington et al., 1990
). It thus follows from Theorem 1 that padded transformers can also simulate 𝖻𝗂𝗍\mathsf{bit} as
well as all of 𝖥𝖮\mathsf{FO}, which will be useful in the following section for simulating 𝖥𝖮\
mathsf{FO} reductions.

Lastly, we note that the arguments leading to Theorem 1 work for both logarithmic and polynomial
precision (cf. Appendix A) transformers. This shows that these two levels of precision lead to
identical power for fixed-depth padded transformers.

5 Log^d-Looped Padded Transformers Recognize Exactly 𝖥𝖮​-uniform​𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{FO}\textrm{-uniform}\ \
mathsf{TC}^{d}

The notion of completeness of a problem (or language) for a complexity class under certain types of
reduction has played a key role in computational complexity. Just like it has been useful for
reasoning about the expressivity of resource-bounded standard models of computation (Turing
machines, circuit models, etc.), we show it can also be used to reason about the expressivity of
padded transformers.

We begin by formally defining the notion of reductions in terms of predicates or languages (rather
than string-to-string functions). This will make it easier to precisely implement reductions inside
transformers, which produce contextual representations of prefixes of the input string in parallel,
in contrast to the more standard definition of reductions as string-to-string functions.

Definition 9.

Let b⁡(i)b(i) be the binary encoding of i∈ℕi\in\mathbb{N} in some alphabet Σ⊇{0,1}\Sigma\supseteq\
{0,1\}. Let 𝖱\mathsf{R} be a class of languages. We say a transduction f:Σ∗→Σ∗f:\Sigma^{*}\to\Sigma
^{*} is an 𝖱\mathsf{R} reduction if |f⁡(w)|\lvert f(w)\rvert is polynomial in |w|\lvert w\rvert and
the language Rf={(w,b⁡(i),σ)∣f​(w)i=σ}R_{f}=\{(w,b(i),\sigma)\mid f(w)_{i}=\sigma\} is in 𝖱\mathsf
{R}.^2^2 2 Here f​(w)if(w)_{i} denotes the ii-th token of f⁡(w)f(w) if i≤|f⁡(w)|i\leq\lvert f(w)\
rvert, and a special symbol □∉Σ\square\not\in\Sigma otherwise.

Definition 9 recovers the standard notions of 𝖥𝖮\mathsf{FO} and 𝖫\mathsf{L} reductions. A
transformer can be said to compute a reduction ff if it recognizes the language of triples (w,i,σ)
(w,i,\sigma) defined above. Since |Σ|\lvert\Sigma\rvert is finite and transformers can equally
easily output a ‘token’ in Σ\Sigma instead of just 0/1, it is in fact more natural to require the
transformer to compute a functional form of this language, namely compute rf​(w,i)r_{f}(w,i)
defined as f​(w)if(w)_{i}. Our constructions work under both views, though the latter is often more
natural and efficient. Formally:

Definition 10.

We say that a transformer computes an 𝖱\mathsf{R} reduction ff if it either recognizes the language
Rf={(w,i,σ)∣f​(w)i=σ}R_{f}=\{(w,i,\sigma)\mid f(w)_{i}=\sigma\} in 𝖱\mathsf{R} or computes the

where, in either case, ii is encoded in binary.

Lemma 3.

Let 𝖢,𝖱\mathsf{C},\mathsf{R} be classes of languages. Let language LL be 𝖢\mathsf{C}-complete under
𝖱\mathsf{R} reductions. If 𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳∗d\mathsf{AHAT}^{d}_{*} transformers can recognize LL and compute

^{\prime} if and only if f⁡(w)∈Lf(w)\in L. From the last precondition of the theorem, there exists
a causally masked log^d-depth padded transformer TfT_{f} that recognizes the corresponding
reduction language RfR_{f} or, equivalently, computes the reduction function rfr_{f} (cf. 
Definition 10). We will assume the latter, i.e., that TfT_{f} computes rfr_{f}, though the
construction can also be made to work if TfT_{f} checks membership in RfR_{f}. Additionally, we
also have from a precondition that there exists a causally masked log^d-depth padded transformer

The idea is to “stack” TLT_{L} on top of TfT_{f} to obtain a log^d-depth padded transformer TT that
recognizes L′L^{\prime}. Intuitively, given an input ww, the first set of layers of TT will compute
f⁡(w)f(w) tokenwise, by computing r⁡(w,i)=f​(w)ir(w,i)=f(w)_{i} for every ii in parallel. To this
end, we will essentially make |f⁡(w)|\lvert f(w)\rvert copies of the padding tokens needed by TfT_
{f} and perform the computation of TfT_{f} independently for each f​(w)if(w)_{i}. The second and
final set of layers of TT will then check whether the string f⁡(w)f(w) produced by the first set of
layers is in LL, which will hold if and only if w∈L′w\in L^{\prime}. See full proof in Appendix C.
∎

Combined with results in prior work, it follows from Lemma 3 (see proof below) that padded
log-depth transformers can recognize any language in 𝖭𝖫\mathsf{NL}:

Lemma 4.

𝖭𝖫⊆𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳∗1\mathsf{NL}\subseteq\mathsf{AHAT}^{1}_{*}.

Proof.

Let LL be the graph connectivity problem, class 𝖢\mathsf{C} be 𝖭𝖫\mathsf{NL}, and class 𝖱\mathsf{R}
be 𝖥𝖮\mathsf{FO}. We will show that the preconditions of Lemma 3 are met, from which it will follow
that 𝖭𝖫⊆𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳∗1\mathsf{NL}\subseteq\mathsf{AHAT}^{1}_{*}. First, graph connectivity is known to be
𝖭𝖫\mathsf{NL}-complete under 𝖥𝖮\mathsf{FO} reductions (Immerman, 1998). Second, Merrill and
Sabharwal (2025) recently showed that mixed-masked log-depth transformers with cubic padding can
recognize the graph connectivity problem LL, i.e., L∈𝗆𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳∗1L\in\mathsf{mAHAT}^{1}_{*}. From
Proposition 1, it follows that L∈𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳∗1L\in\mathsf{AHAT}^{1}_{*}. Finally, it follows from Theorem 
1 that fixed-depth causally masked transformers can recognize languages in 𝖥𝖮⁡[𝖬,𝖻𝗂𝗍]\mathsf{FO}[\
mathsf{M},\mathsf{bit}], and hence also languages in 𝖥𝖮\mathsf{FO}. Such transformers can therefore
compute 𝖥𝖮\mathsf{FO} reductions ff in the sense of Definition 10, i.e., compute the function rf​
(w,i)r_{f}(w,i) defined as f​(w)if(w)_{i}, the ii-th bit of f⁡(w)f(w). Thus, Lemma 3 applies. ∎

following stronger result. For this, we will leverage the notion of reductions and the completeness

We will apply Lemma 3 with the wide-𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} circuit evaluation problem (Appendix D) as

{L} as class 𝖱\mathsf{R}. We next argue that the preconditions of Lemma 3 are met, which will

6 Uniformity Collapse for Circuit Classes

Our results on looped transformers involved substantial analysis of uniform polylogarithmic-depth
circuit classes. This analysis led us to prove a novel result about uniform circuit families, which
both slightly strengthens our results about looped transfomers and may be of independent interest.

The result concerns the strength of different uniformity conditions for circuit families,
characterizing conditions under which variants of circuit classes with different uniformity
conditions collapse to express the same class of languages (Proposition 6 in Appendix E). Applying
this general result to (wide) 𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} circuits, we show that for d≥1d\geq 1, both
𝖭𝖫​-uniformity\mathsf{NL}\textrm{-uniformity} and 𝖫​-uniformity\mathsf{L}\textrm{-uniformity}
collapse to the weaker notion of 𝖥𝖮​-uniformity\mathsf{FO}\textrm{-uniformity} for both 𝖠𝖢d\mathsf
{AC}^{d} and 𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} circuits:

For any d≥1d\geq 1, the following equivalences hold:

The key idea, formalized in Appendix E, is to leverage the fact that 𝖭𝖫\mathsf{NL} and 𝖫\mathsf{L}

7 Conclusion

Our results in this work give a precise theoretical understanding of how padding and looping—two
ways to dynamically expand the computational resources of a transformer at inference time—increase
the expressive power of transformers. Padding expands the circuit width of transformers, allowing
them to resolve logical formulas over more variables. As a consequence of this, polynomially padded
transformers can recognize exactly 𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{TC}^{0}, which was previously known only as an upper
bound (Merrill and Sabharwal, 2023c; Pfau et al., 2024). In contrast, looping increases the depth
of transformers. Applying looping on top of padding, we extended our result to show that log^d
-depth, padded transformers recognize exactly 𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d}. This means that transformers with
polynomial padding and polylogarithmic looping converge to recognizing 𝖭𝖢\mathsf{NC}, the largest
class of problems that can be solved with parallel computation. In contrast, transformers with CoT
have greater expressive power under standard complexity conjectures (Merrill and Sabharwal, 2023b),
but suffer from slow sequential decoding. Thus, while looping and padding are not as powerful as
CoT, our results suggest they are quite effective ways to expand transformers’ expressive power
while preserving parallelism.

Several interesting open questions remain from this work. On the theoretical side, it would be
valuable to develop a more finegrained characterization of 𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳kd\mathsf{AHAT}^{d}_{k}, i.e.,
looped transformers where the padding is at most O⁡(nk)\mathrm{O}(n^{k}) for some fixed kk. On the
empirical side, while looped and padded transformers have both been explored already to an extent,
it would be interesting to see whether these approaches could be successfully integrated to improve
the performance of transformers on hard reasoning tasks. Further developing these approaches could
ultimately lead to inference-time compute methods based on padding and looping that increase
transformers’ expressive power without sacrificing parallelism, providing a more efficient
alternative to CoT for solving moderately parallelizable problems.

Limitations

Practical Concerns for Looped Transformers with Padding. In this paper, we showed looped
transformers with padding are quite expressive. However, the degree to which transformers can learn
to use looped layers is an important open question. In particular, practical details might be
important here, such as an appropriate parameterization where features can be learned in later
layers (Dey et al., 2025). Another practical caveat is that, while padding is efficiently
parallelizable, it will extend the context length, incurring more memory overhead for the forward
pass.

Transformer Model. Here we have assumed AHATs here rather than softmax-attention transformers
(SMATs). For any fixed maximum context length, it is possible scale the temperature of SMAT heads
to arbitrarily approximate AHAT heads, but for unbounded context lengths, SMATs may not be able to
simulate AHATs. We thus view the AHAT as mild simplification of the SMAT that abstracts away issues
with soft attention for simulating hard attention over very long contexts. It would also be
interesting to better understand the necessity of the masked pre-norm assumption.

Appendix A Datatype Assumptions

We adapt the pp-precise datatype model from Merrill and Sabharwal [2025]. We encode scalars as
strings in {0,1}p\{0,1\}^{p}, where pp can be a function of nn. If pp depends on nn, activations,
but not models parameters, can depend on nn. A datatype 𝔻p\mathbb{D}_{p} assigns a numerical
semantics for each string in {0,1}p\{0,1\}^{p}. For x∈ℝx\in\mathbb{R}, let [x]𝔻p[x]_{\mathbb{D}_
{p}} be xx rounded into 𝔻p\mathbb{D}_{p}, i.e., the bitstring whose numerical value in 𝔻p\mathbb{D}
_{p} is closest to xx (breaking ties in favor of the higher value). We define our datatype 𝔻p\
mathbb{D}_{p} to satisfy the following:

Definition 11 (pp-Precise Operations).

Let f:ℝk→ℝf:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R} be an operation with pp-precision realization f~:𝔻pk→𝔻p\
tilde{f}:\mathbb{D}_{p}^{k}\to\mathbb{D}_{p}. We say f~\tilde{f} is pp-precise if, for any
x1,…,xk∈ℝx_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in\mathbb{R} exactly representable in 𝔻p\mathbb{D}_{p},

 [f⁡(x1,…,xk)]𝔻p=f~​([x1]𝔻p,…,[xk]𝔻p).[f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})]_{\mathbb{D}_{p}}=\tilde{f}([x_{1}]_
 {\mathbb{D}_{p}},\ldots,[x_{k}]_{\mathbb{D}_{p}}).

To apply Definition 11, we view the summation in attention heads as an nn-ary operation. We also
view layer-norm as a single operation from ℝm→ℝm\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}. Lastly, we assume
that these operations in the computation graph are defined in the standard way and are thus
computable in 𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{TC}^{0} [Merrill and Sabharwal, 2023c, Merrill and Sabharwal, 2023a].

We consider two natural instantiations of 𝔻p\mathbb{D}_{p} in the main text: log precision, with p=
c​log⁡np=c\log n [Merrill and Sabharwal, 2023c], and polynomial precision, with p=ncp=n^{c} [
Chiang, 2025], for some fixed c>0c>0. All our results go through with either datatype, showing
their equivalence for polylogarithmically-looped polynomially-padded transformers.

B.1 Uniformity Composition Closures

Our later results about uniform circuit families will rely on the property (formalized below) that
for strong-enough classes 𝖠\mathsf{A}, any fixed composition of 𝖠​-uniform\mathsf{A}\textrm
{-uniform} circuit families is also 𝖠​-uniform\mathsf{A}\textrm{-uniform}. Note that any fixed
composition of functions can be decomposed into a fixed number of serial and parallel compositions
of two functions, defined as follows:

Definition 14 (Function Composition).

For functions f,g∈{0,1}∗→{0,1}∗f,g\in\{0,1\}^{*}\to\{0,1\}^{*}, their serial composition is the
function (g∘f)​(w)=g⁡(f⁡(w))(g\circ f)(w)=g(f(w)) and their parallel composition is the function
⟨f,g⟩​(w)=f⁡(w)⋅g⁡(w)\langle f,g\rangle(w)=f(w)\cdot g(w).

For 𝖫​-uniform\mathsf{L}\textrm{-uniform} functions, serial and parallel composition clearly
preserves 𝖫​-uniformity\mathsf{L}\textrm{-uniformity}, as we can maintain a counter to reroute
inputs and outputs between subcircuits appropriately. The same applies to any class 𝖠\mathsf{A}
containing 𝖫\mathsf{L}. For 𝖥𝖮\mathsf{FO} uniform circuit classes, closure under composition
requires a bit more work to justify, as shown in the following two lemmas.

Lemma 7.

    circuit family {Cn}n=0∞∈𝖢\{C_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}\in\mathsf{C}.

  • •
   
    Output: The value C⁡(x)C(x).

For example, the case where 𝖢=𝖯/𝗉𝗈𝗅𝗒\mathsf{C}=\mathsf{P/poly} yields the generic circuit value
problem, which is known to be 𝖯\mathsf{P}-complete. We focus here to the case of 𝖢=𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{C}=\
mathsf{TC}^{d}, i.e., the 𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} circuit evaluation problem. It is somewhat intuitive
that this problem is hard for the class 𝖠​-uniform​𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{A}\textrm{-uniform}\ \mathsf{TC}^{d}
as long as 𝖠\mathsf{A} is strong enough to build circuits; we formalize this later in Lemma 11.
However, the 𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} circuit evaluation problem is not necessarily in the class 𝖳𝖢d\
mathsf{TC}^{d}. To see this, suppose to the contrary that there exists an 𝖠​-uniform​𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{A}\
textrm{-uniform}\ \mathsf{TC}^{d} circuit family 𝒞={Cn}n=0∞\mathcal{C}=\{C_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
that solves the 𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} circuit evaluation problem. Then, for large enough nn, each
circuit Cn∈𝒞C_{n}\in\mathcal{C} has depth upper bounded by c​logd​nc\log^{d}n for a fixed c>0c>0.
This cc being fixed is problematic—if one were to try to evaluate on string xx a circuit Cn′C^{\
prime}_{n} from a 𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} circuit family that has depth c′​logd​nc^{\prime}\log^{d}n
where c′>cc^{\prime}>c (that is, invoke circuit evaluation for input (x,⟨Cn′⟩)(x,\langle C^{\prime}
_{n}\rangle)), the intuitive approach would require a circuit whose depth is larger than that of
CnC_{n}.

To address this, we now formalize a constrained version of the 𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} circuit
evaluation problem that is, in fact, within the class 𝖥𝖮​-uniform​𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{FO}\textrm{-uniform}\
\mathsf{TC}^{d}. We achieve this via the wide-𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} circuit evaluation problem, where
the class wide-𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} is defined as follows:

Definition 16 (Wide Circuits).

Let wide-𝖳𝖢d⊆𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d}\subseteq\mathsf{TC}^{d} be the class of circuit families {Cn}n=0∞\
{C_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty} such that there exists some cc such that, for large nn, the depth of CnC_
{n} is at most c​logd​nc\log^{d}n and, crucially, the size is at least ncn^{c}.

That is, wide-𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} enforces that the size (and hence the width) of the circuit is
large relative to its depth. In particular, for every wide-𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} circuit family 𝒞\
mathcal{C}, there is a c>0c>0 such that the circuit CnC_{n} has serialization of size Ω⁡(nc)\Omega
(n^{c}), and depth at most c​logd​nc\log^{d}n. Thus the depth of CnC_{n} is at most logd⁡N\log^{d}N
where N=n+Ω⁡(nc)N=n+\Omega(n^{c}) is the overall size of the input (x,⟨Cn⟩)(x,\langle C_{n}\rangle)
to the corresponding circuit evaluation problem. As we will show later, this allows the wide-𝖳𝖢d\
mathsf{TC}^{d} circuit evaluation problem to be solved by a transformer (Corollary 13.1) as well as
by an 𝖥𝖮​-uniform​𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{FO}\textrm{-uniform}\ \mathsf{TC}^{d} circuit family (Lemma 14) using
precisely logd⁡N\log^{d}N iterations (manifested as loops of a transformer or 𝖥𝖮\mathsf{FO}-uniform
𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{TC}^{0} block, respectively), irrespective of the 𝒞\mathcal{C}-dependent value of cc.

Since it is a class of circuit families, wide-𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} can be constrained by uniformity
conditions in the natural way. With some abuse of notation, we will use wide-𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} to
refer to both the class of circuit families and the complexity class of language classes the
circuit families recognize. Interestingly, this minimum size constraint imposed by wide-𝖳𝖢d\mathsf
{TC}^{d} does not weaken it as a language class compared to 𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d}:

Proposition 4.

For any d≥0d\geq 0, non-uniform wide-𝖳𝖢d=non-uniform​𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d}=\textrm{non-uniform}\ \
mathsf{TC}^{d}.

Proof.

Every wide-𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} circuit family is, by definition, also a 𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} circuit
family.

Conversely, suppose L∈𝖳𝖢dL\in\mathsf{TC}^{d}. Then there is a circuit family {Cn}n=0∞\{C_{n}\}_{n=
0}^{\infty} recognizing LL, where the depth of CnC_{n} is at most c​logd​nc\log^{d}n for some cc
and large enough nn. Consider a modified circuit family {Cn′}n=0∞\{C^{\prime}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
where, for each nn, Cn′C^{\prime}_{n} is a copy of CnC_{n} that, if needed, is padded with dummy
gates so that it has size at least ncn^{c}. This modified circuit family also recognizes LL but
belongs to wide-𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d}, completing the proof. ∎

In fact, the above equality holds even for uniform variants of these classes:

Proposition 5.

For any d≥0d\geq 0 and 𝖠⊇𝖥𝖮\mathsf{A}\supseteq\mathsf{FO}, 𝖠​-uniform\mathsf{A}\textrm{-uniform}
wide-𝖳𝖢d=𝖠​-uniform​𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d}=\mathsf{A}\textrm{-uniform}\ \mathsf{TC}^{d}.

Proof.

The proof follows that of Proposition 4. Every 𝖠​-uniform\mathsf{A}\textrm{-uniform} wide-𝖳𝖢d\
mathsf{TC}^{d} circuit family is, by definition, also a 𝖠​-uniform\mathsf{A}\textrm{-uniform} 𝖳𝖢d\
mathsf{TC}^{d} circuit family. Conversely, suppose L∈𝖠L\in\mathsf{A}-uniform wide-𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^
{d}. Then, we have a a circuit family 𝒞={Cn}n=0∞\mathcal{C}=\{C_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty} recognizing
LL, where the depth of each CnC_{n} is at most c​logd​nc\log^{d}n for some cc and large enough nn.
By 𝖠\mathsf{A} uniformity, we have the connection language L𝒞∈𝖠L_{\mathcal{C}}\in\mathsf{A}. We
define a circuit family 𝒞′\mathcal{C}^{\prime} that is 𝒞\mathcal{C} padded with dummy gates to make
the number of gates at least ncn^{c}. The connection language L𝒞′L_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}} defaults
to L𝒞L_{\mathcal{C}} for gates that exist in 𝒞\mathcal{C} and simply returns a dummy gate for other
gate indices up to ncn^{c}. This additional logic can be implemented in 𝖥𝖮\mathsf{FO}, so
L𝒞′∈𝖠⊇𝖥𝖮L_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}\in\mathsf{A}\supseteq\mathsf{FO}. Thus, the circuit family 𝒞′\
mathcal{C}^{\prime} recognizes LL and is 𝖠\mathsf{A}-uniform. ∎

D.1 Hardness of Wide-𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} Circuit Evaluation

Lemma 11.

For d≥0d\geq 0, 𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} circuit evaluation is hard for 𝖫​-uniform\mathsf{L}\textrm
{-uniform} 𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} under 𝖫\mathsf{L}-reductions.

Proof.

Given any L∈𝖫​-uniform​𝖳𝖢dL\in\mathsf{L}\textrm{-uniform}\ \mathsf{TC}^{d}, there exists a
log-space Turing machine TLT_{L} that constructs a circuit family {Cn}n=0∞\{C_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
that recognizes LL. We can construct an 𝖫\mathsf{L}-reduction from LL to the 𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d}
circuit evaluation problem as follows. Given an input xx whose membership in LL we would like to
check, the reduction first copies xx to the output. It then uses the log-space Turing machine TLT_
{L} to build the circuit C|x|C_{\lvert x\rvert} and output it in the above serialized format. We
thus have a log-space reduction from xx to (x,⟨C|x|⟩)(x,\langle C_{\lvert x\rvert}\rangle). We
conclude that 𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} circuit evaluation is hard for 𝖫​-uniform\mathsf{L}\textrm
{-uniform} 𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} under 𝖫\mathsf{L}-reductions. ∎

Lemma 12.

For d≥0d\geq 0, wide-𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} circuit evaluation is hard for 𝖫​-uniform\mathsf{L}\textrm
{-uniform} 𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} under 𝖫\mathsf{L}-reductions.

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma 11, we are given LL such that there exists a log-space Turing machine TLT_
{L} that constructs {Cn}n=0∞\{C_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty} recognizing LL, where the depth of each CnC_
{n} is at most c​logd​nc\log^{d}n for some cc and large enough nn. We can create a modified
log-space Turing machine TL′T^{\prime}_{L} that builds {Cn′}n=0∞\{C^{\prime}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
that still recognizes LL in depth c​logd​nc\log^{d}n but is padded, if needed, with dummy gates so
that it has size at least ncn^{c}: we do this by keeping a counter for the number of gates and
wires produced and outputting dummy ones until ncn^{c} is exceeded. We then follow the rest of the
proof of Lemma 11 with TL′T^{\prime}_{L} instead of TLT_{L}. ∎

D.2 Solving Wide-𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} Circuit Evaluation with Transformers

Lemma 13.

There is a mixed-masked looped transformer TT that, on input (x,⟨C⟩)(x,\langle C\rangle) where x∈
{0,1}∗x\in\{0,1\}^{*} and ⟨C⟩\langle C\rangle is the serialization of a depth ℓ\ell circuit with |x
|\lvert x\rvert inputs, computes C⁡(x)C(x) when unrolled ℓ\ell times.

Proof.

We adapt the proof of Merrill and Sabharwal [2023c, Theorem 3], which sketches how log-depth
(unlooped) transformers can implement the 𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{TC}^{0} circuit evaluation problem. We
construct a looped transformer that will “attempt” to evaluate every gate: if its arguments have
already been computed, the gate will return {0,1}\{0,1\}, and, if not, it will return undefined (⊥\
bot).

Let ii be a token index. We say the token wiw_{i} is a gate token if it is X,AND,OR,NOT,MAJ\texttt
{X},\texttt{AND},\texttt{OR},\texttt{NOT},\texttt{MAJ}. We will use gate token ii to store the
value vi∈{0,1,⊥}v_{i}\in\{0,1,\bot\} for the gate it represents as a one-hot encoded vector. In the
base case (embedding layer), we initialize vi=⊥v_{i}=\bot for every gate token. Using a looped
block of 2 layers, we will proceed in a way that propagates the computation of viv_{i} at later
layers in terms of previously computed values.

X Gates. In the setup layers at an X token ii, we use a causally masked uniform attention head with
value 𝟙[wj=X]\mathbbm{1}[w_{j}=\texttt{X}]. Thus, this head computes ri/ir_{i}/i, where rir_{i}
number of X gates before and including ii. We compute ϕ⁡(ri/i,1/i)=ϕ⁡(ri)\phi(r_{i}/i,1/i)=\phi(r_
{i}) and store it in the residual stream.

In the looped layers, we define an attention head with query ϕ⁡(ri)\phi(r_{i}), key ϕ⁡(j)\phi(j),
and value wjw_{j}. This head thus retrieves input token wrjw_{r_{j}}. We update the gate value to
vi←wrjv_{i}\leftarrow w_{r_{j}} (viewing both as vectors in the same space).

Other Gates. In the setup layers, each argument token ii attends with causally masked uniform
attention with value 𝟙[wj=&]\mathbbm{1}[w_{j}=\&] to compute aia_{i}, the number of arguments to
its left (including it). Each & token attends with query ϕ⁡(ai)\phi(a_{i}), key ϕ⁡(aj)\phi(a_{j}),
and value 𝟙[wj=&]\mathbbm{1}[w_{j}=\&], which returns 1/(1+zi)1/(1+z_{i}), where ziz_{i} is the
number of 1’s following & token ii. We compute and store ϕ⁡(zi+1)\phi(z_{i}+1) in the residual
stream. We compute gig_{i} similarly to aia_{i}, the number of gate tokens to left of token ii
(also inclusive).

In the first looped layer, each & token ii attends with query ϕ⁡(zi+1)\phi(z_{i}+1), key ϕ⁡(gj)\phi
(g_{j}), and value vjv_{j}. Thus, the argument token ii retrieves vzi+1v_{z_{i}+1}, the value at
gate zi+1z_{i}+1. In the second looped layer, gate token ii attends with query ϕ⁡(gi)\phi(g_{i}),
key ϕ⁡(gj)\phi(g_{j}), and value vzj+1v_{z_{j}+1}. This, it returns the vector 1|Ai|​∑j∈Aivj\frac
{1}{\lvert A_{i}\rvert}\sum_{j\in A_{i}}v_{j}, where AiA_{i} is the set of gates that are arguments
of gate ii. From this vector, we can apply projections to recover TT, the fraction of j∈Ajj\in A_
{j} with vj=1v_{j}=1, as well as UU, the fraction of j∈Aij\in A_{i} with vj=⊥v_{j}=\bot. If U>0U>0,
we set vi←⊥v_{i}\leftarrow\bot. Otherwise, we set viv_{i} by thresholding TT against some threshold
kk based on the gate type (T≥1T\geq 1 for AND, T>0T>0 for OR, and T≥1/2T\geq 1/2 for MAJ). In
effect, this sets vi←G⁡({vj}j∈Aj)v_{i}\leftarrow G(\{v_{j}\}_{j\in A_{j}}), where GG is the gate
type.

Thus, the looped layers either keep viv_{i} as ⊥\bot or correctly update it to its true value.
Furthermore, the number of looping steps until viv_{i} is updated is exactly the depth of node ii.
Thus, a circuit CC of depth ℓ\ell can be fully evaluated by looping the depth-2 block ℓ\ell times.
∎

Corollary 13.1.

For d≥0d\geq 0, wide-𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d} circuit evaluation is in 𝗆𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳0d\mathsf{mAHAT}^{d}_{0}, and
hence in 𝖠𝖧𝖠𝖳1d\mathsf{AHAT}^{d}_{1}.

Proof.

We are given input (x,⟨Cn⟩)(x,\langle C_{n}\rangle), where CnC_{n} comes from some wide-𝖳𝖢d\mathsf
{TC}^{d} circuit family {Cn}n=0∞\{C_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty} with depth at most c​logd​nc\log^{d}n and

evaluation problem for large enough nn (w.l.o.g. we can solve small-nn examples via table lookup).

We next justify that a mixed-masked mAHAT^d transformer will unroll at least c​logd​nc\log^{d}n
times for large enough nn. This transformer will unroll exactly logd⁡N\log^{d}N times, where N=n+|

instance. Since the size of CnC_{n} is at least ncn^{c}, we have that N≥ncN\geq n^{c}. Thus, our

 logd⁡N≥logd⁡nc=cd​logd​n≥c​logd​n.\displaystyle\log^{d}N\geq\log^{d}n^{c}=c^{d}\log^{d}n\geq c\

Thus, our transformer will unroll a sufficient number of times to solve the wide-𝖳𝖢d\mathsf{TC}^{d}

Finally, we conclude via Proposition 1 that this mixed-masked transformer can be converted to a

{d}_{1}. ∎

Appendix E Uniformity Collapse for Circuit Classes

In general, for classes 𝖠,𝖡\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B} such that 𝖠⊆𝖡\mathsf{A}\subseteq\mathsf{B},
𝖡​-uniformity\mathsf{B}\textrm{-uniformity} often leads to larger classes of languages than
𝖠​-uniformity\mathsf{A}\textrm{-uniformity}, as we have more resources to construct a circuit.
However, as the following lemma shows, this is not always the case:

NeurIPS Paper Checklist

 1. 1.
   
    Claims

 2. Question: Do the main claims made in the abstract and introduction accurately reflect the
    paper’s contributions and scope?

 3. Answer: [Yes]

 4. Justification: Main two results proved in Theorem 1 and Theorem 2.

 5. Guidelines:

      □ •
       
        The answer NA means that the abstract and introduction do not include the claims made in
        the paper.

      □ •
       
        The abstract and/or introduction should clearly state the claims made, including the
        contributions made in the paper and important assumptions and limitations. A No or NA
        answer to this question will not be perceived well by the reviewers.

      □ •
       
        The claims made should match theoretical and experimental results, and reflect how much the
        results can be expected to generalize to other settings.

      □ •
       
        It is fine to include aspirational goals as motivation as long as it is clear that these
        goals are not attained by the paper.

 6. 2.
   
    Limitations

 7. Question: Does the paper discuss the limitations of the work performed by the authors?

 8. Answer: [Yes]

 9. Justification: Lack of empirical support is acknowledged and simplifications made to
    transformer architecture acknowledged in Section 2.

10. Guidelines:

      □ •
       
        The answer NA means that the paper has no limitation while the answer No means that the
        paper has limitations, but those are not discussed in the paper.

      □ •
       
        The authors are encouraged to create a separate "Limitations" section in their paper.

      □ •
       
        The paper should point out any strong assumptions and how robust the results are to
        violations of these assumptions (e.g., independence assumptions, noiseless settings, model
        well-specification, asymptotic approximations only holding locally). The authors should
        reflect on how these assumptions might be violated in practice and what the implications
        would be.

      □ •
       
        The authors should reflect on the scope of the claims made, e.g., if the approach was only
        tested on a few datasets or with a few runs. In general, empirical results often depend on
        implicit assumptions, which should be articulated.

      □ •
       
        The authors should reflect on the factors that influence the performance of the approach.
        For example, a facial recognition algorithm may perform poorly when image resolution is low
        or images are taken in low lighting. Or a speech-to-text system might not be used reliably
        to provide closed captions for online lectures because it fails to handle technical jargon.

      □ •
       
        The authors should discuss the computational efficiency of the proposed algorithms and how
        they scale with dataset size.

      □ •
       
        If applicable, the authors should discuss possible limitations of their approach to address
        problems of privacy and fairness.

      □ •
       
        While the authors might fear that complete honesty about limitations might be used by
        reviewers as grounds for rejection, a worse outcome might be that reviewers discover
        limitations that aren’t acknowledged in the paper. The authors should use their best
        judgment and recognize that individual actions in favor of transparency play an important
        role in developing norms that preserve the integrity of the community. Reviewers will be
        specifically instructed to not penalize honesty concerning limitations.

11. 3.
   
    Theory Assumptions and Proofs

12. Question: For each theoretical result, does the paper provide the full set of assumptions and a
    complete (and correct) proof?

13. Answer: [Yes]

14. Justification: All theorems are numbered, cross-referenced, and given full proofs. Relevant
    definitions are given in Section 2.

15. Guidelines:

      □ •
       
        The answer NA means that the paper does not include theoretical results.

      □ •
       
        All the theorems, formulas, and proofs in the paper should be numbered and
        cross-referenced.

      □ •
       
        All assumptions should be clearly stated or referenced in the statement of any theorems.

      □ •
       
        The proofs can either appear in the main paper or the supplemental material, but if they
        appear in the supplemental material, the authors are encouraged to provide a short proof
        sketch to provide intuition.

      □ •
       
        Inversely, any informal proof provided in the core of the paper should be complemented by
        formal proofs provided in appendix or supplemental material.

      □ •
       
        Theorems and Lemmas that the proof relies upon should be properly referenced.

16. 4.
   
    Experimental Result Reproducibility

17. Question: Does the paper fully disclose all the information needed to reproduce the main
    experimental results of the paper to the extent that it affects the main claims and/or
    conclusions of the paper (regardless of whether the code and data are provided or not)?

18. Answer: [N/A]

19. Justification: No experimental results.

20. Guidelines:

      □ •
       
        The answer NA means that the paper does not include experiments.

      □ •
       
        If the paper includes experiments, a No answer to this question will not be perceived well
        by the reviewers: Making the paper reproducible is important, regardless of whether the
        code and data are provided or not.

      □ •
       
        If the contribution is a dataset and/or model, the authors should describe the steps taken
        to make their results reproducible or verifiable.

      □ •
       
        Depending on the contribution, reproducibility can be accomplished in various ways. For
        example, if the contribution is a novel architecture, describing the architecture fully
        might suffice, or if the contribution is a specific model and empirical evaluation, it may
        be necessary to either make it possible for others to replicate the model with the same
        dataset, or provide access to the model. In general. releasing code and data is often one
        good way to accomplish this, but reproducibility can also be provided via detailed
        instructions for how to replicate the results, access to a hosted model (e.g., in the case
        of a large language model), releasing of a model checkpoint, or other means that are
        appropriate to the research performed.

      □ •
       
        While NeurIPS does not require releasing code, the conference does require all submissions
        to provide some reasonable avenue for reproducibility, which may depend on the nature of
        the contribution. For example

         1. (a)
           
            If the contribution is primarily a new algorithm, the paper should make it clear how to
            reproduce that algorithm.

         2. (b)
           
            If the contribution is primarily a new model architecture, the paper should describe
            the architecture clearly and fully.

         3. (c)
           
            If the contribution is a new model (e.g., a large language model), then there should
            either be a way to access this model for reproducing the results or a way to reproduce
            the model (e.g., with an open-source dataset or instructions for how to construct the
            dataset).

         4. (d)
           
            We recognize that reproducibility may be tricky in some cases, in which case authors
            are welcome to describe the particular way they provide for reproducibility. In the
            case of closed-source models, it may be that access to the model is limited in some way
            (e.g., to registered users), but it should be possible for other researchers to have
            some path to reproducing or verifying the results.

    Experiment Statistical Significance

32. Question: Does the paper report error bars suitably and correctly defined or other appropriate
    information about the statistical significance of the experiments?

33. Answer: [N/A]

34. Justification: No experiments.

35. Guidelines:

      □ •
       
        The answer NA means that the paper does not include experiments.

      □ •
       
        The authors should answer "Yes" if the results are accompanied by error bars, confidence
        intervals, or statistical significance tests, at least for the experiments that support the
        main claims of the paper.

      □ •
       
        The factors of variability that the error bars are capturing should be clearly stated (for
        example, train/test split, initialization, random drawing of some parameter, or overall run
        with given experimental conditions).

      □ •
       
        The method for calculating the error bars should be explained (closed form formula, call to
        a library function, bootstrap, etc.)

      □ •
       
        The assumptions made should be given (e.g., Normally distributed errors).

      □ •
       
        It should be clear whether the error bar is the standard deviation or the standard error of

      □ •

        preferably report a 2-sigma error bar than state that they have a 96% CI, if the hypothesis
        of Normality of errors is not verified.

      □ •
       
        For asymmetric distributions, the authors should be careful not to show in tables or
        figures symmetric error bars that would yield results that are out of range (e.g. negative
        error rates).

      □ •
       
        If error bars are reported in tables or plots, The authors should explain in the text how
        they were calculated and reference the corresponding figures or tables in the text.
