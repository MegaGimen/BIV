PAC Reinforcement Learning for Predictive State Representations

Abstract

In this paper we study online Reinforcement Learning (RL) in partially observable dynamical
systems. We focus on the Predictive State Representations (PSRs) model, which is an expressive
model that captures other well-known models such as Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes
(POMDP). PSR represents the states using a set of predictions of future observations and is defined
entirely using observable quantities. We develop a novel model-based algorithm for PSRs that can
learn a near optimal policy in sample complexity scaling polynomially with respect to all the
relevant parameters of the systems. Our algorithm naturally works with function approximation to
extend to systems with potentially large state and observation spaces. We show that given a
realizable model class, the sample complexity of learning the near optimal policy only scales
polynomially with respect to the statistical complexity of the model class, without any explicit
polynomial dependence on the size of the state and observation spaces. Notably, our work is the
first work that shows polynomial sample complexities to compete with the globally optimal policy in
PSRs. Finally, we demonstrate how our general theorem can be directly used to derive sample
complexity bounds for special models including mm-step weakly revealing and mm-step decodable
tabular POMDPs, POMDPs with low-rank latent transition, and POMDPs with linear emission and latent
transition.

1 Introduction

Efficient exploration strategies in reinforcement learning have been well investigated on many
models from tabular models (Jaksch et al.,, 2010; Azar et al.,, 2017) to models that enable us to
use general function approximation (Du et al.,, 2021; Jiang et al.,, 2017; Jin et al.,, 2021;
Foster et al.,, 2021; Sun et al.,, 2019). These works have focused on fully observable Markov
decision processes (MDPs); however, their algorithms do not result in statistically efficient
algorithms in partially observable Markov decision processes (POMDPs). Since the markovian
properties of dynamics are often questionable in practice, POMDPs are known to be useful models
that capture environments in real life. While strategic exploration in POMDPs was less investigated
due to its difficulty, it has been actively studied in recent few years (Guo et al.,, 2016;
Azizzadenesheli et al.,, 2016; Jin et al.,, 2020). In our work, beyond POMDPs, we consider
Predictive state representation (PSR) (Littman and Sutton,, 2001; Singh et al.,, 2004; Jaeger,,
1998) that is a more general model of controlled dynamical systems than POMDPs.

PSRs are specified by the probability of a sequence of future observations/actions (referred to as
a test) conditioned on the past history. Unlike the POMDP model, PSR directly predicts the future
given the past without modeling the latent state/dynamics. PSRs can model every POMDP, but
potentially result in much more compact representations; there are dynamical systems that have
finite PSR ranks, but that cannot be modeled by any POMDPs with finite latent states (Littman and
Sutton,, 2001; Jaeger,, 1998).

PSRs are not only general but also amenable to learning and scalable. First, PSRs can be
efficiently learned from exploratory data using a spectral learning algorithm (Boots et al.,, 2011)
motivated by method-of-moments (Hsu et al.,, 2012). This learning algorithm allows us to perform
fast closed-form sequential filtering, unlike EM-type algorithms that would be the most natural
algorithm derived from POMDP perspectives. Secondly, while original PSRs are defined in the tabular
setting, PSRs also support rich functional forms through kernel mean embedding (Boots et al.,, 2013
). Variants of PSRs equipped with neural networks have been proposed as well (Sun et al.,, 2016;
Downey et al.,, 2017; Venkatraman et al.,, 2017; Zhang et al.,, 2021).

In spite of the abovementioned advances in research on PSRs made in the recent two decades,
strategic exploration without exploratory data has been barely investigated. To make PSRs more
practical, it is of significant importance to understand how to perform efficient strategic
exploration. To the best of the author’s knowledge, Jiang et al., (2016); Uehara et al., (2022)
tackle this challenge; however, they fail to show results with polynomial sample complexity to
compete with the globally optimal policy. Our aim is to obtain algorithms with polynomial sample
complexity. Another desideratum for algorithms is to permit for general function approximation.
This desideratum is important to enjoy the scalable property of PSRs. In summary, the key question
we wish to address in this work is:

Can we design provably efficient RL algorithms for learning PSR with function approximation?

Our main contributions are summarized below.

We characterize the sample complexity guarantee of CRANE to learn a near-optimal policy for PSRs in

1.1 Related Work

PSRs and its learning algorithm

PSRs represent states as a vector of predictions about future events (Littman and Sutton,, 2001;
Singh et al.,, 2004; Rosencrantz et al.,, 2004; Hamilton et al.,, 2014; Thon and Jaeger,, 2015;
Grinberg et al.,, 2018). Importantly, compared to well-known models of dynamical systems like HMMs
that postulate latent state variables that are never observed, we do not need to refer to latent
state variables and every definition relies on observable quantities. While PSRs were originally
introduced in the tabular setting, PSRs can be extended to the non-tabular setting using
conditional mean embeddings (Boots et al.,, 2013). Using data obtained by exploratory open-loop
policies such as uniform policies, Boots et al., (2011); Boots et al., (2013); Zhang et al., (2021)
proposed a learning algorithm for dynamics by leveraging spectral learning (Kulesza et al.,, 2014;
Hsu et al.,, 2012; Jiang et al.,, 2018). Later, Hefny et al., (2015) pointed out an insightful
connection between spectral learning and supervised learning (more specifically, instrumental
variable regression when histories are instrumental). Based on this viewpoint, Hefny et al., (2015)
proposed a two-stage regression learning algorithm. Compared to these settings, our setting is
significantly challenging. This is because their goal is learning system dynamics with exploratory
offline data while we want to learn the optimal policy when we don’t have access to such
exploratory data.

Provably efficient RL for POMDPs and PSRs.

Seminal works (Kearns et al.,, 1999; Even-Dar et al.,, 2005) obtained AHA^{H}-type sample
complexity bounds for POMDPs. We can avoid exponential dependence with more structural assumptions.
Recently, there is a growing body of literature that discusses provably efficient RL in the online
setting under various structures.

In the tabular setting, one of the most standard structural assumptions is an observability (i.e.,
weakly-revealing) assumption, which implies that observations retain information about latent
states. Under observability and various additional assumptions, in Azizzadenesheli et al., (2016);
Guo et al., (2016); Kwon et al., (2021), favorable polynomial sample complexities are obtained by
leveraging the spectral learning technique (Hsu et al.,, 2012). Later, Jin et al., (2020); Liu et
al., (2022) improve these results and obtain polynomial sample complexity results under only
observability assumptions. Golowich et al., 2022a (); Golowich et al., 2022b () develop algorithms
with quasi-polynomial sample and computational complexity under observability properties.

In the non-tabular POMDP setting, several positive results are obtained. One of the most
investigated models is linear quadratic gaussian (LQG), which is a partial observable version of
LQRs. Lale et al., (2020); Simchowitz et al., (2020) proposed sub-linear regret algorithms.
Polynomial sample complexities are obtained on other various POMDP models such as M-step decodable
POMDPs (Efroni et al.,, 2022) where we can decode the latent state by mm-step back histories (when
m=1m=1, it is Block MDP), weakly-revealing linear-mixture type POMDPs (Cai et al.,, 2022) where
emission and transition are modeled by linear mixture models, weakly-revealing low-rank POMDPs (
Uehara et al.,, 2022) where latent transition have low-rank structures. Our proposed algorithm can
capture all of the abovementioned models except for LQG.

There are few works that discuss strategic exploration in PSRs. None of them obtain polynomial
sample complexity results for learning approximate globally optimal policies (Jiang et al.,, 2016;
Uehara et al.,, 2022). For details, refer to Section 4.

by the definition of the rank, we know that |𝒬h+1|=dPSR,h|\mathcal{Q}_{h+1}|=d_{\mathrm{PSR},h}. To

in[H]}|\mathcal{U}_{h}|,|\mathcal{U}_{A}|:=\max_{h\in[H]}|\mathcal{U}_{A,h}|. In this paper we
assume a core test 𝒰h\mathcal{U}_{h} (we will see that this is a natural assumption for models such
as POMDPs) is given while 𝒬h\mathcal{Q}_{h} is unknown. This setting is standard in literature on
PSRs (Boots et al.,, 2011).

Remark 1.

PSRs do not have latent states, unlike POMDPs. However, the minimum core histories can be viewed as
the "states" in PSRs because we can derive the system dynamics conditioned on any possible history
from the dynamics conditioned on these core histories. As we will see, our algorithm does not
require knowing the minimum core history set. The minimum core history set will only be used in the
analysis.

PSRs vs POMDPs.

PSRs have much stronger expressivity than POMDPs. All POMDPs can be expressed as PSRs with the
minimum core test set size as most |𝒮||\mathcal{S}| while PSRs are not necessarily compact POMDPs (
Littman and Sutton,, 2001). In Appendix A we construct a sequential decision making process where
if we want to formulate it into a POMDP, the number of states we need will be exponentially larger
than the core test set size in the PSR formulation. The key intuition behind the construction is
simple: the non-negative rank of a matrix could be exponentially larger than its rank. In the
literature (Singh et al.,, 2004), there are also some other concrete instances like probability
clock which POMDPs cannot model with finite latent states while PSRs can model with finite rank.

2.2 Function Approximation

To deal with the potentially large observation and action space, we consider learning with function
approximation in this paper. We assume a function class ℱ\mathcal{F} to approximate the true model
and let ℙfπ​(τH)\mathbb{P}_{f}^{\pi}(\tau_{H}) denote the probability of any trajectory τH\tau_{H}
under any policy π\pi and model ff. Here we assume that the models in ℱ\mathcal{F} are all valid
PSRs with core test set {𝒰h}h∈[H]\{\mathcal{U}_{h}\}_{h\in[H]}, which implies that for each f∈ℱf\in
\mathcal{F}, we can calculate its corresponding predictive operator matrices, initial predictive
states and core matrices, denoted by Mo,a,h;f,q0;f,Kh;fM_{o,a,h;f},q_{0;f},K_{h;f} respectively. We
define VfπV_{f}^{\pi} to be the value of policy π\pi under model ff. We also use f∗f^{*} to
represent the true model for consistency.

Generally, we put models on {Mo,a,h;f,q0:f}\{M_{o,a,h;f},q_{0:f}\} since this is the most natural
parametrization in PSRs. When we have more prior knowledge about models like models are POMDPs, we
can also put models on {𝕋h,𝕆h,μ1}\{\mathbb{T}_{h},\mathbb{O}_{h},\mu_{1}\}.

To measure the size of ℱ\mathcal{F}, we use |ℱ||\mathcal{F}| to denote its cardinality when ℱ\
mathcal{F} is finite. For infinite function classes, we introduce the ϵ\epsilon-bracket number to
measure its size, which is defined as follows:

Definition 2 (ϵ\epsilon-bracket and ϵ\epsilon-bracket number).

A size-NN ϵ\epsilon-bracket is a bracket {g1i,g2i}i=1N\{g^{i}_{1},g^{i}_{2}\}_{i=1}^{N} where g1i​
(g2i)g^{i}_{1}(g^{i}_{2}) is a function mapping any policy π\pi and trajectory τ\tau to ℝ\mathbb{R}
such that for all i∈[N]i\in[N], ‖g1i​(π,⋅)−g2i​(π,⋅)‖1≤ϵ\|g^{i}_{1}(\pi,\cdot)-g^{i}_{2}(\pi,\cdot)
\|_{1}\leq\epsilon for any policy π\pi, and for any f∈ℱf\in\mathcal{F}, there must exist an i∈[N]i\
in[N] such that g1i​(π,τH)≤ℙfπ​(τH)≤g2i​(π,τH)g^{i}_{1}(\pi,\tau_{H})\leq\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{f}(\tau_
{H})\leq g^{i}_{2}(\pi,\tau_{H}) for all τH,π\tau_{H},\pi. The ϵ\epsilon-bracket number of ℱ\
mathcal{F}, denoted by 𝒩ℱ​(ϵ)\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{F}}(\epsilon), is the minimum size of such an ϵ\

Although ℙfπ\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{f} is an (|𝒪|​|𝒜|)H(|\mathcal{O}||\mathcal{A}|)^{H}-dimensional
vector, its log ϵ\epsilon-bracket number will not scale exponentially with HH because ℙfπ\mathbb{P}
^{\pi}_{f} is Lipschitz continuous with respect to {Mo,a,h;f,q0;f}\{M_{o,a,h;f},q_{0;f}\}, whose

mathcal{F} can be upper bounded by the covering number of {Mo,a,h;f,q0;f}\{M_{o,a,h;f},q_{0;f}\} in
linear PSRs, and we provide exact upper bounds for tabular PSRs and various POMDPs.

Remark 2.

The reason we need ϵ\epsilon-bracket number instead of ϵ\epsilon-covering number is that the
confidence set of maximum log-likelihood estimators (MLE) for infinite function classes, which we
will utilize in our algorithm, is characterized by the bracket number (Geer et al.,, 2000). That
said, the log bracket number in our setting often only has an additional HH factor compared to log
covering number, as shown in Appendix D.

3 Algorithm: CRANE

The statistical hardness of learning POMDPs due to the partial observability is well-known in the
literature (Krishnamurthy et al.,, 2016), which also exists in PSR learning since PSRs are a more
general model. In addition, existing algorithms (Jin et al.,, 2020; Efroni et al.,, 2022; Liu et
al.,, 2022) for learning sub-classes of POMDPs require the existence of latent states since they
directly put models on 𝕋\mathbb{T} and 𝕆\mathbb{O}. Thus, their methods are not applicable to PSRs.
That said, the existence of predictive states and their evolving dynamics (3) indeed imply the
low-rank linear structure of PSRs. The trajectory probability decomposition (4) further suggests
that we are able to capture a PSR completely as long as we can learn the predictive operator
matrices {Mo,a,h}o∈𝒪,a∈𝒜,h∈[H−1]\{M_{o,a,h}\}_{o\in\mathcal{O},a\in\mathcal{A},h\in[H-1]} and the
initial predictive state q0q_{0} efficiently. Therefore, inspired form the success of maximum
log-likelihood estimation (MLE) in learning weakly-revealing POMDPs (Liu et al.,, 2022), we propose
a new MLE-based PSR learning algorithm to learn these parameters as follows.

CRANE.

Comparision with Liu et al., (2022).

The main difference is that our algorithm can allow more general models. For example, in PSRs, we
can generally take {Mo,a,h,q0}\{M_{o,a,h},q_{0}\} that depends on only observable quantities as a
model ff. On the other hand, Liu et al., (2022) attempts to put models on {𝕋h,𝕆h}\{\mathbb{T}_{h},\
mathbb{O}_{h}\} that involve latent states. The practical benefit of modeling {Mo,a,h,q0}\{M_
{o,a,h},q_{0}\} is we don’t need to specify the latent space while Liu et al., (2022) needs to do.
Since we often do not have good prior knowledge about latent states, our algorithm would be more
practical in this scenario. Due to the generality of our algorithm, we can capture more models such
as mm-step decodable POMDPs and low-rank POMDPs as we will see in the following sections. Another
difference is we only execute the action sequences in the core test set, i.e., 𝒰A,h\mathcal{U}_
{A,h}, actively. On the other hand, Liu et al., (2022) executes the whole mm-step futures.

4 Main result

assumption is often imposed in the PSR literature (Jiang et al.,, 2017, Appendix B.4).

Proof techniques of Theorem 1.

The existing analysis for POMDPs is not applicable to PSRs since we do not assume latent states in
PSRs, let alone the emission matrix and transition matrix. In our proof, we utilize the linear
nature of PSRs and leverage the core matrix KhK_{h} to bound the error propagation induced by the
product of predictive operator matrices, i.e., ∏h=1H−1Moh,ah,h\prod_{h=1}^{H-1}M_{o_{h},a_{h},h} in
(4). This key step enables us to bound the difference of model dynamics ℙfπ\mathbb{P}_{f}^{\pi} and
ℙπ\mathbb{P}^{\pi} (i.e., ℙf⋆π\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{f^{\star}} ) by the estimation error of Moh,ah,hM_
{o_{h},a_{h},h} and q0q_{0}, and thus obtain a polynomial bound on the total suboptimality. This
also implies the core matrix KhK_{h} that characterizes the relationships between the histories and
the future may be a more intrinsic quantity than the emission matrix. As byproduct of our analysis,
even in POMDPs, we can capture models with low-rank latent transition that Liu et al., (2022)
cannot capture.

Comparison with existing works on PSRs.

As far as we know, there are only two works that tackle provably efficient RL for PSRs. Jiang et
al., (2016) shows a polynomial sample complexity result in reactive POMDPs where optimal value
functions only depend on current observations. Later, Uehara et al., (2022) shows a favorable
sample complexity result without this assumption. However, their result is an agnostic-type result
that depends on (|𝒪|​|𝒜|)M(|\mathcal{O}||\mathcal{A}|)^{M} when competing with MM-memory policies.
Thus, to compete with the globally optimal policy, their results do not imply a polynomial sample
complexity bound.

We note existing works (Boots et al.,, 2013; Hefny et al.,, 2015; Boots et al.,, 2010; Kulesza et
al.,, 2014) on the system identification perspective of PSRs explicitly assume the knowledge of
short core histories that include minimum core histories. On the other hand, we do not require this
assumption since our algorithm is agnostic to minimum core histories. Minimum core histories are
just used for analytical purposes.

6.5 mm-Step Decodable Tabular/Low-Rank/Linear POMDPs

Next, we instantiate our result on mm-step decodable POMDPs (Efroni et al.,, 2022) defined as
follows.

Definition 6 (mm-step decodable POMDPs).

There exist unknown decoders {ϕdec,h}m≤h≤H\{\phi_{\mathrm{dec},h}\}_{m\leq h\leq H} such that for
every reachable trajctory τH\tau_{H}, we have sh=ϕdec,h​(zh)s_{h}=\phi_{\mathrm{dec},h}(z_{h}) for
all m≤h≤Hm\leq h\leq H where zh=((o,a)h−m+1:h−1,oh)z_{h}=((o,a)_{h-m+1:h-1},o_{h}).

This definition says that we can decode the current state with mm-step history. When m=1m=1, they
are reduced to block MDPs (Du et al.,, 2019). Surprisingly, mm-step decodable POMDPs can be
formulated as PSRs where core tests are mm-step futures. Intuitively, this is proved by the
observation that mm-step futures can decode the latent state mm-step ahead, i.e., sm+hs_{m+h} by
treating “histories" in the definition as “futures". In Appendix B we have a more detailed
discussion. Thus, further noting the PSR rank is |𝒮||\mathcal{S}| in the tabular case and dtransd_
{\mathrm{trans}} in low-rank POMDPs, we have the following theorem:

  • •

Comparison with Efroni et al., (2022).

Efroni et al., (2022) works on mm-step decodable tabular POMDPs and show sample complexity
polynomial in |𝒮|,H,|𝒜|m,1/ϵ,log⁡(1/δ)|\mathcal{S}|,H,|\mathcal{A}|^{m},1/\epsilon,\log(1/\delta)
and log covering number of a value function class. They also provide a result on mm-step decodable
low-rank POMDPs where the sample complexity scales with dtransd_{\mathrm{trans}} rather than |𝒮||\
mathcal{S}|.

However, there are some differences between their results and Corollary 6. First, the log covering
number of the value function class in their results will typically scale with poly⁡(|𝒪|m)\mathrm
{poly}(|\mathcal{O}|^{m}). Our results, on the other hand, only scale with poly⁡(|𝒪|)\mathrm{poly}
(|\mathcal{O}|) since the log bracket number of our function classes only scales with poly⁡(|𝒪|)\
mathrm{poly}(|\mathcal{O}|). Secondly, the analysis in Efroni et al., (2022) does not require the
regularity-type assumption (Assumption 1). This is because their algorithm is tailored to mm-step
decodable POMDPs. The lower bound in Theorem 2 has shown that the scaling with the regularity
parameter 1/α1/\alpha is inevitable in PSRs, highlighting the necessity of such regularity in
general.

7 Conclusion

We consider PAC learning in PSRs that represent states as a vector of prediction about future
events conditioned on histories. We propose CRANE and show polynomial sample complexities when we
compete with the globally optimal policy. Our work is the first work attending this goal. Since
PSRs are more general than POMDPs, we instantiate our result to several concrete POMDPs such as
mm-step weak-revealing POMDPs, mm-step decodable POMDPs, POMDPs with latent low-lank transition,
and POMDPs with linear emission and latent transition. Notably, our work is the first work that
simultaneously achieves polynomial sample complexities in mm-step weak-revealing POMDPs and mm-step
decodable POMDPs. In POMDPs with latent low-lank transition and POMDPs with linear emission and
latent transition, our result is still new in that we show some of the assumptions in existing
literature (Cai et al.,, 2022; Wang et al.,, 2022) can be replaced with possibly milder
assumptions, i.e., regularity assumption.
