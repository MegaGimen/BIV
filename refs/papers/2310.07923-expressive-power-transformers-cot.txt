The Expressive Power of Transformers with Chain of Thought

William Merrill Affiliation: New York University Email: willm@nyu.edu    Ashish Sabharwal 
Affiliation: Allen Institute for AI Email: ashishs@allenai.org

Abstract

Recent theoretical work has identified surprisingly simple reasoning problems, such as checking if
two nodes in a graph are connected or simulating finite-state machines, that are provably
unsolvable by standard transformers that answer immediately after reading their input. However, in
practice, transformers’ reasoning can be improved by allowing them to use a “chain of thought” or
“scratchpad”, i.e., generate and condition on a sequence of intermediate tokens before answering.
Motivated by this, we ask: Does such intermediate generation fundamentally extend the computational
power of a decoder-only transformer? We show that the answer is yes, but the amount of increase
depends crucially on the amount of intermediate generation. For instance, we find that transformer
decoders with a logarithmic number of decoding steps (w.r.t. the input length) push the limits of
standard transformers only slightly, while a linear number of decoding steps, assuming projected
pre-norm (a slight generalization of standard pre-norm), adds a clear new ability (under standard
complexity conjectures): recognizing all regular languages. Our results also imply that linear
steps keep transformer decoders within context-sensitive languages, and polynomial steps with
generalized pre-norm make them recognize exactly the class of polynomial-time solvable problems—the
first exact characterization of a type of transformers in terms of standard complexity classes.
Together, this provides a nuanced framework for understanding how the length of a transformer’s
chain of thought or scratchpad impacts its reasoning power.



1 Introduction

A series of recent theoretical results (Merrill & Sabharwal, 2023b; Merrill & Sabharwal, 2023a;
Merrill et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023; Chiang et al., 2023; Hao et al., 2022) has unveiled
surprising limits on realistic formal models of transformers. They have shown that standard
transformers, even with ideal parameters, cannot perfectly solve many sequential reasoning problems
at scale, such as simulating finite-state machines, deciding whether nodes in a graph are
connected, or solving matrix equalities. The intuition here is that the transformer lacks recurrent
connections, and recurrence is required to solve these sequential reasoning problems. Empirically,
reasoning problems inspired by these results cannot be solved by cutting-edge transformer language
models such as ChatGPT and GPT-4 (Zhang et al., 2023), and the reasoning performance of GPT-4
negatively correlates with the depth of the problem’s computation graph (Dziri et al., 2023). These
results show certain kinds of sequential reasoning pose a challenge for the transformer and
motivate extensions to address this issue.

One method that has been empirically successful for improving sequential reasoning with
transformers is adding a so-called chain of thought (Wei et al., 2022) or scratchpad (Nye et al.,
2021). These methods allow the transformer to output a sequence of intermediate tokens before
answering, rather than answering right away after reading the input. Intuitively, such methods
could unlock greater expressive power on sequential reasoning problems because the model can use
each intermediate token as a kind of recurrent state. Feng et al. (2023) recently showed how chain
of thought lets transformers solve a specific modular arithmetic problem that they likely cannot
solve without one. Yet there is no general characterization of the class of problems transformers
can solve with chain of thought. Thus, the extent to which chain of thought alleviates
transformers’ weaknesses is unclear, as well as the number of chain of thought steps required to
gain reasoning power.

In this work, we address these open questions by characterizing the reasoning power of transformer
decoders that can take intermediate steps before generating an answer and comparing them against
transformers without intermediate steps. A transformer with a chain of thought constitutes a
special case of a transformer decoder with intermediate steps. Our fine-grained results give upper
and lower bounds on transformers’ power depending on t⁡(n)t(n): the number of allowed intermediate
steps as a function of the input size nn. We focus mainly on understanding three regimes:
logarithmic steps (when t⁡(n)=Θ⁡(log⁡n)t(n)=\Theta(\log n)), linear steps (when t⁡(n)=Θ⁡(n)t(n)=\
Theta(n)), and polynomial steps:

Here 𝖢𝗈𝖳⁡(t⁡(n))\mathsf{CoT}(t(n)) denotes the set of languages recognized by some transformer
using t⁡(n)t(n) decoding steps. Our lower bound (left side of Equation 1) assumes strict causal
saturated attention and projected pre-norm, while upper bounds hold both with and without these
architectural assumptions. Both our time lower bound and space upper bound are fairly tight:
improving either by a factor larger than log⁡t⁡(n)\log t(n) would result in a fundamental
complexity theory advance (Hopcroft et al., 1977).

Capabilities of Transformers with CoT.

The left side of Equation 1 implies that transformer decoders with Θ⁡(n)\Theta(n) steps can
simulate real-time models of computation like automata or counter machines (Merrill, 2020). Under
standard assumptions in complexity theory, transformers with no decoding steps cannot simulate all
automata (Merrill & Sabharwal, 2023b; Merrill, 2023; Liu et al., 2023). Thus, a linear number of
decoding steps makes transformers strictly more powerful. Similarly, the left side of Equation 1
implies transformers with a quadratic number of steps can express a linear-time algorithm (for a
random access Turing machine) to solve directed graph connectivity (Wigderson, 1992), again a
problem known to be beyond the limits of standard transformers. In the same vein, with a polynomial
number of decoding steps, transformers can solve linear equalities, Horn-clause satisfiability, and
universal context-free recognition, all of which are 𝖯\mathsf{P}-complete and thus known to be
inexpressible by standard transformers (Merrill & Sabharwal, 2023b).

The left side of Equation 1 is proven by showing transformer decoders can simulate tt Turing
machine steps with tt intermediate steps. Similar prior results have assumed a transformer with
external memory (Schuurmans, 2023) or an encoder-decoder model with nonstandard-positional
decodings (Pérez et al., 2021). Our construction adapts these ideas to work for a decoder-only
model without external memory or extra positional encodings, but with strict causal masking and
projected pre-norm (cf. Section 2.1).^5^5 5 Our construction (Theorem 2) can be easily modified to
work with an encoder-decoder model as well. The key idea behind our more general construction is
the layer-norm hash (Section 3.1): a simple module for effectively storing memory in decoder-only
transformers. We believe the layer-norm hash could be broadly useful for building algorithms in
transformers. For example, Yao et al. (2021) used a related idea to construct transformers that
recognize bounded-depth Dyck languages, although in a more ad hoc way.

Limitations of Transformers with CoT.

The right side of Equation 1 establishes two upper bounds on transformer decoders with t⁡(n)t(n)
intermediate steps that depend on both t⁡(n)t(n) and nn. We turn to the implications of this
general result in different regimes for t⁡(n)t(n):

 1. 1.
   
    Log Steps: Transformer decoders with O⁡(log⁡n)\mathrm{O}(\log n) intermediate steps can only
    recognize languages in 𝖫=𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤⁡(log⁡n)\mathsf{L}=\mathsf{SPACE}(\log n). This implies that
    transformers with O⁡(log⁡n)\mathrm{O}(\log n) intermediate steps cannot solve 𝖭𝖫\mathsf{NL}- or
    𝖯\mathsf{P}-complete problems^2 like directed graph connectivity, just like transformers with
    no intermediate decoding (Merrill & Sabharwal, 2023b).

 2. 2.
   
    Linear Steps: Transformer decoders with O⁡(n)\mathrm{O}(n) intermediate steps can only
    recognize languages that are in both 𝖳𝖨𝖬𝖤~​(n2)\widetilde{\mathsf{TIME}}(n^{2}) and 𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤⁡(n)\
    mathsf{SPACE}(n). Since 𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤⁡(n)\mathsf{SPACE}(n) falls within the context-sensitive languages
    (Kuroda, 1964), transformers with linear steps can recognize at most context-sensitive
    languages. Alongside our lower bound, this shows transformer decoders with Θ⁡(n)\Theta(n) steps
    fall somewhere between regular and context-sensitive languages in the Chomsky hierarchy.
    Further, transformers with O⁡(n)\mathrm{O}(n) steps cannot recognize all context-free languages
    unless context-free languages can be parsed in soft quadratic time.^6^6 6 The best known
    algorithms for context-free recognition run in time O⁡(nω)\mathrm{O}(n^{\omega}), where ω\omega
    is the matrix multiplication constant (Valiant, 1975); the best lower bounds for context-free
    parsing are sub-quadratic (Lee, 2002).

 3. 3.
   
    Polynomial Steps: If t⁡(n)=O⁡(nc)t(n)=\mathrm{O}(n^{c}) for some cc, we get an upper bound of 𝖯
    =⋃c=1∞𝖳𝖨𝖬𝖤⁡(nc)\mathsf{P}=\bigcup_{c=1}^{\infty}\mathsf{TIME}(n^{c}). Combined with our lower
    bound, this shows that transformer decoders with a polynomial number of steps recognize exactly
    the class 𝖯\mathsf{P}. Thus, a polynomial number of steps turns transformers into strong
    reasoners, though running a polynomial number of forward passes with a large transformer is
    likely intractable in practice.

Together, these results show that intermediate generation like chain of thought or scratchpad can
add reasoning power to transformers and that the number of steps matters as a computational
resource akin to time or space. Some of the limitations identified in prior work (Merrill &
Sabharwal, 2023b; Chiang et al., 2023, etc.) can be overcome with a linear or quadratic number of
steps, and a polynomial number of steps covers all problems in 𝖯\mathsf{P}. On the other hand, we
have not identified any concrete reasoning problem where a logarithmic number of steps would help.
These results provide a unified understanding of the power of transformer decoders across decoding
lengths and problems.

2 Preliminaries

We study the power of decoder-only transformers that can generate intermediate tokens between
reading the input and generating an answer. On input x∈Σnx\in\Sigma^{n}, the transformer consumes
tokens x1,…,xnx_{1},\ldots,x_{n} for the first nn steps, and then, for t⁡(n)t(n) intermediate steps
, consumes the token generated by the previous step. At each step, the transformer can attend over
all previous hidden states. This standard method of generating text from a decoder-only model can
be described formally as follows. Let Σ\Sigma be a finite alphabet and f:Σ∗→Σf:\Sigma^{*}\to\Sigma
be a function mapping a prefix to a next token (parameterized by a transformer). Let ⋅\cdot be
concatenation. We define the kk-step extension of ff as

 cdot f(f^{k}(x)).

We say we have run ff on xx with t⁡(n)t(n) (additional) decoding steps if we compute the function
ft⁡(|x|)​(x)f^{t(\left\lvert x\right\rvert)}(x). We consider ff with t⁡(n)t(n) steps to recognize
the language of strings such that ft⁡(|x|)​(x)=1f^{t(\left\lvert x\right\rvert)}(x)=1, where 1∈Σ1\
in\Sigma is a special “accept” symbol. We denote by 𝖢𝗈𝖳⁡(t⁡(n))\mathsf{CoT}(t(n)) the set of
languages that are recognized by t⁡(n)t(n) decoding steps for some transformer ff.

Transformer Precision.

We consider log-precision transformers (Merrill & Sabharwal, 2023b), i.e., we allow the transformer
at most c​log⁡mc\log m precision for mm decoding steps. As a transformer with intermediate
generation runs for nn input steps and t⁡(n)t(n) intermediate decoding steps, this means we have
precision at most c​log⁡(n+t⁡(n))c\log(n+t(n)). Log precision has been analyzed in prior work (
Pérez et al., 2021; Merrill & Sabharwal, 2023b; Merrill & Sabharwal, 2023a) because it gives the
transformer just enough precision to represent indexes and sums across different positions. This
means it naturally formalizes a bounded-precision transformer that is capable of representing
position and computing uniform attention, two important capabilities for constructing algorithms
with transformers.

Our lower bound constructions (Theorems 1 and 2) assume the following:

 1. 1.
   
    Saturated Attention. A saturated transformer (Merrill et al., 2021) is an idealized transformer
    with “averaging hard attention” (Strobl et al., 2024): per head, all attention scores are
    either 00 or 1/v1/v for some vv. This includes uniform attention (1/n1/n over nn tokens) or
    hard attention as special cases. Following common practice (Pérez et al., 2021; Merrill &
    Sabharwal, 2023b), we use saturated attention for our lower bound constructions.

 2. 2.
   
    Strict Causal Masking. The formulation of attention in Definition 2 makes the slightly
    nonstandard assumption that causally masked attention at position ii can view tokens at all
    positions up to i−1i-1 but not the current token ii. This is required in Theorem 2.

 3. 3.
   
    Projected Pre-Norm. Our lower bound constructions require sℓs_{\ell} and fℓf_{\ell} in
    Definition 2 to allow a generalization of standard pre-norm. Normally, a layer-norm is applied
    to the entire input to each sublayer. We generalize this, allowing each sublayer to apply a
    linear projection before layer-norm. Crucially, in particular, this enables each layer to pick
    out a subset of the previous hidden state to apply layer-norm to (cf. Definition 4 in Section 
    A.1).

For convenience, our proofs with projected pre-norm use an even more general notion of pre-norm,
namely multi-pre-norm, which allows each sublayer to take kk different projections of its input,
apply layer-norm to each, and concatenate (cf. Definition 5 in Section A.1). Multi-pre-norm can,
however, be simulated by multiple layers of projected pre-norm (see Section A.1 for a proof):

Proposition 1 (Chiang, 2024).

Multi-pre-norm with kk norms can be simulated by k+1k+1 projected pre-norm layers.

3 Lower Bounds for Transformer Decoders

Prior work (Merrill & Sabharwal, 2023a) has established strong upper bounds on the reasoning
problems transformers can solve. Specifically, under standard conjectures in complexity,
transformers without intermediate decoding cannot recognize all regular languages. In this section,
we show some of these shortcomings can be overcome with a suitable number of intermediate decoding
steps (and projected pre-norm). Specifically, a linear number of steps enables simulating an
automaton. We also show this can be extended to simulate t⁡(n)t(n) Turing machine steps with t⁡(n)t
(n) decoding steps.

3.1 Introducing Layer-Norm Hash

We first introduce a useful building block for our results that we call the layer-norm hash. The
layer-norm hash is a mechanism that enables retrieval across different columns in the transformer
based on query-key matching of numerical values. Exact-match retrieval is trivial when the query
qiq_{i} and keys k1,…​kik_{1},\ldots k_{i} are items in a finite set: just one-hot encode qiq_{i}
and kjk_{j} and the inner product will be maximized when qiq_{i} and kjk_{j} match. But this does
not work when the keys and values are counts produced by uniform attention, which many transformer
algorithms use (Weiss et al., 2021), as the key qi/iq_{i}/i and query kj/jk_{j}/j have different
denominators. The layer-norm hash helps by transforming qi/iq_{i}/i and kj/jk_{j}/j so hard
attention retrieves jj s.t. qi=kjq_{i}=k_{j}. Let 𝗅𝖺𝗒𝖾𝗋​_​𝗇𝗈𝗋𝗆​(𝐱)=𝐱′‖𝐱′‖\mathsf{layer\_norm}(\
mathbf{x})=\frac{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\rVert}, where 𝐱′=𝐱−x¯\
mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{x}-\bar{x}.

Definition 3 (Layer-norm hash).

For x,y∈ℝx,y\in\mathbb{R}, let ϕ⁡(x,y)≜𝗅𝖺𝗒𝖾𝗋​_​𝗇𝗈𝗋𝗆​(x,y,−x,−y)\phi(x,y)\triangleq\mathsf{layer\
_norm}(x,y,-x,-y).

ϕ⁡(x,y)\phi(x,y) is a unit vector in ℝ4\mathbb{R}^{4}. A key feature is scale invariance, and, in
particular, that ϕ⁡(x/i,1/i)\phi(x/i,1/i) is invariant w.r.t. ii in the sense that it is only a
function of xx, independent of ii. Let ϕx≜ϕ⁡(x,1)\phi_{x}\triangleq\phi(x,1). Then we have the
following properties, whose proof may be found in Appendix C.

Lemma 1 (Scale invariance).

For any x∈ℝx\in\mathbb{R} and i∈ℝ>0i\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}, ϕ⁡(x/i,1/i)=ϕx\phi(x/i,1/i)=\phi_{x}.

Lemma 2 (Equality check via layer-norm hash).

For any q,k∈ℝq,k\in\mathbb{R}, ϕq⋅ϕk=1\phi_{q}\cdot\phi_{k}=1 if and only if q=kq=k.

In other words, the inner product of these representations of two scalars qq and kk, even if
computed at different positions ii and jj, respectively, allows us to check for the equality of qq
and kk. We can look up key qi/iq_{i}/i in a sequence of keys k1/1,…,ki−1/(i−1)k_{1}/1,\ldots,k_
{i-1}/(i-1) by attending with query ϕ⁡(qi/i,1/i)\phi(q_{i}/i,1/i) at position ii and key ϕ⁡(kj/j,1/
j)\phi(k_{j}/j,1/j) at each j<ij<i. By Lemmas 1 and 2 this averages the values at all jj such that
qi=kjq_{i}=k_{j}. The layer-norm hash can also be used to directly compare two values qi,kjq_{i},k_
{j} without removing the denominator by computing ϕ⁡(qi,1)\phi(q_{i},1) and ϕ⁡(kj,1)\phi(k_{j},1).

3.2 Simulating Automata

We can use the layer-norm hash to simulate models of computation like automata or Turing machines
with intermediate-generation transformers. To warm up, we first show how to use the layer-norm hash

show how a transformer can simulate a Turing machine for a bounded number of steps.

Theorem 1 shows that a linear number of decoding steps gives additional reasoning power to
log-precision transformers with projected pre-norm (assuming 𝖳𝖢0≠𝖭𝖢1\mathsf{TC}^{0}\neq\mathsf{NC}^
{1}). This follows because log-precision transformers with no decoding steps are contained in
uniform 𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{TC}^{0} (Merrill & Sabharwal, 2023b), which means they cannot recognize all
regular languages. In contrast, Theorem 1 says a linear number of steps is sufficient for
recognizing all regular languages, establishing a conditional separation. This is an example of
simple and familiar additional computational power granted by additional decoding steps. The core
challenge in simulating an automaton is recurrence, which cannot be done without decoding steps (
Merrill & Sabharwal, 2023b). A linear number of decoding steps allows simulating recurrence, which
is where the additional power comes from. However, this added power does not stop with finite-state
machines: the layer-norm hash can be used to simulate more complex models of computation such as
Turing machines, which we will turn to next.

3.3 Simulating Turing Machines

We now show how a transformer decoder can simulate a Turing machine in real time using the
layer-norm hash. Our decoder-only construction resembles the encoder-decoder construction of Pérez
et al. (2021). However, it avoids simplifying assumptions from Pérez et al. (2021). In addition to
assuming non-standard attention and no layer-norm, they required 1/i,1/i2,1/i,1/i^{2}, and ii in
the positional embeddings, which is problematic because transformers cannot represent unbounded
scalars like ii due to layer-norm. In contrast, our construction works with or without positional
encodings. However, it assumes strict causal masking and projected pre-norm (Section 2.1).

Theorem 2 gives us a general result connecting the power of transformer decoders with t⁡(n)t(n)
steps to Turing machines with the same number of intermediate steps:

Corollary 2.1.

𝖳𝖨𝖬𝖤⁡(t⁡(n))⊆𝖢𝗈𝖳⁡(t⁡(n))\mathsf{TIME}(t(n))\subseteq\mathsf{CoT}(t(n)).

Thus, simulating an automaton (cf. Theorem 1) is not the only new capability unlocked with O⁡(n)\

solve in O⁡(n)\mathrm{O}(n) time, such as simulating real-time counter machines (Weiss et al., 2018

graph traversal algorithms like depth-first search. Depth-first search runs in O⁡(n)\mathrm{O}(n)
time on a random access Turing machine (Wigderson, 1992), which can be simulated in O⁡(n2)\mathrm

with fewer than O⁡(n2)\mathrm{O}(n^{2}) steps, as results from Zhang et al. (2023) hint at.

4 Upper Bounds for Transformer Decoders

Having shown lower bounds on transformers with t⁡(n)t(n) steps, we present two different upper
bounds: one that relates transformer decoders to time complexity classes, and one that relates them
to space complexity classes. The relative strength of the two different bounds will vary depending
on t⁡(n)t(n). A simple upper bound on transformers with chain of thought can be obtained based on
the fact that transformers can be simulated using a quadratic number of arithmetic operations.

Theorem 3.

𝖢𝗈𝖳⁡(t⁡(n))⊆𝖳𝖨𝖬𝖤~​(n2+t​(n)2)\mathsf{CoT}(t(n))\subseteq\widetilde{\mathsf{TIME}}(n^{2}+t(n)^{2}).

Proof.

We sketch a multitape Turing machine that will simulate the transformer. Each forward pass ii
appends key ii onto a work tape and value ii onto another work tape. To simulate the forward pass
at time ii, it suffices to show how to simulate computing self-attention at time ii. To compute
self attention, the Turing machine first computes the query at time ii. It then iterates over pairs
on the key and value work tapes. For each pair jj, we compute the attention score between query ii
and key jj and then multiply it by value jj using additional work tapes. We then add this value to
a running sum tape. We treat the final sum at the output of the attention mechanism.

This runs n+t⁡(n)n+t(n) forward passes, and each forward pass loops over n+t⁡(n)n+t(n) key-value
pairs. This means we run at most O⁡(n2+t​(n)2)\mathrm{O}(n^{2}+t(n)^{2}) inner loop calls. It
remains to be shown that one inner loop runs in polylogarithmic time. An inner loop just involves
adding and multiplying O⁡(log⁡n)\mathrm{O}(\log n)-bit numbers. pp-bit numbers can be added in time
O⁡(p)=O⁡(log⁡n)\mathrm{O}(p)=\mathrm{O}(\log n). Similarly, pp-bit numbers can be multiplied in
time O⁡(p​log⁡p)≤O⁡(p2)\mathrm{O}(p\log p)\leq\mathrm{O}(p^{2}), which comes out to O⁡(log2⁡(n+t⁡
(n)))\mathrm{O}(\log^{2}(n+t(n))) with log precision. Thus, one inner loop can be run in
polylogarithmic time. We conclude that a transformer decoder with t⁡(n)t(n) intermediate steps can
be simulated by a multitape Turing machine in time O~​(n2+t​(n)2)\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}(n^{2}+t(n)^
{2}). ∎

Our second upper bound relies on the 𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{TC}^{0} upper bound for transformers without
intermediate steps.

Theorem 4.

𝖢𝗈𝖳⁡(t⁡(n))⊆𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤⁡(t⁡(n)+log⁡n)\mathsf{CoT}(t(n))\subseteq\mathsf{SPACE}(t(n)+\log n).

Proof.

Sabharwal, 2023b), they can be simulated in 𝖫\mathsf{L}, i.e., with at most c​log⁡nc\log n space

we store a buffer of at most t⁡(n)t(n) generated tokens, which has size O⁡(t⁡(n))\mathrm{O}(t(n)).
To compute the next token, we call the transformer with an input of size O⁡(n+t⁡(n))\mathrm{O}(n+t
(n)) using at most c​log⁡(n+t⁡(n))c\log(n+t(n)) space overhead. We then clear the memory used and
append the finite token generated to the input buffer. It follows from this algorithm that

                                 ⊆𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤⁡(t⁡(n)+log⁡n).∎\displaystyle\subseteq\mathsf{SPACE}(t(n)+\
                                 log n).\qed

With at least Ω⁡(log⁡n)\Omega(\log n) steps, this upper bound can be simplified to 𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤⁡(t⁡(n))\
mathsf{SPACE}(t(n)). The t⁡(n)=Θ⁡(n)t(n)=\Theta(n) case establishes the context-sensitive languages
as an upper bound for transformers with linear steps. Given our 𝖳𝖨𝖬𝖤⁡(t⁡(n))\mathsf{TIME}(t(n))
lower bound (Theorem 2), the tightest possible space upper bound without making fundamental
complexity advances would be 𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤⁡(t⁡(n)/log⁡t⁡(n))\mathsf{SPACE}(t(n)/\log t(n)) (Hopcroft

{TIME}(t(n)\log t(n)).

On the other hand, with only O⁡(log⁡n)\mathrm{O}(\log n) decoding steps, intermediate decoding does
not increase expressive power much beyond 𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{TC}^{0}, because the upper bound simplifies to
𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤⁡(t⁡(n))=𝖫\mathsf{SPACE}(t(n))=\mathsf{L}. Thus, under standard assumptions, transformers with
a logarithmic number of decoding steps cannot solve directed graph connectivity, Horn formula
satisfiability, or other 𝖭𝖫\mathsf{NL}- or 𝖯\mathsf{P}-complete problems. Yet, they may be able to
solve 𝖫\mathsf{L}-complete problems, unlike transformers without decoding steps.

5 Conclusion

We have shown that intermediate decoding steps extend the formal power of transformers well beyond
previously known upper bounds, such as 𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{TC}^{0} circuits and 𝖥𝖮⁡(𝖬)\mathsf{FO(M)} logic,
on transformers without intermediate decoding. Further, the amount of additional power is closely
related to the number of decoding steps. In particular, transformers with a linear number of
decoding steps have the capacity to recognize regular languages, but cannot recognize languages
beyond context-sensitive. With a log number of decoding steps, such transformers can only recognize
languages in 𝖫\mathsf{L}, which is a complexity class relatively close to 𝖳𝖢0\mathsf{TC}^{0}. Thus,
it appears that a linear number of intermediate decoding steps may be required to overcome the
limitations of transformers on many sequential reasoning problems of interest. In future work, it
may be possible to derive a strict separation between transformers with a log and a linear number
of decoding steps and show that certain problems that currently have a quadratic bound can in fact
be solved with a roughly linear number of steps.

We have focused on expressive power, rather than analyzing learnability. Whereas our upper bounds
directly reveal limitations on what transformers with intermediate generation can learn, one caveat
is that our lower bounds do not directly imply transformers can learn to use intermediate steps
effectively. It would be interesting to formally investigate transformers with CoT from a
learning-theoretic lens, possibly along the lines of Malach (2023), and how different kinds of
fine-tuning, such as reinforcement learning, might better allow models to use the power of chain of
thought.

Appendix B Turing MAchines

A Turing machine takes as input a string σ∈Σ∗\sigma\in\Sigma^{*}. A configuration of a Turing
machine is a finite state qq along with the contents of an input tape c0c^{0}, kk work tapes
c1,…,ckc^{1},\ldots,c^{k}, and an output tape ck+1c^{k+1}. Finally, for each tape τ\tau, a
configuration specifies a head position hτh^{\tau}. We start with the initial state q0q_{0} and the
input tape c00c^{0}_{0} containing σ\sigma starting at position 00 with infinite bb’s on each side,
and h00=0h^{0}_{0}=0. All other tapes start containing all bb’s and with their head at 00. At each
time step ii, if qi∉Fq_{i}\not\in F, we recurrently update the configuration by first computing:

 ⟨qi+1,γi1,…,γik+1,di0,…,dik+1⟩=δ⁡(qi,ci0​[hi0],…,cik+1​[hik+1]).\langle q_{i+1},\gamma^{1}_{i},\
 ldots,\gamma^{k+1}_{i},d^{0}_{i},\ldots,d^{k+1}_{i}\rangle=\delta(q_{i},c^{0}_{i}[h^{0}_{i}],\
 ldots,c^{k+1}_{i}[h^{k+1}_{i}]).

We then update tape τ\tau by setting ci+1τ​[hij]=γijc^{\tau}_{i+1}[h^{j}_{i}]=\gamma^{j}_{i} and
keeping all other tape cells the same. We update the head position on tape τ\tau according to hi+1τ
=hiτ+diτh^{\tau}_{i+1}=h^{\tau}_{i}+d^{\tau}_{i}. On the other hand, if qi∈Fq_{i}\in F, the Turing
machine halts and outputs the string of tokens on the output tape from the current head position on
the left up to (but not including) the first bb on the right. A Turing machine can also be viewed
