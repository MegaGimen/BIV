Internalizing the Future: A Unified Agentic Training Paradigm for World Model Planning

Xuan Zhang^1,2,3    Zhijian Zhou^1,2,3    Lingfeng Qiao^3    Yulei Qin^3    Ke Li^3    Xing Sun^3

Institute, ^3Tencent Youtu Lab
June 25, 2026

Abstract

Large language model (LLM) agents have demonstrated strong capability in sequential
decision-making, yet they remains fundamentally reactive in long-horizon tasks. Unlike humans who
employ ”what-if” reasoning to evaluate potential plans before commitment, standard agents lack an
internal world model to simulate future outcomes. Therefore, we propose to internalize future-aware
planning by training a single autoregressive model to verbalize both a prospective state rollout
and a plan-conditioned success estimate—a textual analogue of the QQ-value. Crucially, we identify
a format-capability gap: simply fine-tuning agents on look-ahead traces during post-training leads
to superficial mimicry of foresight without genuine predictive grounding. To bridge this gap, we
introduce a three-stage training paradigm: (i) World Model Agentic Mid-Training (WM-AMT) to inject
latent predictive capabilities into the policy; (ii) Format-Eliciting SFT (FE-SFT) to structure
this injected capability; and (iii) Foresight-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (FC-RL) to refine
the calibration and utility of the generated simulations. Evaluated on search and mathematical
reasoning tasks, our approach consistently outperforms other training baselines. Our results
demonstrate that effective internal world modeling in LLM agents requires a capability-first
training pipeline to achieve grounded and calibrated foresight.

1 Introduction

Refer to caption
Figure 1: Overview of our key design of an agent policy with internalized world model.

LLM agents have become strong controllers for multi-step tasks given their comprehensive
pre-trained semantic knowledge and remarkable capabilities in sequential decision-making [45, 42].
However, the prevailing paradigm in long-horizon settings remains fundamentally reactive, yet human
planning is rarely reactive. Before acting, people often ask a what-if question: if I follow this
plan, what is likely to happen next, and how likely is it to succeed? This style of reasoning is
central to intelligent behavior because it allows alternatives to be mentally compared before
commitment, as shown in Figure 1. In Craik’s classic formulation, effective intelligence depends on
carrying a “small-scale model” of external reality that lets possible actions be tried out in
thought before they are executed [5]. Inspired by this pattern of planning, we do not claim full
counterfactual reasoning; rather, we target a plan-conditioned form of future simulation and
evaluation within a language agent.

While standard autoregressive reasoning methods such as Chain-of-Thought [48] mainly improve
decomposition of the current problem, the ability that we aim to inject is closely related to an
internal world model [43, 16]. Instead of utilizing separate modules to serve as external world
models [3, 47, 15], our key claim is that, for language agents, this kind of future-aware planning
signal should be internalized as part of the policy itself. Moreover, unlike Dreamer-style
model-based RL [17, 18, 19], our goal is not to introduce a separate simulator or a dedicated value
head. Instead, we train a single autoregressive model not only to serve as a reasoning agent, but
also to verbalize both: (i) a compact prospective rollout of what may happen next, and (ii) a
plan-conditioned estimate of success if the current plan is followed. This textual estimate is best
understood as a verbalized analogue of Q⁡(s,a)Q(s,a) or local action value, represented in the same
token space as the policy rather than as a claim of full what-if reasoning. Unlike other methods
that only train agents to predict future states or transition dynamics [4, 8, 52], our design of
internal world modeling is more comprehensive and innovative. Crucially, our verbalized QQ-values
are designed to be continuously updated and corrected by actual environmental feedback obtained
from real execution during RL training, which is also distinct from previous works [20].

Such explicit verbalization offers a transparent reasoning signal, allowing the agent to
effectively prune suboptimal paths through implicit deliberation. To inject this capability, a
natural idea is to leverage the inherent world modeling potential of LLMs [28] by training the
agent to imagine the future, evaluate it, and then act. However, our preliminary experiments show
that this strategy fails when applied only during post-training. With SFT and RL on formatted
look-ahead traces, the agent learns the surface form of a simulation but not the predictive
competence behind it. Experimental results for SFT and RL are shown in Table 7, and an example is
shown in Appendix E.1. We call this failure mode the format-capability gap: the agent can produce a
plausible-looking foresight block while the content remains weakly grounded, miscalibrated, or
outright misleading, thus introducing noise rather than useful guidance.

This motivates us to propose a unified three-stage training paradigm: World Model Agentic
Mid-Training (WM-AMT), Format-Eliciting SFT (FE-SFT), and Foresight-Conditioned Reinforcement
Learning (FC-RL). WM-AMT injects the underlying predict-then-act capability by augmenting
large-scale agentic trajectories with future-aware experience summaries. FE-SFT then teaches the
model when and how to externalize this latent capability in a structured foresight block. FC-RL
finally improves both action quality and foresight quality by rewarding task success, grounded
anticipation, and calibrated confidence. All three stages are implemented within one causal
language model, with no additional value head.

We evaluate this training paradigm on two representative agentic domains: search and mathematical
reasoning. Across both settings, our method consistently outperforms other training baselines. The
results show that future-aware behavior in LLM agents is not well served by format supervision
alone; it benefits from a training pipeline that first builds the capability, then elicits it, and
finally optimizes it under reward.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

  • •
   
    We identify the format-capability gap: post-training on look-ahead formats alone teaches LLM
    agents to mimic the structure of foresight without reliably acquiring grounded future
    prediction.

  • •
   
    We propose a unified three-stage paradigm, consisting of WM-AMT, FE-SFT, and FC-RL, which
    internalizes world-model planning inside a single autoregressive policy. Our method verbalizes
    both future rollout and a plan-conditioned success estimate without introducing an explicit
    value head, and this verbalized Q-like signal is explicitly trained and refined throughout the
    pipeline.

  • •
   
    We show on search and mathematical reasoning that this capability-first pipeline yields
    consistent gains over other training baselines.

2 Preliminaries

2.1 World models in LLM agents

We formulate LLM agents’ decision-making as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) [2], denoted by ℳ=
⟨𝒮,𝒜,𝒫,ℛ,γ⟩\mathcal{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{R},\gamma\rangle. State
st∈𝒮s_{t}\in\mathcal{S} includes the initial instruction, historical trajectory, and observations.
Action at∈𝒜a_{t}\in\mathcal{A} represents discrete operations, e.g., text generation or tool
invocation. Transition dynamics 𝒫⁡(st+1|st,at)\mathcal{P}(s_{t+1}|s_{t},a_{t}) and reward ℛ⁡(st,at)
\mathcal{R}(s_{t},a_{t}) govern environmental feedback. The objective is to learn a parameterized
policy πθ​(at|st)\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}|s_{t}) that maximizes the expected return discounted by γ∈[0,1]
\gamma\in[0,1]. World models traditionally predict transition dynamics 𝒫^​(st+1|st,at)\hat{\mathcal
{P}}(s_{t+1}|s_{t},a_{t}) and rewards ℛ^​(st,at)\hat{\mathcal{R}}(s_{t},a_{t}) [43]. However,
applying token-level simulation to dense, low-level environmental feedback is computationally
intractable. Autoregressively predicting fine-grained future trajectories τf​u​t​u​r​e=
⟨st+1,at+1,ot+1,…,st+k⟩\tau_{future}=\langle s_{t+1},a_{t+1},o_{t+1},\dots,s_{t+k}\rangle may waste
computational capacity on syntax rather than core causal dynamics, thus causing severe compounding
errors [27]. Thus, we reconceptualize the World Model for LLM agents as an internal semantic
simulator. It generates a condensed experience summary of anticipated consequences, defined as z^t=
Ψ⁡(τf​u​t​u​r​e)\hat{z}_{t}=\Psi(\tau_{future}). Here, Ψ\Psi is a semantic compression function
mapping raw rollouts into high-level predictive foresight. This empowers the agent to perform 
look-ahead planning, transitioning from a reactive policy πθ​(at|st)\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}|s_{t}) to a
foresight-conditioned policy πθ​(at|st,z^t)\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}|s_{t},\hat{z}_{t}). Ultimately, this
enables the agent to evaluate consequences and prune suboptimal trajectories in the latent space
before executing real-world actions.

2.2 Mid-training in large language models

To obtain strong initial policies in terms of both performance and exploration diversity,
mid-training [32, 41] has become an essential step between general pre-training and post-training,
which aims to adapt the LLM’s underlying data distribution toward the target domain. Recent
theoretical analyses demonstrate that an effective mid-training stage not only establishes strong
policy priors by efficiently pruning the decision space, but also allows the subsequent RL phase to
effectively jump across multiple steps during Bellman backups, thus accelerating convergence speed
and minimizing post-training regret [53]. By immersing the LLM in high-density, high-quality
specialized sequences, mid-training provides a latent semantic foundation and contextually grounded
initialization for post-training.

2.3 Group-based RL

Unlike traditional Actor-Critic methods (e.g., PPO [38]) that rely on a parameterized value network
Vϕ​(s)V_{\phi}(s) to estimate advantages, group-based RL approaches [39, 51, 11] leverage the
statistical distribution of rewards within a sampled batch to approximate the baseline. For a given
task prompt xx, the agent samples a group of GG trajectories {τ1,τ2,…,τG}\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\dots,
\tau_{G}\} from the current policy πθold\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}. Each trajectory τi\tau_{i}
represents a full episode of interactions and is assigned a scalar reward R⁡(τi)R(\tau_{i})
reflecting the task success. The advantage estimation A⁡(τi)A(\tau_{i}) is derived from the
normalized reward in each group: A⁡(τi)=R⁡(τi)−μ⁡({R⁡(τj)}j=1G)σ⁡({R⁡(τj)}j=1G)A(\tau_{i})=\frac{R
(\tau_{i})-\mu(\{R(\tau_{j})\}_{j=1}^{G})}{\sigma(\{R(\tau_{j})\}_{j=1}^{G})}, where μ\mu and σ\
sigma denote the mean and standard deviation of rewards in the group, respectively.

3 Methodology

3.1 The format-capability gap in post-training

A natural idea to internalize world model into the LLM policy is training the LLM with SFT data
that explicitly includes anticipated futures, and then followed by RL. However, our preliminary
experiments on the model mid-trained with routine agentic trajectories (see Appendix D.1 and E.1)
reveal a format-capability gap. While the agent learns the format of look-ahead reasoning, it fails
to simulate accurate future dynamics, often resulting in hallucinations. We attribute it to the
inherent nature of post-training: while SFT and RL excel at eliciting latent capabilities, they
struggle to inject fundamentally new ones. Without a prior understanding of future consequences,
enforcing a look-ahead format merely triggers hallucinated pattern matching rather than genuine
future simulation. It motivates us to intervene earlier in the training pipeline via mid-training
to construct a foundational data distribution that intrinsically models the future. We propose a
three-stage training paradigm to close the format-capability gap, as shown in Figure 2. Given an
LLM policy, our goal is not only to produce a well-formed look-ahead trace, but also to internalize
transition-aware foresight that improves policy capability. The paradigm consists of: (i) World
Model Agentic Mid-Training (WM-AMT) for policy prior formation, (ii) Format-Eliciting Supervised
Fine-Tuning (FE-SFT) for structured elicitation, and (iii) Foresight-Conditioned Reinforcement
Learning (FC-RL) for reward-aligned policy optimization.

3.2 World model agentic mid-training

Standard pre-training and agentic SFT trajectory data primarily consist of observational
trajectories. While this paradigm trains the model to predict the next action given the historical
context, we aim to instill look-ahead capabilities in LLM agents via WM-AMT (Algorithm 1). Let 𝒟raw
={τi}i=1N\mathcal{D}_{\text{raw}}=\{\tau_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N} denote a large-scale dataset of agentic
trajectories, where each trajectory is defined as a sequence of state-action pairs: τ=
(s1,a1,s2,a2,…,sT,aT)\tau=(s_{1},a_{1},s_{2},a_{2},\dots,s_{T},a_{T}). Instead of relying on
human-annotated chain-of-thought data, we systematically augment 𝒟raw\mathcal{D}_{\text{raw}}
through a trajectory summarization mapping.

Formally, for a given trajectory τ\tau of length TT, we sample a set of indices ℐ∼𝒰⁡(1,T−1)\mathcal
{I}\sim\mathcal{U}(1,T-1). For each step t∈ℐt\in\mathcal{I}, we construct a structured world model
block ztz_{t}. We define a summarization function Φ⁡(⋅)\Phi(\cdot), which maps the current
observation sts_{t} and the future trajectory τ>t\tau_{>t} into a composite textual representation:
zt=[zt(traj)⊕zt(eval)]z_{t}=[z_{t}^{(\text{traj})}\oplus z_{t}^{(\text{eval})}]. Here, zt(traj)z_
{t}^{(\text{traj})} serves as an abstracted roadmap of the simulated future. zt(eval)z_{t}^{(\text

Monte Carlo rollouts, it is computationally prohibitive given the massive scale of mid-training
data. Consequently, we adopt an LLM to heuristically estimate this success probability. Though this
estimation may be not accurate enough, it is acceptable because these confidence priors are
ultimately grounded and calibrated against true execution outcomes during subsequent RL training.

prediction and value evaluation capabilities.

3.3 Format-eliciting supervised fine-tuning

While WM-AMT successfully injects latent look-ahead capabilities into LLM agents, the base policy
lacks the structural schema to autonomously and reliably invoke this predictive capability during
inference. The FE-SFT stage bridges this gap by aligning the generated trajectory with a
predefined, explicit look-ahead reasoning structure. We curate a filtered task-specific
instruction-tuning dataset 𝒟FE-SFT\mathcal{D}_{\text{FE-SFT}}. Given a current observation sts_{t},
the policy is trained to project its internal world model into a textual representation of
anticipated future states and estimated QQ-values, i.e., world model block z^t\hat{z}_{t}. While
adopting a similar generation pipeline for these instances, FE-SFT strictly encloses z^t\hat{z}_{t}
within distinct structural delimiters. Following z^t\hat{z}_{t}, the policy generates the
corresponding executable action ata_{t}. Since WM-AMT has established a robust causal prior via a
large-scale general agentic trajectories, FE-SFT avoids the hallucinations typical of world
modeling from scratch. We also use the standard autoregressive negative log-likelihood objective to
optimize the agent on 𝒟FE-SFT\mathcal{D}_{\text{FE-SFT}}.

The policy may still suffer from compounding errors during long-horizon inference or produce

4 Related Work

4.1 World models in autonomous agents

World models are core to model-based RL, where agents learn transition and reward for look-ahead
control [43, 33]. Classical lines focus on latent-dynamics learning in visually observed
environments [16, 17, 18, 19] and more recently, embodied agent for future action-conditioned
simulation [12]. It enables models to improve sample efficiency in a latent space or simulate
outcomes without real-world interaction. The idea has shifted from pixel-space simulation to
language-mediated future prediction and planning due to the rise of LLMs [20, 3]; it also extends
to code-generation settings, where world modeling is used to study long-horizon program synthesis
and repair [8]. Despite these advancements, most existing systems treat the world model as an
explicit simulator or external module [47, 15]. For example, WebDreamer [15] and WebEvolver [9]
construct explicit world models to simulate the environmental feedback, which improve deliberation
quality but may introduce additional inference overhead and possible simulator-policy mismatch. In
parallel, recent implicit world modeling strategies suggest that early experiences can improve
downstream agent behavior without relying on another simulator [4, 52]. Extending this implicit
world modeling line, our work targets a unified agentic training pipeline for world model planning.

4.2 Agentic data synthesis and mid-training

The scarcity of expert-level demonstrations has motivated the synthetic data for training LLM
agents [52]. Early imitation-based distillation from stronger teachers [34, 7] aids initial
alignment but suffers from limited diversity and weak on-policy coverage. Subsequent works, such as
AgentSynth [49] and SCA [54], broaden task synthesis to expand the exploration space for agentic
RL. Another direction shifts from trajectory synthesis to environment synthesis, e.g., classical
world-model methods in Section 4.1, which uses learned environments to generate scalable
interaction data. However, translating these synthetic interactions into reusable decision-making
capabilities necessitates a dedicated training phase. Consequently, mid-training [32] is
increasingly adopted to bridge general pre-training and task-level alignment [41, 53]. Unlike SFT,
mid-training scales to vastly larger, diverse corpora of expert rollouts, such as human
demonstrations in device control, tool-use records and code writing [37, 22, 1]. Our perspective is
to synthesize agentic data with compressed future experience summary, and internalize a robust 
predict-then-act policy prior via mid-training.

5 Experiments

5.1 Experiment setup

We select an intermediate checkpoint of Youtu-LLM-2B [29] as our foundation model, and refer to it
as Youtu-LLM-2B-Init. This selection is motivated by two practical considerations: (1) the
Youtu-LLM family inherently possesses strong native agentic capabilities; (2) a lightweight 2B
scale makes our extensive mid-training computationally feasible. To validate the effectiveness of
our method, we initialize from the Youtu-LLM-2B-Init immediately following its general pre-training
and compare two mid-training variants: (1) Youtu-LLM-2B-Base is trained with standard agentic
mid-training on 200B tokens of high-quality agentic trajectory data without changing its structure;
(2) Youtu-LLM-2B-Base (WM-AMT, ours) augments the same agentic trajectory data by inserting an
explicit world model block into a random split of multi-turn interaction trajectories, which
provides supervised signals for future-state prediction. We conduct experiments on two
representative agentic tasks: search and mathematical reasoning.

Search

Following Jin et al. [23], we evaluate our training paradigm on 7 search-augmented QA tasks,
including single-hop QA datasets (NQ [26], TriviaQA [24], and PopQA [31]) and multi-hop QA datasets
(HotpotQA [50], 2Wiki [21], MuSiQue [44], and Bamboogle [35]). For retrieval, we use the 2018
Wikipedia dump [25] as the knowledge source and E5 [46] as the retriever, and set the number of
retrieved passages to 3 across all retrieval-based methods. During evaluation, the results are
obtained via greedy sampling, and the metrics are computed via LLM-as-a-judge using DeepSeek V3.1 [
6].

Mathematical reasoning

Following Feng et al. [10], we evaluate our paradigm on AIME2024/2025/2026 [30]. We repeat the
evaluation set AIME2024&2025&2026 30 times and report both mean@3030 and pass@3030. The evaluation
hyperparameters of evaluation are set to temperature 1.0 and top-p 0.6.

Baselines and configurations

(1) Post-Training Only*: Our preliminary experiments are conducted directly on Youtu-LLM-2B-Base,
serving as a vanilla baseline that only applies post-training without any world-model-augmented
mid-training. (2) State-Only Prediction (Post-Training Only)†\dagger: The agent is trained only via
post-training to predict future states but omits the verbalized QQ-values. This setup mirrors other
methods of predicting only state transitions [4]. (3) State-Only Prediction (With Mid-Training)‡\

5.2 Main results

Necessity of our three phases.

(1) WM-AMT: At the SFT stage, replacing standard agentic mid-training with our WM-AMT improves the
average score under the same SFT method (e.g., in Table 1, 38.2 →\rightarrow 39.9 for SFT and 37.4
→\rightarrow 41.8 for FE-SFT). We provide a case study in Appendix E.1: under the same FE-SFT
format, our WM-AMT model generates more coherent, causally accurate world model blocks, while the
base model fails. These results indicate that FE-SFT is substantially effective when the model has
internalized future-dynamics priors via WM-AMT. (2) FE-SFT: Under our WM-AMT model, our FE-SFT
outperforms standard SFT (e.g. 39.9 →\rightarrow 41.8 in Table 1), showing that explicitly
eliciting structured foresight is beneficial after the world-model capability is established. (3)
FC-RL: In RL-stage comparisons, our FE-SFT & FC-RL achieves the best overall average (50.6 in Table
1, mean@30: 29.5 and pass@30: 60.0 in Table 2), outperforming all the other baselines.

Stage dependency.

The overall results exhibit a progressive dependency: WM-AMT provides a capability foundation,
FE-SFT activates this capability in a structured format, and FC-RL optimizes end-task behavior with
grounded foresight. Removing or replacing any stage weakens the final performance, confirming the
need for the full pipeline.

Comparison with other world modeling methods.

In Table 1, the highest average score of state-only baselines is 48.7; while in Table 2, it
achieves a pass@30 of 56.7 and a mean@30 of 27.7. By adding the verbalized QQ-value and applying
our FC-RL to jointly optimize the policy and its internalized world model, our final model (WM-AMT
& FE-SFT & FC-RL) increases these scores to 50.6 in Table 1, 60.0 (pass@30) and 29.5 (mean@30) in
Table 2, respectively. It proves that simply predicting future states is not enough for world
modeling. Instead, explicitly adding the confidence estimation and utilizing the calibrated
confidence to guide the policy are crucial for making better decisions. Notably, IWM (+RL) achieves
strong performance on Youtu-LLM-2B-Base, but our final model still shows a significant and
consistent improvement.

Efficacy on complex multi-hop reasoning.

Another notable fact is that our proposed paradigm yields particularly pronounced improvements on
complex, multi-step reasoning tasks in Table 1. Up to the SFT stage, the transition from SFT to 
FE-SFT under our WM-AMT model brings a significant improvement (MuSiQue: 20.6 →\rightarrow 25.6,
2WikiMultiHopQA: 31.2 →\rightarrow 35.8). Our final model also demonstrates substantial superiority
on multi-hop datasets compared to the other baselines, e.g., HotpotQA (59.0), MuSiQue (34.2) and
Bamboogle (56.0). It indicates that the internalized world model and foresight-conditioned planning
are especially critical for tasks requiring long-horizon reasoning, whereas simple factual
retrieval (single-hop) relies less heavily on future simulation.

Qualitative analysis of world model blocks.

We provide examples in Appendix E.2 and E.3 to analyze the world model blocks. Two key capabilities
emerge: (1) Long-horizon planning coherence: these blocks establish reasoning frameworks that
persist across multiple turns, even when intermediate errors require correction; and (2) Calibrated
confidence quantification: confidence estimates correlate meaningfully with the actual task
success, decreasing upon error detection and remaining low for inherently challenging problems.

6 Conclusion

We address the limitations of reactive LLM agents by internalizing future-aware planning into the
policy. We identify the format-capability gap, wherein post-training on look-ahead formats merely
teaches structural mimicry instead of grounded prediction. To bridge this gap, we propose a unified
three-stage training paradigm consisting of WM-AMT, FE-SFT and FC-RL. Evaluations across search and
mathematical reasoning show the superiority of our approach over other training baselines. Our
results underscore that effective internal world modeling in LLM agents demands a capability-first
paradigm to achieve truly grounded and calibrated foresight.

B.1 World model agentic mid-training

To construct the structured world model Block ztz_{t} during the World Model Agentic Mid-Training
(WM-AMT) phase, we employ an LLM acting as the summarization function Φ⁡(⋅)\Phi(\cdot). The prompt
enforces abstract planning to prevent future-state data leakage and explicitly requires confidence
estimation for QQ-value modeling. The exact prompt template is provided below:

Prompt Template for Summarization (Φ\Phi)

Act as an agent’s "World Model". Based on the **FUTURE GROUND TRUTH** (the
verified success path), generate the internal reasoning.

**CRITICAL RULES:**
1. **NO SPOILERS:** Plan the future steps using *abstract placeholders* (e.g.,
   "expect to find a candidate name"), NOT specific entities found in the future.
2. **BE SPECIFIC:** In the Action Plan, specify exact keywords/intents,
   avoiding generic phrases like "Proceed".

**OUTPUT FORMAT (Markdown Only):**

## World Model Simulation
- **Trajectory:** Step-by-step roadmap to the answer based on the Future Truth.
- **Gap:** Why the current state is insufficient.
- **Confidence:** [0-100]%. (High=Direct path, Low=Complex/Trial-error).
  Brief reason.

## Immediate Action Plan
- The precise intent for the *immediate next action* in the
  Ground Truth.

**DATA:**
[Current Obs]:
{current_obs}
[FUTURE TRUTH]:
{future_traj}

We leverage DeepSeek V3.1 [6] as the foundational data generator for World Model Agentic
Mid-Training. The model is queried using the above prompt, with generation parameters set to a
temperature of 0.6, a top-p of 0.95, and a maximum response length of 32768 tokens.

The original agentic trajectory data consists of 200B tokens of high-quality trajectory data,
including 25B Agentic-CoT trajectories, 20B mathematical trajectories, 70B code execution
trajectories, 60B Deep Research trajectories, and 25B other trajectories (e.g., tool using,
function calling, and planning).

In WM-AMT, we augment the original agentic trajectory data by inserting an explicit world model
block into a random split of multi-turn interaction trajectories, which are primarily concentrated
in Deep Research trajectories and mathematical trajectories. After our data augmentation, we
obtained roughly ∼62\sim 62B tokens for Deep Research trajectories and ∼21\sim 21B for mathematics,
whereas the remaining trajectory categories remained unaltered.

C.3.2 Outcome reward function

Search

We adopt an LLM-as-a-judge outcome reward as follows:

 R⁡(a,a^)={1,if exact_match​(a,a^)LLM_judge​(a,a^),otherwise\small R(a,\hat{a})=\begin{cases}1,&\
 text{if exact\_match}(a,\hat{a})\\ \text{LLM\_judge}(a,\hat{a}),&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

where aa and a^\hat{a} represent the ground-truth answer and the predicted answer, respectively. A
reward of 1 is assigned directly for an Exact Match (EM), while in other cases, an LLM-as-a-judge
(DeepSeek-V3.1 in our experiments) is employed to evaluate the response and determine the reward,
such that LLM_judge​(a,a^)∈{0,1}\text{LLM\_judge}(a,\hat{a})\in\{0,1\}.

The prompt for LLM-as-a-judge is as follows:

Labeled Answer: {gt_answer}

Predicted Answer: {pred_answer}

respond with "Correct" if they are equivalent, or "Incorrect" if they are not
equivalent.

The output should in the following json format:
"‘json
{{
    "judgement": your judgement result, can only be "Correct" or "Incorrect",
}}

The generation parameters are set to a temperature of 0.6, a top-p of 0.95, and a maximum response
length of 1024 tokens.

Particularly, invalid actions are penalized with a reward of -0.01.

Mathematical reasoning

We adopt a composite outcome reward function that evaluates both the final answer correctness and
the agent’s intermediate tool utilization. We require the model to present final answers in a
specified format (e.g., within \boxed{}), enabling reliable rule-based correctness verification.
The reward is formulated as:

 Rbase​(a,a^)={1,is_equivalent​(a,a^)−1,otherwiseR_{\text{base}}(a,\hat{a})=\begin{cases}1,&\text
 {is\_equivalent}(a,\hat{a})\\ -1,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

where aa and a^\hat{a} represent the ground-truth answer and the predicted answer, respectively.

To encourage the model to engage in multi-turn reasoning and actively call tools, we also introduce
a tool utilization reward, denoted as RtoolR_{\text{tool}}, which scales linearly with the number
of conversation turns NN:

 Rtool=0.05×(N−2)R_{\text{tool}}=0.05\times(N-2)

The final reward SfinalS_{\text{final}} integrates the base score and the tool reward through a
conditional aggregation strategy to prevent reward hacking:

 Sfinal={min⁡(0,Sbase+Rtool),if ​Sbase<0Sbase+𝕀⁡(N>2),otherwiseS_{\text{final}}=\begin{cases}\min
 (0,S_{\text{base}}+R_{\text{tool}}),&\text{if }S_{\text{base}}<0\\ S_{\text{base}}+\mathbb{I}(N>
 2),&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

where 𝕀⁡(⋅)\mathbb{I}(\cdot) is an indicator function that yields 11 if the condition is met and 00
otherwise.

To address the concern regarding whether success-driven RL alone is sufficient, we introduce and
evaluate the RL only baseline across both the standard base model and our mid-trained architecture.
As summarized in Table 12, directly applying RL (GRPO) optimization without a preparatory SFT phase
results in severe performance degradation in the search task.

Crucially, our results in Figure 3 reveals a severe optimization pathology during the RL-only
training loop on tool-integrated search tasks. Without a preliminary SFT phase to anchor the
structural tool-calling syntax and reasoning schema, the model suffers from an alignment collapse
in the later stages of training. Specifically, the agent completely forgets how to interact with
external search tools. Driven by the sparse success rewards, the policy prematurely converges on a
degenerative local optimum: it collapses into a naive single-turn generation format, directly
outputing what it speculates to be the answer while entirely bypassing the necessary multi-hop tool
invocation sequences. This finding firmly demonstrates that success-driven RL alone cannot robustly
bootstrap complex, structured action sequences from scratch. Consequently, a warm-start SFT phase
(such as standard SFT or our proposed FE-SFT) is crucial to establish a s structural prior before
exposing the agent to RL.
