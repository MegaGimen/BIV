Theoretical Foundations and Effective Algorithms
for Policy-Aware Simulator Learning

Abstract

Model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL) agents typically learn world models by minimizing
predictive loss. However, powerful RL optimizers inevitably exploit minor model inaccuracies,
leading to “simulator exploitation” and a reality gap where policies succeed in simulation but fail
in the real world.

We propose that the objective for learning simulators should be strategic robustness rather than
predictive accuracy, and formulate this as a zero-sum minimax game between a model player and an
adversarial policy player. We provide a comprehensive theoretical analysis: (1) an online learning
guarantee showing the game is learnable with sublinear regret bounds; (2) a tractable critic-based
simplification bounding the global policy-value gap by the local critic’s loss; and (3) an
Error-MDP duality, proving that finding the worst-case policy is formally dual to a standard RL
problem where the reward is the one-step critic error. This duality yields a provably convergent
active data selection algorithm. Experiments on continuous control tasks demonstrate that our
approach reduces prediction error in strategically important regions by 1.51.5–2.2×2.2\times and
enables policies trained purely in simulation to match near-optimal real-world performance.

1 Introduction

Model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL) represents a promising path toward agents with high
sample efficiency, strong planning capabilities, and the ability to test policies in a safe,
offline environment (Sutton, 1990; Hafner et al., 2020). In this paradigm, an agent first learns a
simulator, or “world model” (M^\widehat{M}), from data collected in the real world (MM). Second,
the agent trains its policy (π\pi) entirely within this learned simulator, using it as a fast and
safe sandbox to find an optimal strategy.

The critical flaw in this decoupled approach is the inevitable discrepancy between the learned
model and the true environment. No matter how much data is collected or how large the model, the
simulator is never a perfect copy of reality. This discrepancy leads to a well-known failure mode:
the reinforcement learning algorithm, as a powerful optimizer, will inevitably discover and exploit
the tiniest inaccuracies in the simulator’s physics or rules to achieve an artificially high score
(Chua et al., 2018). This phenomenon, often called the reality gap or simulator exploitation,
results in policies that are brilliant in simulation but fail catastrophically when deployed in the
real world.

The standard approach to simulator learning is to minimize a one-step predictive loss, such as the
Mean Squared Error (MSE) or the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence via Maximum Likelihood Estimation
(MLE). This objective, however, is fundamentally misaligned with the agent’s downstream task. MLE
optimizes for average predictive accuracy across all visited states, whereas the RL agent’s
performance is determined by the worst-case, exploitable error along the specific trajectories it
discovers. A model can have 99% predictive accuracy on average but fail on a single, crucial
transition that an adversary can learn to visit repeatedly.

Existing work has proposed several ways to mitigate this. One popular method is to learn an
ensemble of models to quantify uncertainty (Chua et al., 2018) and train a policy that is robust to
this distribution. Another line of work, particularly in offline RL, introduces pessimism or
conservatism, penalizing the agent for actions that deviate from the known data (Yu et al., 2020;
Kidambi et al., 2020). A third category uses robust control formulations, but these often require a
pre-defined and known ”uncertainty set” for the dynamics (Nilim and El Ghaoui, 2005; Wiesemann et
al., 2013), which is unavailable when the model is learned.

More recently, adversarial methods have been explored. Some works introduce adversarial
disturbances to the state (Pinto et al., 2017) rather than the model itself. Others use Generative
Adversarial Networks (GANs) to train the simulator, using a critic to enforce that one-step
transitions are ”realistic” (Volodin et al., 2023). While promising, this still optimizes a local,
one-step metric of realism. It fails to address the global, policy-level objective of strategic
robustness. The critic does not ask, Is this transition plausible?; it should ask, Can a policy
exploit this transition to create an unrealistic, high-value trajectory?

To bridge this gap from local plausibility to global strategic robustness, we argue for a new
objective. We reframe simulator learning not as a prediction task, but as a two-player, zero-sum
game between a Model Player (the simulator) and a Policy Player (the adversary). The Policy
Player’s goal is to find the policy π\pi that reveals the largest discrepancy between the true
value in the real world Vπ​(M)V_{\pi}(M) and the predicted value in the simulator Vπ​(M^)V_{\pi}(\
widehat{M}). The Model Player’s goal is to update its parameters to minimize this worst-case
discrepancy.

This leads to our central minimax objective:

 minM^∈ℳ⁡maxπ∈Π​|Vπ​(M)−Vπ​(M^)|\displaystyle\min_{\widehat{M}\in\mathcal{M}}\max_{\pi\in\Pi}   (1)
 \,\left\lvert V_{\pi}(M)-V_{\pi}(\widehat{M})\right\rvert

By solving this game, we produce a simulator that is not just accurate on average, but is
specifically robust against being exploited by the very agent that will be trained within it. This
formulation also provides a principled theoretical foundation for related proxy hacking problems,
such as reward hacking in RLHF (Gupta et al., 2025; Christiano et al., 2017) and
out-of-distribution exploitation in offline RL (Levine et al., 2020). Our contributions are as
follows:

  • •
   
    A Policy-Aware Minimax Objective: We formulate robust simulator learning as a minimax game and
    connect it to a practical online learning framework. We provide a regret bound (Theorem 3)
    showing that if the model player achieves sublinear regret, its average value-gap against the
    adversary also vanishes.

  • •
   
    Tractable Critic-Based Guarantees: We show that the intractable policy-maximization maxπ\max_{\
    pi} in (1) can be relaxed to a tractable maxD\max_{D} over a critic function DD. We provide
    finite-time guarantees (Lemma 4) that bound the true policy-value gap by the loss of this
    critic-based game, scaled by a coverage constant κ\kappa.

  • •
   
    The Error-MDP Duality: We introduce our most significant theoretical novelty. We prove
    (Theorem 9) that the global problem of finding the worst-case policy π\pi is formally dual to a
    local RL problem: finding the optimal policy in an Error-MDP where the reward is precisely the
    one-step critic error.

  • •
   
    Provably Stable Active Learning: We resolve the circular dependency between learning a model
    and collecting its counter-examples. We formalize active data selection as an iterative game
    and prove its convergence to a saddle point (Theorem 11). This guarantees that our
    critic-guided sampling mechanism is not merely heuristic, but a theoretically stable method for
    minimizing the coverage constant κ\kappa.

  • •
   
    A General Framework for Proxy Hacking: We show this framework is a general solution for proxy
    hacking, with direct applications to solving reward hacking in RLHF and OOD-exploitation in
    Offline RL (Section 8).

Related Work.

Our approach intersects with several key areas. Uncertainty-aware MBRL relies on ensembles or
pessimism to mitigate model error, whereas we actively seek and correct strategic flaws.
Adversarial RL typically perturbs dynamics to robustify policies; in contrast, we perturb policies
to robustify models. Finally, Value-Aware Model Learning weights errors by value gradients for a
fixed policy, while our minimax formulation actively anticipates the worst-case policy shift. We
provide an extended discussion in Appendix A.

Paper Organization.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 3 formalizes the landscape of simulator fidelity metrics
and justifies our choice. Section 4 presents our first main result, an online-learning guarantee
for the full minimax objective. Section 5 introduces our practical, critic-based simplification of
the game and provides robust value-gap guarantees. Section 6 presents our novel theoretical results
on active data selection, including the Error-MDP duality and convergence proofs. In Section 7, we
present an experimental validation of our theoretical results by examining a controlled 2D
scenario. Section 8 discusses the broader applications of our framework to other proxy-hacking
problems. Section 9 generalizes our framework to the joint learning of dynamics and rewards.

3 What Should a Good Simulator Match? Metrics and Desiderata

Before formalizing our minimax objective, we must first establish what it means for a simulator to
be good. In standard supervised learning, fidelity is measured by predictive accuracy on a fixed
dataset. In reinforcement learning, however, fidelity is measured by the success of policies
trained within the simulator when deployed in the real world. This distinction requires a careful
choice of metric.

For clarity, throughout the main analysis we identify the true world model MM with its transition
kernel 𝖯\mathsf{P}, and the simulator M^\widehat{M} with 𝖯^\widehat{\mathsf{P}}, treating the
reward function rr as known.

A (possibly randomized, stationary) policy π\pi induces a trajectory distribution over infinite
sequences τ=(s0,a0,s1,a1,…)\tau=(s_{0},a_{0},s_{1},a_{1},\ldots) under transition kernel 𝖯\mathsf
{P} and initial state law ρ0\rho_{0}; we write ℙ𝖯π(τ)\Pr_{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}(\tau) for this
distribution. We denote by P𝖯πP_{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi} the marginal distribution over finite trajectory
prefixes (e.g. length-HH segments). The discounted state-action occupancy for π\pi under 𝖯\mathsf
{P} is

 dπ𝖯(s,a)=𝔼[∑t=0∞γt𝟏{(st,at)=(s,a)}],d_{\pi}^{\mathsf{P}}(s,a)=\E\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^
 {t}\mathbf{1}\{(s_{t},a_{t})=(s,a)\}\right],

and the one-step conditional transition at (s,a)(s,a) is 𝖯(⋅∣s,a)\mathsf{P}(\cdot\mid s,a).

Below we consider several notions of simulator fidelity, ranging from the strongest
(trajectory-level) to the weakest (optimal-value only).

3.1 Trajectory or reward-free closeness

Definition 1.

We say 𝖯^\widehat{\mathsf{P}} is trajectory-close to 𝖯\mathsf{P} (for horizon HH) if some
divergence between their finite-horizon trajectory distributions is small:

 Dtraj(H)(𝖯,𝖯^)=supπ∈ΠD(P𝖯π(τ0:H−1),P𝖯^π(τ0:H−1)),D_{\mathrm{traj}}^{(H)}(\mathsf{P},\widehat{\
 mathsf{P}})=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}D\left(P_{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}(\tau_{0:H-1}),P_{\widehat{\mathsf{P}}}^{\
 pi}(\tau_{0:H-1})\right),

where DD is a divergence such as TV, KL, or Wasserstein.

This is a reward-free criterion: if trajectory distributions match, then all reward functions
measurable on trajectories, including state- or action-dependent ones, induce the same return
distributions. It is extremely strong: matching trajectory distributions implies matching all
marginals, including (s,a)(s,a) occupancies and one-step conditionals, but is infeasible for large
or continuous state spaces. For discounted infinite-horizon problems one can instead consider the
distribution over discounted trajectories. In practice, one truncates to large HH and weights by γt
\gamma^{t}. This metric corresponds to the ideal of a “Digital Twin”, that is a simulator that
captures the physics of the environment so indistinguishably that an outside observer cannot tell
the difference. While desirable, we argue that when the model class is misspecified (limited
capacity), striving for this physical perfection is inefficient compared to optimizing for
strategic equivalence.

Matching one-step conditionals for all (s,a)(s,a) implies trajectory closeness (via simulation

3.3 Policy-value matching

Definition.

Require that for every policy π\pi in some class Π\Pi, the following inequality holds:

 |Vπ𝖯−Vπ𝖯^|≤ϵ.\left\lvert V_{\pi}^{\mathsf{P}}-V_{\pi}^{\widehat{\mathsf{P}}}\right\rvert\leq\
 epsilon.

Variants include restricting Π\Pi to deterministic stationary, randomized stationary, or
near-optimal policies. The following is a straightforward result about deterministic versus
randomized policies.

Proposition 2.

If Vπ𝖯=Vπ𝖯^V_{\pi}^{\mathsf{P}}=V_{\pi}^{\widehat{\mathsf{P}}} for every deterministic stationary
policy π\pi, then equality also holds for all stationary randomized policies.

Proof.

A stationary randomized policy is a state-wise mixture of deterministic policies. The value VπV_{\
pi} is linear in the induced occupancy dπd_{\pi}, which itself depends linearly on the action
mixture. Equality on deterministic policies (the extreme points) thus extends by convexity to their
mixtures. ∎

Note that this argument applies to stationary state-wise randomization. For history-dependent
policies, the equivalence may fail, but the restriction suffices for most theoretical analyses.
This restriction simplifies the theoretical landscape significantly. For example, it allows us to
replace complex stochastic reward distributions with their constant expected values without loss of
generality, as the value function depends only on the expectation.

3.4 Optimal-policy-value matching

Definition.

Require that optimal values coincide (or are close):

 |V𝖯∗−V𝖯^∗|≤ϵ,V∗=supπVπ.\big|V_{\mathsf{P}}^{*}-V_{\widehat{\mathsf{P}}}^{*}\big|\leq\epsilon,\
 qquad V^{*}=\sup_{\pi}V_{\pi}.

This is the weakest requirement: useful for value estimation but insufficient for policy transfer.
Two MDPs may share the same optimal value yet have disjoint sets of near-optimal policies; hence
matching V∗V^{*} alone gives little guarantee of behavioral fidelity. For example, in a Multi-Armed
Bandit, this criterion only requires the optimal arm to have the correct expected value;
sub-optimal arms could be modeled with arbitrarily incorrect values. If the environment dynamics
shift slightly, a previously sub-optimal action might become optimal, but the model would provide
no useful guidance.

3.5 Implications and recommendations

The logical relationships between the criteria are straightforward: trajectory closeness
(Subsection 3.1) implies one-step matching (Subsection 3.2), which in turn implies policy-value
matching (Subsection 3.3), as follows from standard simulation lemmas. The converse directions
generally fail unless the dynamics satisfy additional structural constraints. Furthermore, equality
of values for all deterministic stationary policies already guarantees equality for all stationary
randomized policies, owing to linearity in state-action occupancies. By contrast, requiring only
that optimal values coincide, as in (Subsection 3.4), is far too weak to ensure the successful
transfer of policies across environments.

From both a theoretical and a practical perspective, the most promising compromise seems to be to
train simulators using occupancy-weighted, value-aware one-step losses. Specifically, we can seek
to minimize

 min𝖯^𝔼(s,a)∼μ[Done-step(𝖯(⋅∣s,a),𝖯^(⋅∣s,a))],\min_{\widehat{\mathsf{P}}}\E_{(s,a)\sim\mu}\left[D_
 {\text{one-step}}(\mathsf{P}(\cdot\mid s,a),\widehat{\mathsf{P}}(\cdot\mid s,a))\right],

where μ\mu is an occupancy measure concentrating on the regions of the state–action space visited
by policies of interest. In the theoretical analysis of Section 4, choosing Done-step=𝖣KLD_{\text
{one-step}}=\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{KL}} yields the occupancy-weighted KL divergence 𝔼dπ[𝖣KL(𝖯∥𝖯^)]\E_
{d_{\pi}}[\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathsf{P}\|\widehat{\mathsf{P}})], which not only provides an
upper bound on the policy value gap but also admits an interpretation as a regret-like objective in
an online-learning framework.

In continuous or high-dimensional state spaces, it is often preferable to replace the KL divergence
with weaker, geometry-aware distances such as the Wasserstein or Sinkhorn metrics. These respect
the underlying geometry of the state space, are more robust to estimation noise, and can be
combined with KL-based analysis to obtain hybrid surrogates that balance tractability and fidelity.

In practice, model learning should focus on the reachable region of the state space, that is, the
subset of states visited by near-optimal or adversarial policies. Matching trajectories globally is
typically infeasible and unnecessary; fidelity within this region is what ultimately determines
policy robustness and transfer performance.

Finally, of course, empirical evaluations could report several complementary metrics, including the
occupancy-weighted one-step KL or TV divergence, short-horizon trajectory Wasserstein distance, and
the worst-case value-gap over a held-out policy set. Each captures different aspects of simulator
fidelity and exposes distinct failure modes.

Overall, the most useful and theoretically grounded criterion for decision-making seems to be an
occupancy-weighted, value-aware one-step divergence, typically the expected KL divergence under
relevant occupancies, or a Wasserstein analogue for continuous spaces. This criterion is weaker,
and therefore more practical, than full trajectory matching, yet substantially stronger and more
decision-relevant than aligning only the optimal values. With these metrics in hand, we now
formalize the minimax game that optimizes for strategic robustness.

Example: case of minimal MDPs. Consider a true environment 𝖬\mathsf{M} with high-entropy,
irrelevant noise (e.g., a TV playing static in the background) and its minimal equivalent 𝖬′\mathsf
{M}^{\prime} (a bisimulation that ignores the static). Under a reward-free metric like trajectory
KL (Subsection 3.1), 𝖬′\mathsf{M}^{\prime} is a poor simulator because it fails to model the
complex noise of 𝖬\mathsf{M}. But, under our strategic objective (Subsection 3.3), 𝖬′\mathsf{M}^{\
prime} is a perfect simulator, as Vπ​(𝖬)=Vπ​(𝖬′)V_{\pi}(\mathsf{M})=V_{\pi}(\mathsf{M}^{\prime})
for all π\pi. This illustrates why predictive accuracy (modeling the noise) is often misaligned
with robust control. Our framework favors 𝖬′\mathsf{M}^{\prime}, dedicating capacity only to
dynamics that affect the value function.

A concrete example is a Block MDP, where the control problem can be solved entirely in the latent
space. A reward-free metric might waste capacity modeling the complex, high-dimensional mapping
from latents to observations, whereas our strategic objective focuses solely on the latent dynamics
required to estimate value.

The theorem says that a model-player running any no-regret online algorithm (e.g. Online Gradient
Descent or Mirror Descent in the conditional-distribution space) guarantees that the average
value-gap on the sequence of adversarial policies is controlled by two terms: (i) the approximation
term, which is the best average occupancy-weighted KL that any model in ℳ\mathscr{M} can achieve on
the adversarial occupancies {πt}\{\pi_{t}\}, and (ii) the model-player’s regret per round. If an
adversary concentrates on the worst-case policies and the model class ℳ\mathscr{M} is rich enough,
the average gap approaches the minimax value-gap.

The statement above assumes access to the exact occupancy-weighted expected negative
log-likelihoods ℒπt​(𝖯^)\mathcal{L}_{\pi_{t}}(\widehat{\mathsf{P}}). In practice these must be
estimated from a finite number of samples, mm (either by rolling out πt\pi_{t} in the real world or
by using an off-policy estimator from a dataset). This finite-sample estimation of the loss
introduces an additional error term, often on the order of O⁡(1/m)O(1/\sqrt{m}), which must be
incorporated into the analysis.

We note that standard MLE-based approaches suffer a slower O(T−1/4)O(T^{-1/4}) convergence rate for
control due to the geometry of KL divergence (via Schützenberger-Pinsker), while our minimax
formulation targets the optimal O(T−1/2)O(T^{-1/2}) rate directly. We provide a rigorous proof and
discussion of this lower bound in Appendix B.2.

5 Game Formulation using Upper-Bounds

Since learning a simulator by maximum likelihood estimation is standard, it may appear natural to
approximate the objective in (1) using negative log likelihood losses. This indeed yields a valid
approach which we discussed and analyzed in the previous section. While this gives strong
guarantees (see Theorem 3), it involves a maximization over the entire policy space, which can be
computationally expensive. Moreover, evaluating the negative log likelihood loss requires knowledge
of the true dynamics 𝖯\mathsf{P} to compute the KL divergence. We here instead present a tractable
simplification. We replace the intractable maxπ\max_{\pi} player with a feasible maxD\max_{D}
player, a critic, and substitute the KL loss with a dual-divergence loss that can be estimated
directly from samples.

The difference in policy values under the true and learned dynamics is upper-bounded by the
worst-case single-step transition error, measured by the Total Variation (TV) distance:

 maxπ∈Π⁡|Vπ​(𝖯)−Vπ​(𝖯^)|≤Rmax(1−γ)2​supx=(s,a)TV(𝖯⁡(x),𝖯^​(x)),\displaystyle\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\left\
 lvert V_{\pi}(\mathsf{P})-V_{\pi}(\widehat{\mathsf{P}})\right\rvert\leq\frac{R_{\max}}{(1-\gamma)
 ^{2}}\sup_{x=(s,a)}\TV\bigl(\mathsf{P}(x),\widehat{\mathsf{P}}(x)\bigr),

where 𝖯⁡(x)=𝖯⁡(s,a)\mathsf{P}(x)=\mathsf{P}(s,a) is short-hand for 𝖯(⋅|s,a)\mathsf{P}(\cdot|s,a).
This motivates a simplified, more conservative game: instead of defending against the worst-case
policy, the simulator defends against the worst-case local error in predicting next-state
transitions. The TV distance admits the dual characterization

 TV(𝖯,𝖰)=12​sup‖D‖∞≤1{𝔼x∼𝖯[D⁡(x)]−𝔼x∼𝖰[D⁡(x)]},\TV(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Q})=\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\|D\|_
 {\infty}\leq 1}\Big\{\E_{x\sim\mathsf{P}}[D(x)]-\E_{x\sim\mathsf{Q}}[D(x)]\Big\},

where absolute values are unnecessary, since the maximization allows the critic DD to effectively
learn the sign of the difference 𝖯−𝖰\mathsf{P}-\mathsf{Q}. This dual form naturally suggests an
adversarial training setup: replace the intractable supremum over (s,a)(s,a) with an expectation
under a data distribution ddata​(s,a)d_{\text{data}}(s,a), and represent the supremum over bounded
DD by an adversarial critic D∈𝒟D\in\mathcal{D} whose output lies in [−1,1][-1,1]. This leads to the
following practical two-player game:

 min𝖯^∈ℳ⁡maxD∈𝒟​𝔼(s,a)∼ddata[ΔD,𝖯^​(s,a)] where ΔD,𝖯^​(s,a)=𝔼s′∼𝖯[D⁡(s,a,s′)]−𝔼s′∼𝖯^[D⁡(s,a,s′)].\

To make the game tractable, we replace the policy maximization with a critic maximization using the
dual representations of Total Variation (TV) or Wasserstein distances. This matches the definition
of witnessed model misfit in Sun et al. (2019). The result is a GAN-like objective where the critic
distinguishes real transitions from simulated ones, and the model minimizes this distinction.

The reformulation yields a concrete, trainable game that upper-bounds the original objective from (
1). However, the simplification incurs a key trade-off: replacing the policy adversary with a
critic focused on local discrepancies removes the strategic, policy-aware aspect of the problem.
The resulting simulator is encouraged to be accurate everywhere under ddatad_{\text{data}}, rather
than focusing capacity on regions that policy optimization would actually visit or exploit. While
substantially more tractable, this objective can therefore be overly conservative and less
sample-efficient. Nonetheless, under a mild coverage assumption relating ddatad_{\text{data}} to
the occupancies of relevant policies, we can recover guarantees on the original policy value-gap:

5.3 Wasserstein-critic – continuous spaces

For continuous state spaces, TV is too strong and Rmax/(1−γ)R_{\max}/(1-\gamma) is a loose, uniform

the support of the learned model 𝖯^\widehat{\mathsf{P}} and true dynamics 𝖯\mathsf{P} are disjoint
(Arjovsky et al., 2017). A geometry-aware metric like the 1-Wasserstein distance (W1W_{1}) is
preferable as it provides meaningful gradients even for disjoint distributions, ensuring the model
player receives a learning signal everywhere. By Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality, the W1W_{1}

5.6 Robustness to model misspecification

A critical advantage of the minimax formulation arises when the model class ℳ\mathscr{M} is
misspecified, meaning it does not contain the true dynamics 𝖯\mathsf{P} (i.e., inf𝖯^∈ℳ𝖣KL(𝖯∥𝖯^)>0\
inf_{\widehat{\mathsf{P}}\in\mathscr{M}}\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathsf{P}\parallel\widehat{\
mathsf{P}})>0). Standard maximum likelihood estimation seeks the projection of 𝖯\mathsf{P} onto ℳ\
mathscr{M} that minimizes the global KL divergence. However, this projection may sacrifice accuracy
in strategic regions to fit irrelevant stochasticity elsewhere, as discussed in the Minimal MDP
example.

In contrast, our framework serves as a selection rule that prioritizes value-relevant dynamics. We
can formalize this as an oracle inequality.

Proposition 8 (Strategic Oracle Inequality).

Let 𝖯^MM\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{\text{MM}} be the solution to the population minimax game (e.g.,
TV-critic) and let 𝖯^MLE\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{\text{MLE}} be the asymptotic solution to maximum
likelihood estimation. There exist environments 𝖯\mathsf{P} and model classes ℳ\mathscr{M} such
that:

 supπ∈Π|Vπ​(𝖯)−Vπ​(𝖯^MM)|≪supπ∈Π|Vπ​(𝖯)−Vπ​(𝖯^MLE)|.\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}\left\lvert V_{\pi}(\mathsf
 {P})-V_{\pi}(\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{\text{MM}})\right\rvert\ll\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}\left\lvert V_{\pi}
 (\mathsf{P})-V_{\pi}(\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{\text{MLE}})\right\rvert.

Sketch.

Consider the Minimal MDP case (Section 3). Let 𝖯\mathsf{P} be a system with high-entropy irrelevant
noise (e.g., a background TV screen) and deterministic relevant dynamics. Let ℳ\mathscr{M} be a
class of deterministic models with limited capacity.

  • •
   
    𝖯^MLE\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{\text{MLE}} attempts to match the high entropy of 𝖯\mathsf{P}. Since
    ℳ\mathscr{M} is deterministic, 𝖯^MLE\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{\text{MLE}} might oscillate or
    predict the mean of the noise, potentially distorting the relevant dynamics to minimize the
    global KL/MSE, leading to high strategic error.

  • •
   
    𝖯^MM\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{\text{MM}} minimizes the worst-case value gap. Since the noise does
    not affect VπV_{\pi}, the minimax solution will ignore the noise and perfectly fit the relevant
    deterministic dynamics, achieving zero value gap despite high KL divergence.

Thus, under misspecification, the minimax objective selects the model that is most useful rather
than most likely. ∎

6 Active Data Selection for Improved Coverage Guarantees

The coverage-based guarantees in Theorem 5 and 6 are powerful, but they depend critically on the
coverage constant κ\kappa of the data distribution ddatad_{\mathrm{data}} relative to the policies
of interest Π\Pi. This raises a chicken-and-egg problem: to get a good guarantee, one must sample
from a distribution ddatad_{\mathrm{data}} that already covers the occupancies dπd_{\pi} of the
very policies π\pi that might exploit the simulator.

This suggests an active learning approach, where the sampling distribution dtd_{t} is updated
iteratively to find these exploitative policies. We now present our most significant theoretical
insight. We show that the global problem of finding the worst-case policy π\pi is formally
equivalent to a local reinforcement learning problem: finding the optimal policy in an Error-MDP
where the reward signal is precisely the one-step modeling error.

We first define a new Error-MDP, 𝖬err\mathsf{M}_{\text{err}}, which has the same state space 𝒮\
mathscr{S}, action space 𝒜\mathscr{A}, and true transition dynamics 𝖯\mathsf{P} as the real
environment, but with a different reward function. The concept of using model error as an intrinsic
reward has been explored empirically in active exploration methods like MCX (Gehring et al., 2024);
here, we provide the formal duality proving that this strategy is not just a heuristic, but the
optimal response in a minimax robustness game.

Define the error-reward rerrr_{\text{err}} as the local, one-step modeling error, as measured by
our Wasserstein-based critic rerr​(s,a)=W1​(𝖯⁡(s,a),𝖯^​(s,a))r_{\text{err}}(s,a)=W_{1}\big(\mathsf

The theorem implies that for any policy π\pi, the performance gap between the true MDP and the

Consequently, a policy that achieves a large Error-MDP value is likely to also induce a large
discrepancy in returns. This observation motivates treating the Error-MDP as a proxy optimization
problem for identifying policies that expose weaknesses in the learned model, a standard RL problem
we can solve using familiar algorithms.

The Error-MDP provides a principled mechanism to identify where the learned model 𝖯^\widehat{\

modeling errors, thereby revealing parts of the state–action space that lack sufficient coverage.
Using this policy for active sampling directs exploration toward these high-error regions,
improving model accuracy and coverage, and thus tightening the worst-case performance bound
guaranteed by the theorem.

Note that our Error-MDP framework provides a theoretical justification for intrinsic motivation (
Chentanez et al., 2005). While methods like random network distillation (RND) (Burda et al., 2019)
use prediction error as a heuristic for novelty, our Theorem 9 proves that maximizing model error
is the optimal adversarial strategy for robust simulator learning. This also suggests that
off-the-shelf exploration algorithms (like PPO with intrinsic rewards) can be directly plugged in
as the Policy Player in our game.

7.3 Evaluation of Stand-alone Policy Training in Simulation on Control Tasks

Having demonstrated that Algorithm 3 leads to tangible improvements in simulation accuracy, we now
evaluate how such improvements translate to better policy performance in one of the hardest
applications of a simulator: training a policy purely in simulation to perform well in the real
world. This setting is a much more difficult compared to those typically tackled by model-based RL
works, as there is no access to the real world domain whatsoever during training of the final
policy for evaluation. We demonstrate this on several control tasks from the Deepmind Control
Suite (Tunyasuvunakool et al., 2020) and Gymnasium (Towers et al., 2024).

Algorithm implementation. To implement Algorithm 3 with scalable neural networks, we replace the
explicit critic guided distribution in line 6 with a policy gradient update step and use this
policy in line 3 to sample transitions for the next iteration. Our implementation builds on SAC for
this policy optimization step. Thus, the algorithm maintains 3 neural networks, the critic model
DtD_{t}, the simulator model and the data collection policy πt\pi_{t}. Each model is a fully
connected neural network with 3 hidden layers of 256 units each and linear activations in the final
layer. The critic model outputs the score, the simulator model the reward as well as the mean and
log-variance for each dimension of the next state, which is then sampled using a Gaussian
distribution. The policy also uses this Gaussian distribution as output. For all details of our
implementation, see Appendix F.

Baseline. Aside from demonstrating the practicality of Algorithm 3, our experiments seek to show
the benefits of using the iterative Wasserstein critic to guide data collection. We therefore

simulator model using maximum likelihood estimation – cross entropy loss for state transitions and
mean squared error for reward prediction. Each method uses the same architectures for policy and
simulator.

Setup and Evaluation. We run each method for 1M steps in the real environment, the Mujoco task and
afterwards evaluate the final learned simulator. For evaluation, we train a new policy using the

7.4 Convergence Stability

A common concern with minimax objectives is training instability arising from the adversarial game
between the model and the critic (Salimans et al., 2016). In standard GAN training, this
instability can manifest in three ways: (i) training divergence, (ii) mode collapse, and (iii)
 cycling or oscillation around the equilibrium. We address this concern at three levels: a formal
convergence guarantee, structural differences from standard GANs, and empirical validation.

Theoretical guarantee.

Theorem 11 proves that when both players follow no-regret algorithms, the time-averaged strategies
converge to an approximate saddle point at rate O⁡((RegretM⁡(T)+RegretD⁡(T))/T)O((\Regret_{M}(T)+\
Regret_{D}(T))/T). This directly rules out cycling (iii): the averaged iterates cannot oscillate,
since the duality gap is provably decreasing. The KL regularization term λ𝖣KL(d∥u)\lambda\mathsf{D}
_{\mathrm{KL}}(d\parallel u) in the payoff ensures the game has a unique saddle point, ruling out
the multiplicity of equilibria that causes instability in unregularized minimax problems.

Structural differences from standard GANs.

Mescheder et al. (2018) showed that Wasserstein-GANs with gradient penalty do not provably converge
locally under standard simultaneous gradient descent. However, our setting differs from standard
GANs in several important ways that avoid the specific failure modes identified in their analysis:

  • •
   
    Convex-concave structure. Our regularized payoff L(𝖯^,d)=𝔼d[W1(𝖯,𝖯^)]−λ𝖣KL(d∥u)L(\widehat{\
    mathsf{P}},d)=\E_{d}[W_{1}(\mathsf{P},\widehat{\mathsf{P}})]-\lambda\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(d\
    parallel u) is convex in 𝖯^\widehat{\mathsf{P}} (as an expectation of the convex Wasserstein
    distance) and concave in dd (linear minus convex KL). In standard GANs, both players’ losses
    are non-convex in parameter space, which is the root cause of the cycling dynamics and purely
    imaginary eigenvalues in the Dirac-GAN analysis. Our convex-concave structure guarantees that
    the minimax theorem applies and a saddle point exists.

  • •
   
    Iterative, non-simultaneous updates. We use an iterative, active-learning RL loop where the
    data distribution is dynamically updated at each round, rather than a static, simultaneous
    gradient descent loop. Each round collects new real-world data, retrains the critic, and
    updates the model sequentially. This avoids the simultaneous-update instabilities analyzed by
    Mescheder et al. (2018).

  • •
   
    Stochastic model. In our continuous control implementation, the model outputs a stochastic
    Gaussian distribution (predicting mean and log-variance) rather than a Dirac delta. This
    provides a smooth loss landscape and avoids the degenerate dynamics of the Dirac-GAN setting.

We note that the gradient penalty in our implementation serves a different purpose than in standard
WGAN-GP: it is not a training stabilizer per se, but a strict requirement of the
Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality to enforce the 1-Lipschitz constraint on the critic. This constraint
is necessary for the critic to estimate the 1-Wasserstein distance, which is the foundation of our
coverage guarantee (Lemma 4) and the Error-MDP duality (Theorem 9).

8.1 Reward learning and RLHF

Our framework directly provides the theoretical foundation for tackling ”reward hacking” in
Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) (Christiano et al., 2017). Our solution has some
relationship with that of Gupta et al. (2025), which is also based on a minimax problem. In RLHF,
there is no true reward function RR. Instead, a proxy ”reward model” R^\widehat{R} is learned from
human preference data. Next, a policy π\pi is trained to maximize this proxy: π∗=argmaxπVπ​(R^)\pi^

inevitably find and exploit inaccuracies in R^\widehat{R} to achieve a high score, leading to
behavior that is absurd or dangerous according to the true human intent RR. This mirrors recent
formalizations of reward hacking as the divergence between a proxy and the true objective (Laidlaw
et al., 2024).

This is a perfect analogy to the method described in this work with the following substitutions:

True World MM           →\to True Reward RR (the human’s intent);
Simulator M^\widehat{M} →\to Reward Model R^\widehat{R} (the learned proxy);

8.2 Offline reinforcement learning

Our framework also offers a novel perspective on pessimism and conservatism in Offline RL (Levine
et al., 2020). While often framed as a distributional shift problem, offline RL can be rigorously
viewed as a proxy-hacking game: the agent attempts to maximize a value function derived from
limited data (the proxy), and without constraints, it naturally drifts toward OOD regions where the
proxy erroneously predicts high reward.

regions with no data, where the Q-function is erroneously high.

10 Conclusion

We introduced a minimax framework for simulator learning that replaces predictive accuracy with
strategic robustness as the training objective. By formulating the sim-to-real gap as a zero-sum
game, we derived a tractable critic-based bound (Lemma 4) that transforms the intractable
policy-space maximization into a practical GAN-like training objective.

Our central theoretical contribution, the Error-MDP Duality (Theorem 9), establishes a formal
equivalence between the global problem of finding the worst-case policy and a local RL problem with
model error as reward. This duality enables a principled active data selection algorithm whose
convergence to a saddle point we proved (Theorem 11). Experiments on continuous control benchmarks
validate these theoretical predictions: the minimax approach consistently improves simulation
accuracy in strategically important regions and enables policies trained purely in simulation to
achieve near-optimal real-world performance.

Beyond simulator learning, our framework provides a general solution to proxy hacking, applicable
to reward hacking in RLHF and out-of-distribution exploitation in offline RL (Section 8). Future
work could extend the empirical evaluation to generative language models and tackle settings where
the sampling distribution cannot be chosen freely. See Appendix C.1 for further discussion.

Appendix A Extended Discussion of related work

Our work addresses the fundamental challenge of learning robust proxy models in reinforcement
learning. This intersects with several active research areas.

Uncertainty and Pessimism in MBRL. A standard approach to mitigating the reality gap is quantifying
epistemic uncertainty, often via model ensembles [Chua et al., 2018]. In Offline RL, where
exploration is impossible, this uncertainty is used to penalize the policy, ensuring it stays
within the support of the data (pessimism) [Levine et al., 2020, Yu et al., 2020, Kidambi et al.,
2020]. While effective for avoiding known bad regions, these methods are inherently passive. Our
approach is active: it uses the adversary to explicitly find high-uncertainty, exploitable regions
and then demands more data from them (via our Error-MDP formulation), rather than just avoiding
them.

Robust and Adversarial RL. Robust control formulations [Nilim and El Ghaoui, 2005, Wiesemann et
al., 2013] optimize policies against a worst-case environment chosen from a pre-defined uncertainty
set. Our work can be viewed as learning this uncertainty set simultaneously with the policy.
Adversarial Reinforcement Learning (ARL) typically introduces an adversary that perturbs dynamics
or observations during training [Pinto et al., 2017]. Our framework differs crucially in its
target: our adversary does not perturb the state to break the policy; it perturbs the policy to
break the model.

Adversarial Model Learning. Using GANs to learn environment dynamics is a growing trend. Volodin et
al. [2023] propose adversarial world models that use a discriminator to ensure generated
transitions are realistic. Our work unifies this with the control objective. A standard GAN
discriminator checks if a one-step transition (s,a,s′)(s,a,s^{\prime}) looks real (local
consistency). Our Policy Player checks if an entire sequence of transitions leads to a falsely high
value (global strategic consistency). Our theoretical results show that under certain conditions
(Wasserstein critics), these local and global objectives can be related, justifying standard
GAN-like training as a proxy for our true minimax objective.

Dynamics Adaptation and Sim-to-Real. Recent approaches have sought to bridge the gap between source
and target dynamics by learning invariant feature representations or adapting policies directly.
For instance, Guo et al. [2024] propose DARAIL, which leverages domain adaptation and
reward-augmented imitation learning to align the agent’s state distribution with that of an expert
in the target domain. However, such methods typically assume the existence of optimal expert
demonstrations in the target environment to guide the adaptation. In contrast, our work addresses
the unsupervised setting of Simultaneous Policy Learning and Sampling (SPLS), where no target
demonstrations are available. Instead of imitating an expert, our Strategic SPLS agent must
actively explore the target environment to identify and correct discrepancies in the simulator’s
physics, often targeting regions a stable expert would strictly avoid.

Value-Aware Model Learning. Prior work has recognized that not all model errors are equal.
Value-Aware Model Learning (VAML) [Farahmand et al., 2017] proposes weighting the standard
predictive loss by the gradient of the value function, focusing model capacity on states that
matter for the current policy. Our work can be viewed as the robust generalization of VAML. Instead
of weighting errors based on a single, fixed policy, our minimax framework actively finds the
worst-case policy that maximizes these errors, ensuring robustness against future policy shifts.
Recent extensions, such as λ\lambda-models [Voelcker et al., 2023], have similarly recognized the
need for iterative updates to resolve the circular dependency between the learned model and the
policy’s data distribution.

Reward Learning and Alignment. The problem of ”simulator exploitation” in MBRL is formally
isomorphic to ”reward hacking” in methods like RLHF [Christiano et al., 2017]. In both cases, an
agent optimizes a learned proxy (world model or reward model) and finds adversarial exploits.
Recent theoretical work has begun to unify these perspectives [Gupta et al., 2025]. Our Error-MDP
duality provides a concrete mechanism, active sampling based on critic disagreement, that is
applicable to both domains.

Distributionally Robust Reinforcement Learning. In short, distributionally robust RL [Shi and Chi,
2024, e.g.,] aims to find a robust policy given a worst-case simulator in a predefined uncertainty
set. Our approach solves the inverse problem: finding a robust simulator that minimizes value
prediction error against a worst-case policy. Even without active data collection, the resulting
model and policy are distinct in general.

There has also been a series of theoretical works which design model-based algorithms with strong
regret or PAC learning guarantees [Sun et al., 2019, Ayoub et al., 2020, Foster et al., 2021,
Agarwal and Zhang, 2022, e.g.,]. While our approach and analysis shares many ideas with this line
of work, our focus is slightly different. These works learn a model during policy optimization to
improve sample complexity of learning the optimal policy, e.g., in factored MDPs. Instead, our goal
is to learn a simulator of the environment, not necessarily tied to optimizing a specific policy
but allows us to accurately estimate the value of any policy in the considered set (see the main
objective in Equation (1))

B.2 Lower bounds

The rate of convergence derived in Theorem 3, which scales as O(T−1/4)O(T^{-1/4}), raises a natural
question: is this rate fundamental to the problem of learning simulators, or is it an artifact of
the analysis? We argue that this rate represents a fundamental limitation of the standard Maximum
Likelihood Estimation (MLE) paradigm, stemming from the geometric mismatch between the learning
objective and the control objective.

Standard model-based RL separates learning into two phases:

 1. 1.
   
    Estimation: The model 𝖯^\widehat{\mathsf{P}} minimizes the KL divergence (via MLE) to the true
    dynamics 𝖯\mathsf{P}:

     min𝖯^𝔼dπ[𝖣KL(𝖯∥𝖯^)].\min_{\widehat{\mathsf{P}}}\E_{d_{\pi}}[\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathsf
     {P}\parallel\widehat{\mathsf{P}})].

 2. 2.
   
    Control: The agent maximizes a value function, whose error scales with the Total Variation (TV)
    distance (Lemma 12):

     |Vπ​(𝖯)−Vπ​(𝖯^)|≤γ​Rmax1−γ​𝔼dπ[TV(𝖯,𝖯^)].|V_{\pi}(\mathsf{P})-V_{\pi}(\widehat{\mathsf{P}})|\
     leq\frac{\gamma R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\E_{d_{\pi}}[\TV(\mathsf{P},\widehat{\mathsf{P}})].

To translate the guarantee from phase 1 (KL) to phase 2 (TV), one must invoke
Schützenberger-Pinsker’s inequality [Rioul, 2023]: TV(P,Q)≤12𝖣KL(P∥Q)\TV(P,Q)\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\
mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)}. This inequality introduces a square root that degrades the
convergence rate. This square-root dependence is optimal near zero [Rioul, 2023]. If a learner
achieves a standard fast rate of ϵ≈O(T−1/2)\epsilon\approx O(T^{-1/2}) for the KL-divergence (e.g.,
using parametric MLE), the resulting guarantee for the value gap degrades to:

 Value Gap≈O(T−1/2)=O(T−1/4)\text{Value Gap}\approx\sqrt{O(T^{-1/2})}=O(T^{-1/4})

This Schützenberger-Pinsker tax implies that MLE is theoretically inefficient for control,
requiring quadratically more samples to achieve the same guarantee on policy performance compared
to a method that optimizes the relevant metric directly.

This tax is not merely hypothetical; it reflects a divergence in convergence rates for online
learning. In the general agnostic setting, standard no-regret algorithms guarantee that the average
loss decays at a rate of O(T−1/2)O(T^{-1/2}).

  • •
   
    MLE Approach: Minimizes KL. Achieving an average KL error of O(T−1/2)O(T^{-1/2}) implies, via
    Schützenberger-Pinsker’s inequality, a TV error of only O(T−1/2)=O(T−1/4)\sqrt{O(T^{-1/2})}=O(T
    ^{-1/4}).

  • •
   
    Minimax Approach: Minimizes TV directly. By using a loss function that scales linearly with the
    metric of interest (e.g., Wasserstein or dual-TV), one can theoretically recover the standard O
    (T−1/2)O(T^{-1/2}) rate for the control-relevant error.

This O(T−1/4)O(T^{-1/4}) vs. O(T−1/2)O(T^{-1/2}) gap highlights that MLE-based simulators are
inherently sample-inefficient for control tasks compared to value-aware learners. We formalize this
as a strict mathematical separation between MLE and metric-aware learners below.

Our proposed Minimax formulation (Section 5) circumvents this tax by defining the game directly
over the transport metric (TV or Wasserstein) that bounds the value difference.

 min𝖯^⁡maxD​𝔼(s,a,s′)∼d[D⁡(s,a,s′)−𝔼s^′∼𝖯^[D⁡(s,a,s^′)]]\min_{\widehat{\mathsf{P}}}\max_{D}\E_
 {(s,a,s^{\prime})\sim d}\left[D(s,a,s^{\prime})-\E_{\widehat{s}^{\prime}\sim\widehat{\mathsf{P}}}
 [D(s,a,\widehat{s}^{\prime})]\right]

Since the loss function is the metric of interest, no conversion inequality is required. Standard
results for convex-concave games suggest that such objectives can theoretically achieve the faster
O(T−1/2)O(T^{-1/2}) rates, verifying that our approach is not just strategically robust, but
potentially sample-efficient in a way that MLE cannot be.

We argued above that minimizing KL divergence (via MLE) incurs a Schützenberger-Pinsker tax that
degrades the convergence rate for control tasks from O(T−1/2)O(T^{-1/2}) to O(T−1/4)O(T^{-1/4}).
Here, we provide a rigorous justification for this claim by exhibiting a strict mathematical
separation between MLE-based and metric-aware learners.

Proposition 14 (Lower Bound for Metric-Mismatched Regret).

selected by an adversary from a convex class ℳ\mathcal{M}. There exists a randomized i.i.d.
adversarial sequence such that:

 1. 1.
   
    An MLE-based learner (minimizing KL divergence) achieves the mathematically optimal expected
    cumulative KL regret of 𝒪⁡(log⁡T)\mathcal{O}(\log T), yet inherently suffers an expected
    average Total Variation regret of Ω(T−1/4)\Omega(T^{-1/4}).

 2. 2.
   
    In contrast, an algorithm directly minimizing the Total Variation metric achieves an expected
    average Total Variation regret bounded by 𝒪(T−1/2)\mathcal{O}(T^{-1/2}).

This demonstrates a strict mathematical separation: bounding KL regret is fundamentally
insufficient for optimal control, as the geometric mismatch forces MLE to incur the
Schützenberger-Pinsker tax even against a simple i.i.d. sequence.

It is important to distinguish this result from standard batch (I.I.D.) estimation of a single
fixed distribution.

  • •
   
    I.I.D. Estimation Setting: In standard statistical estimation, where all data comes from a
    single unknown distribution PP and the goal is to estimate PP itself, MLE is asymptotically
    efficient and achieves “fast rates” of O⁡(1/T)O(1/T) for the KL divergence. In this specific
    case, the TV error decays as O⁡(1/T)=O(T−1/2)\sqrt{O(1/T)}=O(T^{-1/2}). Since direct TV
    minimization also typically achieves O(T−1/2)O(T^{-1/2}), there is no rate penalty in the batch
    estimation setting.

  • •
   
    Online Prediction Setting: Our work focuses on the online prediction setting (Section 4), where
    the distribution sequence is chosen adaptively by a Policy Player. Critically, Proposition 14
    shows that the Schützenberger-Pinsker tax arises even against a simple i.i.d. adversary: MLE
    incurs Ω(T−1/4)\Omega(T^{-1/4}) average TV regret while a direct TV minimizer achieves O(T−1/2)
    O(T^{-1/2}).

Thus, in the online setting relevant to robust control, the geometric mismatch between KL and TV
incurs a genuine rate penalty (T−1/4T^{-1/4} vs T−1/2T^{-1/2}), justifying the need for the direct
Minimax formulation proposed in this paper.

A theoretical counter-argument might suggest that because the KL divergence is strongly convex with
respect to the L1L_{1} norm (or exp-concave), one could achieve logarithmic regret O⁡(log⁡T)O(\log
T), corresponding to an average KL rate of O⁡(log⁡T/T)O(\log T/T). If one then applied the Pinsker
inequality TV≤𝖣KL/2\TV\leq\sqrt{\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}/2}, the TV error per round would scale as
log⁡T/T\sqrt{\log T/T}, apparently recovering a near-T−1/2T^{-1/2} rate. However, Proposition 14
directly refutes this argument: even though MLE achieves the optimal 𝒪⁡(log⁡T)\mathcal{O}(\log T)
KL regret in our construction, its TV regret remains Ω(T−1/4)\Omega(T^{-1/4}). The reason is that
low KL regret ensures MLE tracks the mean of the distribution sequence (analogous to L2L_{2}
optimality), but the TV-relevant quantity is the median (analogous to L1L_{1} optimality). These
two can diverge significantly, and the Pinsker inequality cannot close the gap because it bounds
absolute TV error, not TV regret.

Moreover, in the context of learning deep simulators, even the 𝒪⁡(log⁡T)\mathcal{O}(\log T) KL rate
is generally inaccessible for two practical reasons:

 1. 1.
   
    Non-Convex Parameterization: While the negative log-likelihood is convex in the space of
    probability distributions PP, it is highly non-convex with respect to the parameters θ\theta of
    a neural network. Standard gradient-based algorithms (like Adam or SGD) used in Deep RL do not
    satisfy the strong convexity assumptions required for logarithmic regret in parameter space.

 2. 2.
   
    High Dimensionality: Algorithms that theoretically achieve fast rates for exp-concave losses
    (such as the Online Newton Step) typically incur computational costs or regret bounds that
    scale poorly with the dimension dd of the parameter space (e.g., O⁡(d​log⁡T)O(d\log T) regret
    with O⁡(d2)O(d^{2}) or O⁡(d3)O(d^{3}) runtime). For modern world models where dd is in the
    millions, these methods are computationally intractable.

Consequently, practical Deep Model-Based Reinforcement Learning faces a double penalty: the
fundamental metric mismatch demonstrated in Proposition 14, compounded by the practical inability
to achieve fast KL rates. Direct Minimax optimization of the transport metric sidesteps both
issues.

Appendix C Additional Discussion and Analysis of Games using Upper-Bounds

C.1 Limitations

Our approach assumes that active sampling in the real environment is possible during simulator
learning. This is true in many real-world applications but there may be cases where the sampling
distribution is more constraint or even fixed. Our approach could still be used in such cases,
e.g., by dataset reweighting but we did not investigate this.

While our algorithmic approach is broadly applicable to various settings and tasks, our empirical
evaluation only covers control tasks as they are meaningful benchmarks with sufficient complexity
and allow assessing the methodological improvements. However, a more extensive evaluation on other
domains such as generative language models could be of interest in future work.

Appendix F Algorithm Implementation and Experimental Details

In this section, we detail the practical implementation of our minimax simulator learning
framework. We adopt the formulation from Algorithm 3 but introduce two crucial modifications for
stability and efficiency: (1) we enforce the Lipschitz constraint on the critic using a gradient
penalty as is standard in WGAN-GP (Wasserstein Generative Adversarial Network with Gradient
Penalty) [Gulrajani et al., 2017]; and (2) we constrain the adversarial exploration to
task-relevant regions.

F.1 Task-aware active sampling via constrained optimization

While our theory defines a zero-sum game over the space of all policies Π\Pi, in practice we are
primarily interested in the simulator’s accuracy on the manifold of states visited by competent
agents. A pure minimax adversary might focus on modeling complex but irrelevant dynamics (e.g., the
chaotic motion of a robot after it has already fallen/failed), which wastes sampling budget on
regions that do not contribute to task performance.

To formally address this, we reframe the sampling step. Instead of an unconstrained maximization of
model error, we formulate it as a constrained optimization problem. We seek the policy π\pi that
maximizes the estimator’s error (finding the model’s blind spots) subject to the constraint that
the policy remains competent at the task, achieving a return above a threshold VminV_{\min}.

 maxπ∈Π𝔼π[∑t=0HD⁡(st,at,st+1)]s.t.𝔼π[∑t=0Hrtask​(st,at)]≥Vmin.\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\quad\E_{\pi}\left[\
 sum_{t=0}^{H}D(s_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1})\right]\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\E_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{H}r_{\
 mathrm{task}}(s_{t},a_{t})\right]\geq V_{\min}.

We solve this problem via Lagrangian relaxation. We introduce a Lagrange multiplier λ≥0\lambda\geq
0 (represented by 1/α1/\alpha in our algorithm) to convert the hard constraint into a soft
unconstrained objective:

 maxπ𝔼π[∑t=0H(D⁡(st,at,st+1)+λ​rtask​(st,at))]−λ​Vmin.\max_{\pi}\quad\E_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{H}\
 left(D(s_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1})+\lambda r_{\mathrm{task}}(s_{t},a_{t})\right)\right]-\lambda V_{\
 min}.

Since λ​Vmin\lambda V_{\min} is a constant with respect to π\pi, maximizing this Lagrangian is
equivalent to maximizing the cumulative sum of a hybrid reward function rsampler_{\mathrm{sample}}:

 rsample​(s,a)=α​rtask​(s,a)+[D⁡(s,a,s′)−𝔼s′′∼𝖯^[D⁡(s,a,s′′)]].r_{\mathrm{sample}}(s,a)=\alpha r_
 {\mathrm{task}}(s,a)+\left[D(s,a,s^{\prime})-\E_{s^{\prime\prime}\sim\widehat{\mathsf{P}}}[D
 (s,a,s^{\prime\prime})]\right].

Here, the hyperparameter α\alpha controls the tightness of the constraint. When α→0\alpha\to 0, we
recover the pure Minimax formulation (Global Robustness). When α\alpha is large, the agent
prioritizes task performance, using the model error term purely as an intrinsic exploration bonus
to explore the boundaries of the relevant state space.

F.2 Algorithm details and representation

The full procedure is summarized in Algorithm 4. It proceeds in three key iterative steps:

 1. 1.
   
    Metric Learning (Critic Step). We update the critic DD to maximize the Wasserstein distance
    between real transitions (s,a,s′)(s,a,s^{\prime}) and dreamed transitions (s,a,s′′)(s,a,s^{\
    prime\prime}).

      □ •
       
        Representation: The critic Dϕ​(s,a,s′)D_{\phi}(s,a,s^{\prime}) is a standard multi-layer
        perceptron neural network (MLP). Crucially, the final layer is a linear projection without
        activation (that is, no Sigmoid or Tanh). This is required because the
        Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality defines the distance as a supremum over 1-Lipschitz
        functions f:𝒮→ℝf\colon\mathscr{S}\to\mathbb{R}, which must be unbounded. Sigmoid
        activations would restrict the range to [0,1][0,1], collapsing the metric to the
        Jensen-Shannon divergence.

      □ •
       
        Gradient penalty: To enforce the Lipschitz constraint required for the linear objective to
        be valid, we apply a gradient penalty λGP\lambda_{\mathrm{GP}} to the norm of the critic’s
        gradients.

 2. 2.
   
    Simulator Learning (Model Step). The model 𝖯^θ\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{\theta} is updated to fool
    the critic.

      □ •
       
        Representation: The model can be represented as a probabilistic neural network (e.g., an
        ensemble of Gaussian MLPs) that outputs the parameters of a distribution s′′∼𝒩⁡(μθ​
        (s,a),Σθ​(s,a))s^{\prime\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\theta}(s,a),\Sigma_{\theta}(s,a)).
        Gradients are propagated through the sampling step using the reparameterization trick.

      □ •
       
        Linear Objective: We update the model via gradient ascent on 𝔼[D⁡(s,a,s′′)]\E[D(s,a,s^{\
        prime\prime})]. Unlike standard GANs which often require a log-loss surrogate (e.g., log⁡D\
        log D) to prevent vanishing gradients when the discriminator saturates, our use of the
        Wasserstein metric with WGAN-GP ensures that the critic provides useful linear gradients
        even when the model is far from the target. Thus, we optimize the linear objective
        directly, preserving the theoretical connection to the W1W_{1} distance.

      □ •
       
        While we use the linear objective to guide the data collection, we also train a second
        model using log-loss on the same data and use this as the final simulator. Depending on the
        model architecture, this can help improve generalization. Importantly, unlike in GANs, the
        log-loss surrogate trained model is not affecting the metric learning and data collection.

 3. 3.
   
    Active Sampling (Policy Step). The policy πψ\pi_{\psi} is updated to maximize the hybrid reward
    rsampler_{\mathrm{sample}} derived above. This guides data collection toward regions where the
    current model 𝖯^t\widehat{\mathsf{P}}_{t} has high discrepancy from the real dynamics, while
    staying on the manifold of relevant tasks.

Sample vs. Computational Efficiency. We acknowledge that Algorithm 3 involves solving an inner RL
problem (optimizing π\pi in the Error-MDP) at each iteration. While computationally more intensive
than standard MLE or heuristic uncertainty sampling (e.g., ensembles), this cost is justified in
settings where real-world samples are the scarce resource (e.g., robotics, medical AI). Our
framework effectively trades increased offline computation for minimized real-world interaction,
ensuring that every sample collected targets a strategically relevant deficiency in the model.
Furthermore, we note that finding the exact optimal policy π∗\pi^{*} for the Error-MDP at every
iteration is not strictly necessary. In practice, taking a few gradient steps on the policy (e.g.,
via PPO) to improve its error-seeking capability is sufficient to discover new, high-error regions,
making the inner loop computationally tractable.

The direct implementation of Algorithm 3 samples from the real environment iteratively, similar to
standard MBRL methods. However, in computationally constrained settings, one can decouple this by
maintaining a replay buffer and using importance sampling to sample heavily from the buffer’s
historical data, falling back on the real environment only when the buffer lacks coverage in the
high-error regions identified by the critic.

Inaccurate critic predictions harming training? Please note that initial inaccuracies in the critic
do not derail training because the algorithm is inherently self-correcting. If the critic falsely
assigns a high error score to a specific state-action pair, the sampling distribution will place
high probability mass there. In the next iteration, the algorithm will collect ground-truth samples
from exactly this pair, automatically correcting both the critic’s estimate and the simulator’s
accuracy in that region.

F.3 Additional Details on Experimental Setup

We build our implementation for the experiments on policy training in control tasks on top of the
Acme framework [Hoffman et al., 2020]. The implementation of domains are from Gymnasium [Towers et
al., 2024] and the DM-Control Suite [Tunyasuvunakool et al., 2020]. For ease of computation, we
limit the horizon for each task to 200 steps.

For policy, critic and simulator model, we use a feed forward neural network with two hidden layers
of 256 nodes each and ReLU activations. The final layers are all linear with the following output

  • •
   
    Policy: Mean and log standard deviation of the Gaussian from which the action is drawn.

  • •
   
    Critic: Single output as the critic score.

  • •
   
    Simulator model: Number of state dimensions outputs predicting the next state and an additional
    output predicting reward.

We use an Adam optimizer with learning rate as 3​e−43e-4 for each model. The baseline only uses the
task reward when optimizing the data collection policy while Algorithm 3 uses a uniform mixture of
task reward and critic difference score, strategically seeking high error regions that are relevant
for high reward policies.

All experiments were performed on an internal cluster with 6464 CPUs per worker and a H100 GPU.
However, none of the neural networks and domains in our empirical evaluation require extensive
compute and we believe that the results can be reproduced on a single consumer hardware machine
with any modern GPU within as most 1 week of compute time.

Details on Narrow Passage Domain

We define a continuous 2D domain where relevant model errors are concentrated in a small region
that is rarely visited by uniform exploration, but strictly necessary for optimal policy
performance:

  • •
   
    State Space: 𝒮=[0,1]2\mathscr{S}=[0,1]^{2}. Agent starts at (0.1,0.5)(0.1,0.5). Goal region is
    x>0.9x>0.9.

  • •
   
    Action Space: 𝒜=[−0.1,0.1]2\mathscr{A}=[-0.1,0.1]^{2} (velocity vector (x˙,y˙)(\dot{x},\dot
    {y})).

  • •
   
    True Dynamics (𝖬\mathsf{M}):

      □ –
       
        Normal Region: st+1=st+at+𝒩⁡(0,0.01​I)s_{t+1}=s_{t}+a_{t}+\mathcal{N}(0,0.01I).

      □ –
       
        The Wall: A barrier at x=0.5x=0.5 blocks all movement, except for a narrow passage at y∈
        [0.45,0.55]y\in[0.45,0.55].

      □ –
       
        The Trap: Inside the narrow passage, there is a strong ”wind” pushing the agent down: y˙eff
        =y˙−0.05\dot{y}_{\text{eff}}=\dot{y}-0.05.

  • •
   
    Initial Simulator (𝖬^0\widehat{\mathsf{M}}_{0}): A naive linear model, st+1=st+ats_{t+1}=s_{t}
    +a_{t}, trained on initial uniform random data. It likely learns free movement everywhere,
    missing the wall and the trap.

While policies and models are stochastic (Gaussian) for experiments in control tasks, we here use a
deterministic transition model. Then the 1-Wasserstein distance cleanly reduces to the expected
distance between predicted and observed state. This simplification was an intended benefit of the
experimental design. It allowed us to clearly visualize and validate that the critic was accurately
learning the Error-MDP reward function without the added confounding variance of sampling from a
stochastic learned model.
